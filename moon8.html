Moon 8: The Python King: Eke

1. The Python That Was Our Father

A young man named Obiora was walking through the forest when he saw a great python coiled upon a rock. Its skin shimmered in the dappled light, and its eyes were calm, ancient, and knowing.

Obiora raised his machete. He had been taught to kill snakes, for snakes were dangerous. But something stayed his hand. The python did not move. It did not hiss. It simply watched him.

He returned to the village and told his father what he had seen. His father's face grew grave.

"My son," the old man said, "do you not know what you nearly did? That python is not a snake. It is an ancestor. It is your grandfather, returned from the spirit world to watch over you."

Obiora was confused. "How can a snake be my grandfather?"

His father sighed. "The elders say that when we die, our spirits do not simply vanish. They remain with us. Some live in the spirit world. Some are reborn as children. And some return as the Eke, the sacred python. The Eke is the reincarnation of our ancestors. It is addressed as 'our father.' To kill one is to kill your own blood."

Obiora never again raised his hand against a python. He learned that the Eke was not to be feared, but honored. It was a messenger from the world beyond.

The Lesson: The Eke is the reincarnation of ancestors. It is not an animal to be feared, but a kin to be honored.

2. The Messenger of Ala

A woman named Nneka was troubled. Her crops were failing. Her children were sick. Her husband had grown distant. She went to the dibia and asked what she had done wrong.

The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He was silent for a long time.

"You have offended Ala," he said.

Nneka was shocked. "How? I have done nothing wrong."

"Think back," the dibia said. "Have you harmed any of Ala's messengers?"

Nneka thought. And then she remembered. Two moons ago, she had found a small python in her barn. She had been frightened. She had called her husband, and he had killed it.

The dibia nodded. "The Eke is the messenger of Ala, the earth goddess. It is the guardian of the land and the underworld. When you kill the Eke, you kill the messenger. You sever the connection between yourself and the earth. You invite Ala's anger."

Nneka wept. "What can I do?"

The dibia told her to make sacrifices. She was to offer a white cock and a white cloth at the shrine of Ala. She was to beg for forgiveness. She was to bury the python with the honors due to a kinsman.

Nneka did as she was told. In time, her crops recovered. Her children grew well. Her husband returned to her. And she never again harmed a python.

The Lesson: The Eke is the messenger of Ala, the earth goddess. To harm it is to offend the very ground beneath your feet.

3. The Founding Father of Njaba

In the community of Njaba, in Orlu, the people tell a story that has been passed down for generations. Long ago, before the villages were built, before the farms were cleared, a great python led their ancestors to the land they now call home.

The python was not an ordinary serpent. It was a spirit, a guide sent by Chukwu himself. It slithered through the forest, and the people followed. It crossed rivers, and the people crossed with it. It stopped at a clearing, and the people knew that this was where they were meant to settle.

The python coiled itself around a great tree and watched as the people built their homes. And when the first child was born in the new village, the python was there, blessing the child with its presence.

From that day forward, the people of Njaba revered the python as their founding father. They called it Eke, and they did not harm it. When a python appeared in the village, it was considered a sign of blessing. When it entered a home, it was welcomed. It was the living link between the people and the land, the spirit of their ancestors made manifest.

The elders say that if you kill the Eke in Njaba, you are not just killing a snake. You are killing the founder of the community. You are killing your own heritage.

The Lesson: In some communities, the Eke is the founding father. It is the spirit of the land, and it must be protected.

4. The Eke of Ogbunike Cave

In the land of Ogbunike, in what is now Anambra State, there is a cave that the elders say was carved by the hands of the ancestors. It is called the Ogbunike Cave, and it is a sacred place. Its tunnels stretch deep into the earth. Its chambers echo with the sound of an underground river. And in its darkest corners live the sacred pythons.

The elders say that long ago, when the people of Ogbunike were first settling the land, a great python emerged from the cave. It was not aggressive. It did not attack. It simply coiled itself at the entrance to the cave and watched the people as they built their homes.

The people were afraid at first. They did not know what the python wanted. They sent a young man into the cave to learn its purpose. The young man walked through the tunnels for three days. He saw the underground river. He saw the chambers of stone. And then he saw a vision.

The vision showed him that the cave was the dwelling place of the ancestors. The python was their messenger. The python guarded the cave because the cave was the gateway between the human world and the spirit world. It was the place where the living could speak with the dead.

The young man returned to his people and told them what he had seen. And from that day forward, the people of Ogbunike honored the python. They did not hunt it. They did not harm it. They brought offerings to the cave. They treated the python as a priest—the priest of the ancestors—and the cave as a shrine.

Even today, the Ogbunike Cave is a sacred place. The pythons still live within it. And the people of Ogbunike still honor them, for they remember the young man who walked through the tunnels and saw the truth.

The Lesson: The Eke guards the places where the ancestors dwell. The cave is a shrine, and the python is its priest. To harm the python is to desecrate the shrine.

5. The Python Lord of the Forest

The elders say that the python is the king of the forest. It is called Eke nwe ọhịa—the python, lord of the forest. It is not the largest animal. It is not the fastest. It is not the strongest. But it is the wisest. It is the most patient. It is the most powerful in ways that cannot be seen.

The python does not roar like the lion. It does not charge like the elephant. It waits. It watches. It moves in silence. And when it strikes, it strikes with precision.

This is why the Igbo admire the python. They see themselves in it. The Igbo are not a people of loud boasts. They are a people of quiet strength. They are patient. They are calculating. They are relentless.

The python is the lord of the forest because it knows the secrets of the forest. It knows where the prey hides. It knows where the water flows. It knows the paths that others have forgotten. It is the keeper of the forest's wisdom.

And so, when the Igbo call the python Eke nwe ọhịa, they are not just giving it a title. They are acknowledging its power. They are honoring its wisdom. They are reminding themselves that true strength is not loud. It is patient. It is knowing.

The Lesson: The python is the lord of the forest, not because of its size or speed, but because of its wisdom and patience.

6. The Python That Came to the Wedding

A young couple in Idemili was getting married. The whole village was gathered. The drums were beating. The palm wine was flowing. Everyone was happy.

And then, in the middle of the ceremony, a great python appeared. It slithered into the compound and coiled itself beside the bride and groom.

Some of the guests were afraid. They began to move away. But the elders smiled. They raised their hands for silence.

"This is a blessing," the eldest man said. "The Eke has come to witness the marriage. The ancestors are here. They are happy."

The bride and groom were not afraid. They bowed their heads. The python remained for a moment, its eyes fixed on the couple. And then it slithered away, disappearing into the forest.

The marriage was a happy one. The couple prospered. And the elders said it was because the Eke had blessed them.

In Idemili and many other Igbo communities, the python is an expected guest at all significant celebrations—weddings, naming ceremonies, chieftaincy installations, and burials. When it appears, it is considered god-sent. It is a sign that the spirits are present, that the ancestors are watching, that the occasion is sacred.

The Lesson: The Eke appears at important ceremonies as a blessing. It is a sign that the ancestors are present.

7. The Python That Retrieves Its Venom

A man named Okeke was walking through the forest when he accidentally stepped on a python. The python, startled, bit him. Okeke cried out in pain. He looked at his leg. There were two small puncture wounds. Blood was trickling out.

But then something strange happened. The python did not flee. It did not strike again. It looked at Okeke with what seemed like sorrow. And then it sighed. A long, deep, mournful sigh.

Okeke felt a strange sensation. The pain in his leg began to fade. The wound closed. The venom was gone.

The elders later explained what had happened. The python in Idemili and other communities is not like other snakes. It is sacred. It does not want to harm anyone. If it bites, it is because it was startled. And if it bites, it will sigh, drawing the venom back out of the victim.

The python is not aggressive. It is not dangerous. It is a gentle creature, a guardian, a mother. It does not want to hurt its children.

The Lesson: The Eke is not a creature to be feared. Even when it bites, it withdraws its venom. It is a protector, not a predator.

8. The Killing of the Eke Nworie

In Owerri, there is a river called Nworie. And in that river, there lived a great python called Eke Nworie. It was an ancestral python, a deity, a guardian of the community. It had lived in the river for more than a hundred years. It had never harmed anyone.

But then, some students came to the area. They did not know the customs of the land. They did not understand the sacredness of the Eke. And they killed the python.

The community was outraged. The chief priest stormed the campus. He issued a seven-day ultimatum to the authorities. He warned that mysterious deaths might befall the community. He demanded that the students undergo a compulsory cleansing ceremony.

The killing of the Eke Nworie was not just a crime against an animal. It was a sacrilege. It was an offense against the ancestors. It was a desecration of the land.

The elders say that when you kill the Eke, you are not just killing a snake. You are killing your own father. You are severing the connection between the living and the dead. You are inviting the anger of the gods.

The Lesson: Killing the Eke is a sacrilege. It is like killing one's own father. It invites the wrath of the ancestors and the gods.

9. The Python of Eke-Nnechukwu

The elders say that the python is not just an ancestor. It is not just a messenger. It is the feminine double of Chukwu himself. She is called Eke-Nnechukwu, the python that is the mother of the creator.

In the ancient mysteries, the python is the symbol of the earth goddess, Ala. But it is also the symbol of Chukwu's feminine aspect. It is the divine mother, the source of all life, the womb of creation.

The python's sinuous pose, its slow and graceful movements, its ability to shed its skin and be reborn—all of these are symbols of the cycle of life, death, and rebirth. The python is the ultimate symbol of transformation. It is the spirit that never dies, that simply changes form and continues.

When the Igbo look at the python, they do not see a snake. They see the mother of all things. They see the spirit of the earth. They see the source of life itself.

The Lesson: The Eke is the feminine double of Chukwu, the mother of creation. It is the symbol of transformation and eternal life.

10. The Python That Guards the Sacred Grove

In every village, there are sacred groves. These are the places where the spirits dwell. These are the places where the ancestors are buried. These are the places where no human foot should tread without permission.

And in these sacred groves, the python is the guardian. It lives among the trees. It watches over the spirits. It ensures that no one disturbs the sacred ground.

The elders say that if you enter a sacred grove and see a python, you should not be afraid. You should bow your head and retreat. The python is not there to harm you. It is there to protect the grove. It is there to remind you that this is not your place. This is the place of the spirits.

If you harm the python, you harm the grove. If you harm the grove, you harm the ancestors. And if you harm the ancestors, you invite the wrath of the gods.

The Lesson: The Eke guards the sacred groves. It is the protector of the places where the spirits dwell.

11. The Python That Was Buried Like a Man

In some Igbo communities, when a python is accidentally killed, it is not thrown away. It is not eaten. It is not treated like a dead animal. It is buried with the honors due to a kinsman.

The culprit must go to the dibia. He must make sacrifices. He must undergo a ritual of cleansing called ikpu ani. He must appease the goddess of the land. And then he must bury the python in an elaborate ceremony, similar to the burial rites accorded to a human being.

The elders say that this is because the python is not an animal. It is a person. It is an ancestor. It is a member of the family. To kill it is to kill a kinsman. And a kinsman must be buried with honor.

When the python is buried, the people mourn. They do not shave their heads for a year, as a sign of mourning. They abstain from certain foods. They observe the rites of mourning as if a human being had died.

The Lesson: The Eke is not an animal. It is a kinsman. When it dies, it must be buried with the honor due to a human being.

12. The Python That Brought Peace

A village was divided. Two families had been fighting for generations. The land was cursed. The crops failed. The children died. The people were suffering.

An old woman came to the village. She was a dibia, a woman of great wisdom. She looked at the two families and said: "You are fighting over land. But the land does not belong to you. It belongs to the ancestors. And the ancestors are angry."

She told them to go to the sacred grove. She told them to find the python that lived there. She told them to ask the python for forgiveness.

The two families went to the grove. They found the python coiled around a tree. They bowed their heads. They confessed their sins. They asked for peace.

The python did not move. It did not hiss. It simply looked at them with its ancient eyes. And in that look, the two families saw their ancestors. They saw the futility of their quarrel. They saw the pain they had caused.

They embraced. They made peace. And from that day forward, the village prospered.

The Lesson: The Eke is a peacemaker. It can heal divisions and restore harmony between families and communities.

13. The Python That Never Dies

The elders say that the python is the only creature that never truly dies. When it grows old, it sheds its skin. It emerges new, young, and strong. It is reborn.

This is why the python is the symbol of reincarnation. This is why the Igbo believe that the ancestors return as pythons. The python is the proof that death is not the end. It is the proof that the spirit continues.

When an Igbo person dies, their spirit goes to the spirit world. But it does not stay there forever. In time, it returns. It may return as a child. It may return as a python. It may return in a form that we cannot see, but that we can feel.

The python is the reminder that life is a cycle. We are born. We live. We die. And we are born again. The python sheds its skin, and it is renewed. We shed our bodies, and we are renewed.

And so, when the Igbo see a python, they do not see death. They see life. They see the promise of rebirth. They see the endless cycle of the spirit.

The Lesson: The Eke is the symbol of reincarnation and eternal life. It reminds us that death is not the end, but a transformation.

14. The Python That Testified Before the Elders

In a certain village, a man was found dead in his barn. His brother was accused of the murder. The brother denied it. He swore on the ofo. He called upon the ancestors. But no one believed him.

The elders were divided. There was no evidence. There were no witnesses. The case could not be settled.

Then an old woman spoke. She said: "Take the accused to the sacred grove. Let the Eke be the judge."

The man was taken to the grove. A great python was coiled beneath the sacred tree. The man was told to stand before it and speak the truth. He stood before the python. He looked into its ancient eyes. And then he broke down and confessed.

He had killed his brother over a piece of land. He had hidden the body. He had thought no one would ever know. But the Eke had seen. The Eke knew. And the Eke had judged.

The elders say that the python is the witness who never lies. It sees everything. It remembers everything. When no human can judge, the Eke can. When no human can testify, the Eke will speak.

The Lesson: The Eke is the silent witness. It sees all and remembers all. No crime is hidden from the python.

15. The Python That Blessed the Barren Woman

A woman named Nneoma had been married for many years, but she had no child. Her husband's family mocked her. Her co-wives laughed at her. She was alone in her sorrow.

She went to the dibia and asked: "Why am I barren? What have I done to offend the gods?"

The dibia cast the stones. He read the patterns. He said: "You have not offended the gods. But you have not asked the right spirit. Go to the sacred grove. Seek the Eke. Ask for its blessing."

Nneoma went to the grove. She found a python coiled beneath the sacred tree. She bowed before it. She poured palm wine on the ground. She said: "Eke, mother of my people, I have no child. Please bless me."

The python did not move. It did not hiss. It simply looked at her with its ancient eyes. And in that look, Nneoma felt a warmth, a peace, a promise.

Nine moons later, she gave birth to a son. She named him Ekechukwu—"the python of Chukwu." And she taught him to honor the Eke, for the python had blessed his mother.

The Lesson: The Eke blesses those who seek it with humility. Its power is gentle, but it is real. Ask, and you shall receive.

16. The Python of Nnewi

In Nnewi, in Anambra State, the python is sacred. It is not hunted. It is not eaten. It is not harmed. The people of Nnewi have kept this law for generations, and they do not break it.

There was once a young man named Okonkwo who had returned from the city. He had learned new ways. He laughed at the old superstitions. One day, he saw a python in his compound. He picked up a stick and killed it.

That night, Okonkwo fell ill. He burned with fever. He raved in his sleep. He saw visions of the python, growing larger and larger, wrapping itself around him, squeezing the life from his body.

His family took him to the dibia. The dibia said: "You have killed the Eke. You have killed an ancestor. You must make a sacrifice. You must be cleansed."

Okonkwo made the sacrifice. He offered a white ram. He poured palm wine on the earth. He begged the ancestors for forgiveness. And slowly, he recovered. But he was never the same. He walked with a limp for the rest of his life. And he never again harmed a python.

The Lesson: The laws of the ancestors are not superstitions. They are covenants. Those who break them will feel the consequences.

17. The Python That Led the Hunters Home

A group of hunters went deep into the forest. They hunted for many days. They killed many animals. But when it was time to return home, they were lost. The trees all looked the same. The paths all led in circles. They wandered for days, growing weak, growing afraid.

And then, they saw a python. It was a great python, golden-brown, its eyes shining like dark stones. It did not attack. It did not flee. It simply began to move, slowly, deliberately, through the forest.

The hunters followed. They did not know why. They simply trusted. The python led them through the thickest part of the forest. It led them across streams. It led them around swamps. And finally, it led them to the edge of their village.

When the hunters turned to thank the python, it was gone. It had disappeared into the forest without a sound.

The elders said: "The Eke led you home. It is the guide of the lost. It is the spirit of the forest, and it will always lead its children home."

The Lesson: The Eke is the guide. When you are lost, it will show you the way. Trust in the spirit of the ancestors.

18. The Python That Cursed the Wicked Chief

There was once a chief who was cruel. He took the best yams from his people. He demanded the finest animals from their hunts. He beat those who disobeyed him. He was feared by all.

One day, the chief saw a python in his compound. He was angry. He ordered his guards to kill it. The guards hesitated. They knew the python was sacred. But the chief threatened them. They killed the python.

That night, the chief had a dream. In the dream, a great python came to him. It wrapped itself around his body. It looked into his eyes. And it said: "You have killed my messenger. You have broken the covenant. Your rule will end. Your lineage will end. Your name will be forgotten."

The chief woke in terror. He went to the dibia. He begged for forgiveness. But the dibia said: "It is too late. You have offended the ancestors. The curse has been spoken."

Within a year, the chief lost his title. His sons died. His wives left him. He died alone, a broken man. And his name was struck from the memory of the village.

The Lesson: The Eke protects the innocent and punishes the wicked. Those who harm it will lose everything.

19. The Python That Protected the Market

In a certain town, the market was the heart of the community. It was where the people gathered. It was where they traded. It was where they celebrated. And it was protected by the python.

There was once a group of thieves who came to the market at night. They planned to steal the goods. They planned to rob the traders. They thought no one would see them.

But as they entered the market, they saw a great python coiled in the center of the square. Its eyes gleamed in the moonlight. It did not move. It did not hiss. It simply watched.

The thieves froze. They could not move. They could not breathe. The python's gaze held them as if in a trance. They stood there until morning, until the traders arrived, until they were caught.

The elders said: "The Eke guards the market. It watches over the trade. It protects the honest. It punishes the wicked."

The Lesson: The Eke is the guardian of the community. It watches over the market, the shrine, and all the sacred places of the people.

20. The Python and the Christian Convert

In the early days of the missionaries, a young man named Nnamdi converted to Christianity. He learned the white man's religion. He learned to laugh at the old ways. He learned to say that the python was just a snake.

One day, he saw a python in the church compound. He picked up a stone and killed it. The people of the village were horrified. They said: "You have killed an ancestor. You will be punished."

Nnamdi laughed. "The python is just a snake," he said. "Your gods are nothing. Your ancestors are nothing."

That night, Nnamdi fell ill. The fever came and would not leave. His body swelled. His skin turned yellow. The missionaries prayed over him. The doctors gave him medicine. But nothing worked.

Finally, in desperation, Nnamdi's mother called for the dibia. The dibia said: "He must confess. He must make the sacrifice. He must honor the Eke he killed."

Nnamdi refused at first. But as the days passed and his life slipped away, he finally agreed. He confessed. He made the sacrifice. He begged for forgiveness. And slowly, he recovered. But from that day forward, he respected the old ways. He did not mock the python. He did not mock the ancestors.

The Lesson: The old ways are not superstitions. They are the wisdom of the ancestors. Do not mock what you do not understand.

21. The Python That Returned After Death

There was once a great man named Ogbuefi. He was a chief. He was a warrior. He was a titled man. He was respected throughout the land. And when he died, the whole village mourned.

But a strange thing happened. On the day of his burial, a great python appeared at the compound. It slithered into the house. It coiled itself beside the body of Ogbuefi. It did not move. It did not hiss. It simply stayed there, as if in mourning.

The elders said: "The python is Ogbuefi. He has returned. He has come to say goodbye to his people."

The python remained for three days. It did not eat. It did not drink. It simply lay beside the body of the man it had been. And then, on the fourth day, it slithered away, disappearing into the forest.

The people of the village knew then that Ogbuefi had joined the ancestors. They knew that he would return. They knew that the python would come again.

The Lesson: The Eke is the reincarnation of the ancestors. When a great man dies, his spirit may return as a python. Watch for it. Honor it.

22. The Eke That Taught the People to Farm

Long ago, in the days after the sky moved away, the people did not know how to farm. They did not know how to plant. They did not know how to harvest. They wandered the land, hungry and confused, eating whatever they could find in the forest.

One day, a great python appeared at the edge of the village. It was the Eke, the messenger of Ala. It did not attack. It did not hiss. It simply began to move, slowly, deliberately, through the bush.

The people followed. They did not know why. They simply trusted. The python led them to a clearing. It coiled itself in the center of the clearing. It looked at them with its ancient eyes. And then, it began to dig with its body.

The python dug a mound of earth. It dug another. It dug a third. And then it stopped. It looked at the people. And in its gaze, they understood.

The elders said: "The Eke has shown us the way of the yam. We must dig the mound. We must plant the yam. We must tend the field. We must harvest the crop. This is the covenant between the people and the earth."

From that day forward, the people farmed. They dug the yam mounds as the python had shown them. They planted the yams. They harvested the crops. And every year, they brought the first yam to the shrine of Ala, and they honored the Eke that had taught them to farm.

The elders say that the python is the first farmer. It was the python that taught the people to work the earth. It was the python that showed them that work is sacred, and that food is not a gift—it is a covenant.

The Lesson: The Eke taught the people to farm. It is the guardian of the covenant between humans and the earth. Honor the python, and the harvest will be plentiful.

23. The Python That Helped the Woman Find Her Child

A woman named Ada lost her child. The little boy had wandered into the forest while she was working. She searched for him. She called his name. She wept. But she could not find him.

She went to the dibia. The dibia cast the stones. He said: "The child is in the forest. He is lost. But he is not alone. The Eke is with him. Go to the forest. Call for the Eke. It will lead you to your son."

Ada went to the forest. She called out: "Eke! Eke! I am looking for my son. Please help me." And then, she saw a great python. It was coiled beneath a tree. It looked at her with its ancient eyes. And then, it began to move.

Ada followed the python. It led her through the forest. It led her across streams. It led her to a clearing. And there, curled up and asleep, was her son. He was safe. He was well. The python had watched over him.

Ada wept with joy. She thanked the python. She thanked the ancestors. And she taught her son to honor the Eke, for the python had saved his life.

The Lesson: The Eke watches over the children of the village. It protects the innocent. It guides the lost.

24. The Python That Killed a Thief

In a certain village, there was a thief who terrorized the people. He stole yams from the barns. He stole goats from the pens. He stole cloth from the market. No one could catch him. He was as clever as he was wicked.

One night, the thief decided to rob the shrine. He climbed the wall. He crept through the darkness. He reached for the offerings. And then, he felt something move beneath him.

He looked down. A great python was coiled around the altar. Its eyes gleamed in the darkness. It did not hiss. It did not strike. It simply wrapped itself around the thief. It squeezed. And squeezed. And squeezed.

In the morning, the people found the thief dead, coiled in the embrace of the python. The python was gone. The shrine was untouched.

The elders said: "The Eke does not kill the innocent. It only punishes the guilty. The thief came to rob the shrine. The python protected it. The punishment was death."

The Lesson: The Eke protects the sacred. Those who come to steal from the gods will be struck down by the guardians of the shrine.

25. The Python That Ate the Poison

There was once a woman whose husband was trying to kill her. He wanted to marry another woman. He wanted his wife gone. So he decided to poison her.

He made a soup. He put poison in it. He gave it to his wife. She was about to eat it when a python appeared. It slithered into the house. It coiled itself beside her bowl. It dipped its head into the soup. It drank the poison. And then it slithered away.

The woman was astonished. She looked at her husband. She saw the fear in his eyes. She knew what he had tried to do.

She went to the elders. She told them what had happened. The husband was tried. He was punished. And the woman was set free.

The elders said: "The Eke saved the woman. It drank the poison meant for her. It is the protector of the innocent."

The Lesson: The Eke protects the innocent. It will even take the poison meant for you. Honor it, and it will honor you.

26. The Python That Was the King's Advisor

There was once a king who was wise and just. He ruled his people well. But he did not rule alone. He had an advisor. Not a human advisor. A python.

The python lived in the king's palace. It coiled itself beside the throne. It watched the proceedings. And when the king was uncertain, he would go to the python and sit in silence. The python would look at him with its ancient eyes. And in that look, the king would find his answer.

The people of the village knew about the python. They respected it. They brought offerings to it. They called it the king's spirit. The python was the link between the king and the ancestors. It was the link between the king and the gods.

And so the king ruled wisely, because he did not rule alone. He ruled with the python. He ruled with the ancestors. He ruled with the gods.

The Lesson: The Eke is a wise advisor. It is the voice of the ancestors. Those who listen to it will rule wisely.

27. The Python That Welcomed the Ozo

There was once a man named Nwosu who was about to take the Ozo title. He was a man of integrity. He was a man of honor. He was a man who had lived a life of truth and justice.

On the day of his installation, he went to the shrine. He was to be presented to the ancestors. He was to be blessed by the elders. He was to be given the ofo. And as he entered the shrine, a great python appeared.

It slithered out from behind the altar. It coiled itself before him. It raised its head. It looked at him with its ancient eyes.

The elders said: "The Eke welcomes you. The ancestors accept you. You are now one of us."

Nwosu took the ofo. He spoke the words of the oath. The python watched. And when he was finished, the python lowered its head. It slithered back to the altar. The ceremony was complete.

And from that day forward, Nwosu was an Ozo. He was a keeper of the truth. He was a guardian of the laws. He was a man who had been blessed by the Eke.

The Lesson: The Eke blesses those who take the Ozo title. It is the witness of the covenant. It is the guardian of the truth.

28. The Python That Will Always Return

In every generation, the Eke returns. In every village, the python appears. It is the spirit of the ancestors. It is the messenger of the gods. It is the guardian of the land.

The elders say that the python is not just an animal. It is a symbol. It is a promise. It is a covenant. The promise that the ancestors are always with us. The promise that the spirit world is not far away. The promise that death is not the end.

When you see a python in your compound, do not fear. It is a blessing. It is an ancestor, returned to watch over you. Bow your head. Offer it respect. And it will protect you.

When you see a python in the forest, do not harm it. It is the lord of the forest. It is the keeper of the secrets. It is the guide of the lost. Honor it. Follow it. And it will lead you home.

When you see a python at a wedding, at a naming ceremony, at a burial, do not turn away. It is a sign. It is a message. It is a blessing. The ancestors have come. The spirits are present. The occasion is sacred.

The Eke will always return. As long as the Igbo people remember their ancestors, the python will appear. As long as the traditions are kept, the spirit will live. As long as the covenant is honored, the Eke will protect.

And so, the Igbo people continue. They remember the Eke. They honor the Eke. And the Eke honors them in return.

The Lesson: The Eke is eternal. It is the symbol of the ancestors, the guardian of the land, and the promise of eternal life. As long as the Igbo people remember, the Eke will always return.
