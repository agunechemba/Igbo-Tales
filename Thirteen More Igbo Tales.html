📖 Thirteen More Igbo Tales

1. The Tortoise and the Boar's Debt

The Tortoise, ever the schemer, borrowed a substantial sum of money from the Boar. He promised to repay the debt on an agreed date, but as the day approached, the Tortoise conveniently "forgot." The Boar, known for his impatience and quick temper, grew tired of waiting. One day, he confronted the Tortoise and, in his anger, seized the Tortoise's wife's precious grinding stone and threw it into the river.

The Tortoise, seeing an opportunity, went to the King. He wept and claimed that the stone the Boar had thrown away was not just any stone, but contained the very money he had intended to repay. The Boar, horrified at the accusation, was forced to search the riverbed in vain. This is why, even today, the pig can be seen constantly nosing around on the ground, still searching for the lost grinding stone. The Lesson: It is not the same humility used in borrowing money that is used in repaying it.

2. The Lizard's Judgement

The Lizard and the Wall Gecko were once the best of friends. The Lizard was honest and hardworking, while the Gecko was lazy and deceitful. One day, the Gecko tricked some lepers into giving him all their food, promising them a cure he did not possess. The Lizard, discovering this, went to the lepers and confessed his friend's crime. The Earth Goddess, Ala, angered by the Gecko's cruelty, struck him with leprosy. The Lizard, for his honesty, was blessed. From that day on, the Lizard lived freely, while the Gecko hid in the walls of houses, shamed and outcast. The Lesson: Never repay good with evil, and honesty is always rewarded.

3. The Elephant and the Ant

The Elephant was arrogant, believing his size and strength made him superior to all other creatures. He trampled anthills and drank from streams without sharing. One day, a tiny Ant crawled into the Elephant's ear and began to bite. The pain was maddening. The Elephant trumpeted, shook his head, and ran through the forest, but he could not dislodge her. Exhausted and humbled, the Elephant finally collapsed. The Ant crawled out and simply said, "Size is not everything." The Elephant never again disrespected the smallest of creatures. The Lesson: Do not despise the small, for even the smallest can bring down the mighty.

4. The Woodpecker's Pride

The Woodpecker was once a beautiful bird with a sweet song. He was the envy of the forest, but he refused to share his food during a famine, mocking the starving birds. When the famine ended, the other birds reported him to Chukwu. As punishment, Chukwu took away his beautiful feathers and his melodious voice. He was given a new task: to peck endlessly at the trees for insects. His song became a harsh, repetitive knock. The Lesson: Greed and selfishness lead to loss; share what you have, for abundance is meant to be shared.

5. The First Mermaid

Long ago, a young woman named Adaeze lived by the river. One day, she found a beautiful mirror in the water. It belonged to Mami Wata, the water spirit. Instead of keeping it, Adaeze returned the mirror, asking for nothing in return. Mami Wata, touched by her honesty, blessed Adaeze with wealth, beauty, and a family. She warned her to never forget the river. Adaeze prospered and brought offerings to the spirit every year, living a long and happy life. The Lesson: Kindness and honesty are always rewarded, especially by the spirits.

6. The Ogbanje Who Chose to Stay

Nneka was a heartbroken mother who had lost five children, all spirit-children known as ogbanje who returned to the spirit world. When she bore a sixth child, Obiageli, she feared the same fate. She went to a diviner, who told her the child had chosen to stay. Love for her mother had broken the pact with the spirit world. Obiageli grew strong and healthy, married, and had her own children. She taught them the story of her choice, proving that love can bridge the gap between the human and spirit worlds. The Lesson: Love can break even the strongest bonds, and the human and spirit worlds are not as far apart as they seem.

7. The King's Drum

A terrible drought struck a village. An old woman recalled a legend of a magical drum hidden deep in the forest, guarded by spirits, that could call the rain. The strong young men went to find it but returned empty-handed, having tried to take it by force. A young girl named Ada went next, armed only with faith and humility. She walked without fear or anger and was able to find the drum. She played it, and the rain returned. Ada did not boast, simply saying, "The rain came because we trusted." The Lesson: Faith and humility can achieve what strength and force cannot.

8. The Promise of the Kolanut

Okafor, a man known for his wisdom, broke a sacred promise to his dying brother to care for his children. He treated them as servants, keeping them hungry and scared. A stranger came to the village and refused to share a kolanut with Okafor, saying he would not eat until the truth was told. The stranger was the spirit of Okafor's brother, who had returned to judge him. Okafor was punished with poverty and shame, but his brother's children stayed and forgave him. The Lesson: Promises are sacred. Breaking a promise dishonors the ancestors and the kolanut that binds the living to the dead.

9. Why the Crocodile Lives Alone

Long ago, the Crocodile and his wife lived on the riverbank. The Crocodile was a hard worker, but his wife was lazy and greedy. She would eat all the food he caught before he returned home. Tired of her behavior, the Crocodile devised a test. He caught a large fish and told his wife to cook it. He then pretended to leave, but hid and watched. His wife ate the entire fish herself. The Crocodile was so disgusted that he drove her away from his home. From that day forward, the Crocodile has lived alone in the deepest parts of the river. The Lesson: Greed and selfishness drive love and companionship away.

10. The Wrestler's Ghost

There was a wrestler whose back never touched the ground. He was so proud that he boasted no one, man or spirit, could defeat him. A silent stranger with eyes that did not reflect light came to the village and challenged him. The match was strange. The wrestler could not move the stranger, who was as heavy as stone. The stranger then revealed he was a ghost from the land of the dead. The wrestler's back touched the ground in defeat, teaching him a lesson he never forgot. The Lesson: Pride goes before a fall, and the strongest man is weak before the spirit world.

11. The Shapeshifting Crow

Udenolu was a large, black bird known as the king of the crows. He possessed a magical gift: he could shapeshift. He often transformed into a handsome young man to walk among the villagers, tricking them and stealing their food. The people grew suspicious of this beautiful stranger who was always followed by a large crow. They set a trap. When Udenolu was discovered, he tried to fly away, but the villagers caught him. His shame was so great that he never again returned to the village in human form. The Lesson: Deceit may bring temporary gain, but it always leads to a fall.

12. The Tortoise and the Cane Rat

The Tortoise and the Cane Rat were friends, but the Tortoise was lazy. When the other animals went on a hunting expedition, the Tortoise refused to join them, preferring to stay and eat the food they had stored. The Cane Rat shared his food with the Tortoise, but soon the Tortoise ate more than his share. When the hunters returned, they found their stores empty and the Tortoise fat and happy. The Cane Rat, ashamed and angry, banished the Tortoise from his home, and the two were never friends again. The Lesson: Laziness and greed will exhaust the patience of even the best of friends.

13. The Mysterious Messengers

In the days when humans and spirits still walked together, the people were worried about death. They sent two messengers to Chukwu to ask for a reversal. One, a fast Dog, was told to deliver a message of life. The other, a slow Sheep, was told to deliver a message of death. The Dog, distracted by the smell of fresh palm oil, stopped to eat and forgot his errand. The Sheep, slow and steady, delivered his message first. And so, death became a permanent part of the world. The Lesson: Great consequences can hinge on small distractions.
