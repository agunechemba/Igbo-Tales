Moon 13: The Sky That Fed Us

1. The Voice Before the World

Before there was earth, before there was sky, before there was river or forest or the small red soil that stains the feet of farmers, there was Chukwu.

Chukwu was not a man. Chukwu was not a woman. Chukwu was the Great God, the One Who Created All Things, the source from which everything came and to which everything returns. The elders say that Chukwu existed alone, in a vast and silent darkness that had no beginning and no end. There was no sound. There was no light. There was only Chukwu, and Chukwu's thought.

And then Chukwu thought: Let there be something.

The thought became a word. The word became a breath. The breath became light, and the light spread through the darkness like palm oil poured on still water. And where the light touched, things began to exist.

Chukwu created the sky first. Not the sky as we know it now—far away, distant, untouchable—but a sky that lay close to the earth, like a blanket spread over a sleeping child. The sky was called Igwe, and Igwe was given the task of watching over the heavens, of holding the clouds and the stars and the moon in their proper places.

Then Chukwu created the earth. The earth was called Ala, and she was given the task of holding all things that live and grow. Ala was the ground beneath the feet, the soil that receives seeds, the womb that receives the dead. She was the goddess of fertility and morality, the one who would judge human actions and uphold the laws of the land. The elders say that Ala is so pervasive in Igbo consciousness that in traditional morning prayers, she is mentioned second only to Chukwu himself.

Chukwu created the waters—the rivers and the streams and the great ocean that surrounds the world. Chukwu created the forests and the trees and the animals that would dwell within them. Chukwu created the mmụọ, the spirits, who move between the visible and the invisible, carrying messages and watching over the balance of things. Chukwu created the alusi, the deities, who would serve as intermediaries between the human world and the divine.

And then Chukwu created light.

The light was called Anyanwu, the sun, and it was given the task of illuminating the day. The moon was called Mmanwu, and it was given the task of illuminating the night. In those early days, the Sun and the Moon were the closest of companions, living side by side in the sky, their light blending together in harmony. Wherever they went, joy followed. The earth below bloomed with plenty, the rivers sparkled with life, and all creation looked up with gratitude.

But the greatest creation was yet to come.

Chukwu took a handful of earth—the red earth of Ala—and shaped it into the form of a man. Chukwu breathed into the man's nostrils, and the man became a living being. His name was Eri, and he was the first of all humans, the father of the Igbo people. Eri was lowered down from the heavens onto an anthill in a land that was still waterlogged from the great flood that had covered the earth. The land was soft and wet, and Eri sat upon the anthill, not knowing what to do.

Then Chukwu spoke to Eri.

"You are the first," Chukwu said. "From you will come many. But you must learn to live with the sky, for the sky is my gift to you. As long as you honor it, it will feed you."

And so it was.

2. Eri and the Sky That Fed Us

In the days of Eri, the sky did not sit far above the earth as it does now. It rested just above the heads of men, close enough to touch, close enough to eat. When Eri was hungry, he reached up and broke off a piece of the sky. It tasted like whatever his heart desired—sometimes like roasted yam, sometimes like fresh fish, sometimes like the sweetness of palm wine that has not yet fermented.

Eri did not need to plant. He did not need to harvest. He did not need to cook. The sky provided everything. And the sky did not only feed Eri—it fed all the people who came after him, the children of Eri, the Umu-Eri, who spread across the land of the rising sun, the land that would one day be called Igbo land.

The elders say that during Eri's time, there was no sickness. There was no hunger. There was no war. The sky fed everyone, and no one took more than they needed. When a child was born, the sky provided. When an elder grew weak, the sky sustained them. When the people gathered to give thanks, they did not bring offerings of food, because food was always available. They brought offerings of gratitude.

Eri himself did not sleep as we sleep. He did not grow old as we grow old. The sky sustained him in a way that we can no longer understand. He walked among the spirits. He spoke with the animals, who in those days spoke the same language as men. He listened to the whispers of Chukwu, which came not from the sky alone but from the earth, from the trees, from the rivers, from the wind.

Eri had a wife, and together they bore children. The children bore children, and the world began to fill. The people did not build houses, for the sky was their roof. They did not dig wells, for the sky gave them drink. They did not hunt, for the animals were their companions. They did not fight, for there was nothing to fight over. Everything was shared. Everything was given.

But something changed.

The elders do not always agree on what it was. Some say that the people began to forget. They ate from the sky and did not give thanks. They took and took and did not remember the hand that gave. Others say that Eri himself grew proud. He began to think that the abundance was his own doing, that he had earned the sky's gifts, that he was the source of his own sustenance.

Still others say that it was not pride but death that ended the age of plenty. When Eri died—for even Eri had to die—the sky stopped feeding the people directly. Chukwu withdrew his hand. The sky rose, slowly at first, then faster, until it settled where it now sits, far above the reach of human hands.

And the people wept, because they had never known hunger before. They had never known work.

3. Why the Sky Moved Away

The elders say that the sky did not leave in anger. The sky left in sorrow.

For many seasons after Eri's death, the people continued to eat from the sky. They reached up and broke off pieces, as they had always done. But they had forgotten the lesson of gratitude. They took more than they needed. They broke off large pieces and threw away what they could not finish. They let the children trample on what had fallen to the ground.

The sky watched. The sky felt the waste. And the sky grew heavy with grief.

One day, a woman was pounding yam. She had taken a large piece of the sky and was preparing it for her family. But she was careless. She let pieces fall to the ground. She let the children play with what she had dropped. She did not stop them when they threw the sky's gift into the fire.

The sky saw this, and the sky spoke. Not in words that human ears could hear, but in a language that the earth itself understood. The sky said: If you cannot respect what is given, you will learn to work for what you eat.

And then the sky began to rise.

Slowly at first. The people reached up, and their fingers could no longer touch it. They stretched, and they stretched, and they cried out, but the sky kept moving. It rose above the trees. It rose above the hills. It rose until it was so far away that no human hand could ever reach it again.

And there the sky has remained, my child, far above us, where it watches but does not feed.

That is why we farm. That is why we cook. That is why we sweat under the sun, because the sun, Anyanwu, is the eye of the sky, and he sees everything we do. We work for our food now, and we must not waste it. The sky taught us that lesson.

But the sky did not abandon us entirely. When the rains fall, they come from the sky. When the sun shines, it warms the earth. When the moon rises, it lights the night. The sky is still there, still watching, still waiting. And every time we look up, we remember.

The elders say that this is not just a story about the sky. It is a story about everything. It is about the relationship between human beings and the cosmos. We are not separate from the sky, or from the earth, or from the spirits. We are part of one great whole. When we act with greed, we disturb that whole. When we act with respect, we keep it in balance.

4. The Quarrel Between Earth and Sky

After the sky moved away, the people learned to farm. They learned to plant yam and cocoyam, to harvest and to store. They learned to build houses with mud walls and thatched roofs, to dig wells and to make fire. They learned to live without the direct nourishment of the sky.

But the sky was not always kind.

There was a time, long after the age of Eri, when Earth and Sky quarreled. The elders say that the quarrel began over something small—a matter of pride, a matter of words spoken in anger. But whatever the cause, the result was terrible. Sky withheld rain for seven years.

Seven years without rain.

The rivers dried up. The streams became dust. The crops withered in the fields, and the yams that had been planted with hope yielded nothing but shriveled roots. The animals grew thin and weak. The people grew thin and weak. And because the earth was too hard to dig, the dead could not be buried. The hoes broke on the stony ground, and the bodies of the departed lay unburied, their spirits unable to find rest.

The people cried out to Chukwu. They cried out to Ala. They cried out to the ancestors. But the sky did not answer.

Finally, the people sent Vulture to plead with Sky. Vulture was chosen because he could fly high, higher than any other bird, high enough to reach the dwelling place of the sky. Vulture was given a message of suffering, a song of sorrow, a plea for mercy.

Vulture flew and flew. He flew until the earth was a small brown circle below him. He flew until he reached the gates of the sky. And there he sang. He sang of the withered crops and the dying animals. He sang of the broken hoes and the unburied dead. He sang of the hunger and the thirst and the grief of the sons of men.

Sky was moved to pity. Sky's heart softened. And Sky gave Vulture rain wrapped in leaves of coco-yam.

"Take this to the earth," Sky said. "Let it fall gently, so that the soil may drink and the crops may grow."

Vulture took the rain in his talons and began the long journey back to earth. But as he flew, his long talon pierced the leaves, and the rain fell. It fell not gently, but in torrents. It fell as it had never fallen before, flooding the rivers and drowning the fields. And so heavily did it rain on Vulture that he could not return to deliver his message. He flew to a distant land, where he saw a fire, and he warmed himself and ate the entrails of a sacrifice.

And that is why, whenever sacrifices are being offered, vultures are to be seen hovering in the sky, often descending to eat the substance of the sacrifice. And that is why Nigeria has two seasons: a rainy season and a dry season, to describe the effect of seasonal changes on migratory birds like the vulture.

5. The Fortune Hunt

There was once a young man named Emeka who was born into poverty. His father had nothing. His father's father had nothing. Their compound was the smallest in the village, their yam barn the emptiest, their goats the thinnest. And the people of the village said: "The Chi of that family has chosen poverty. There is nothing that can be done."

But Emeka did not believe them. He believed that a man could change his destiny. He believed that the ancestors did not mock those who worked. He believed that fortune was not given—it was hunted.

One day, Emeka went to the dibia. He said: "Tell me where fortune lives. I will go and find it."

The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He was silent for a long time.

"Fortune does not live in one place," he said. "Fortune lives in the places where men have not yet looked. Go east, to the land where the sun rises. Walk until the path ends. Then walk further. There you will find what you seek."

Emeka gathered his things. His mother wept. His father blessed him. And he set out to the east, where the sun rises, where the edge of the world touches the sky.

He walked for many days. He crossed seven rivers. He climbed three mountains. He passed through the territory of the Leopard, and the Leopard did not harm him, for the Leopard saw the hunger in his eyes and respected it. He passed through the village of the spirits, and the spirits did not detain him, for the spirits saw his purpose and honored it.

Finally, he came to the edge of the world. There, where the path ended, sat an old woman. She was so old that her skin was like the bark of the iroko tree, and her eyes were like the surface of a still pool.

"You have come for fortune," the old woman said. "Fortune is not a thing. Fortune is a choice."

"What do you mean?" Emeka asked.

"Fortune is the decision to keep walking when the path ends. Fortune is the courage to plant when there is no rain. Fortune is the wisdom to speak when others are silent. Fortune is not given. Fortune is taken."

She handed Emeka a single seed. "Plant this when you return home. Water it with your sweat. Guard it with your life. And when it grows, you will know what fortune is."

Emeka walked home. He planted the seed. He watered it every day with the sweat of his labor. He guarded it from the birds and the goats and the thieves. In time, it grew into a great tree. And under the tree, Emeka found that the yams in his barn had multiplied, the goats in his pen had doubled, and the respect of the village had returned to his name.

And Emeka learned that fortune is not found. Fortune is built. And the building begins with a single seed.

The Lesson: Fortune does not live in a place; it lives in the man who dares to seek it. Plant what you have, water it with work, and the harvest will be yours.

6. The Tortoise and the Lion King

In the days when the animals still held their councils and their kings sat upon stools of carved iroko, the Lion was king. He was not the strongest of the animals—the Elephant was stronger. He was not the fastest—the Antelope was faster. He was not the most cunning—the Tortoise was more cunning. But the Lion was the king because the ancestors had chosen him, and the ancestors do not explain their choices.

The Lion ruled with a heavy paw. He demanded tribute from every animal. He demanded the finest fruits from the Monkey, the sweetest honey from the Bee, the fattest antelope from the Leopard. And the animals paid, for they feared the Lion's roar.

But the Tortoise did not pay. The Tortoise was cunning, and the Tortoise was patient. He waited until the other animals had grown tired of the Lion's demands. He waited until they murmured among themselves. He waited until the murmurs became whispers, and the whispers became plans.

Then the Tortoise went to the Lion. He said: "Great King, I have found a treasure. It is buried at the foot of the great iroko tree. It is yours by right. But I am too small to dig it up. You must come and take it yourself."

The Lion was pleased. He followed the Tortoise to the great iroko tree. He began to dig. He dug and dug, and the earth grew deeper, and the hole grew wider. And while the Lion dug, the Tortoise gathered the other animals.

When the Lion had dug a hole so deep that he could not climb out, the Tortoise signaled the animals. They came from every direction—the Elephant, the Leopard, the Buffalo, the Antelope, the Monkey, the Goat. They surrounded the hole. They looked down at the Lion. And they said: "You have taken from us for the last time. You will not be king anymore."

The Lion roared. He tried to climb out. But the walls of the hole were too steep, and his claws could not find purchase. He tried to leap. But the hole was too deep. He was trapped.

The animals held a council. They decided that the Lion would be banished from the kingdom. He would be driven into the wilderness, where he would hunt alone, without tribute, without subjects, without a throne.

And so the Lion was driven out. And the animals chose a new king—the Tortoise, who had freed them with his cunning and his patience.

The Tortoise ruled for many years. He ruled with wisdom. He ruled with fairness. He ruled with the memory of the day he had defeated the Lion not with claws, but with a hole in the earth.

The Lesson: Cunning and patience can defeat strength. The one who digs the hole for another may be digging the hole for himself.

7. Gourd Daughter

There was once a woman named Nneoma who had no children. She had been married for many years. She had prayed to Chukwu. She had sacrificed to Ala. She had consulted every dibia in the land. But her womb remained closed.

One day, she went to the stream to fetch water. As she bent down, she saw a gourd floating on the surface of the water. It was small and round, and its skin shone like the moon. She reached into the water and picked it up. And as she held it, the gourd spoke to her.

"I have been waiting for you," the gourd said. "I will be your daughter. Take me home."

Nneoma was astonished. But she was also hopeful. She took the gourd home. She placed it on a soft mat in the corner of her hut. She spoke to it every day. She sang to it at night. She called it her daughter.

And in time, the gourd began to grow. It stretched and swelled. It took the shape of a child—a daughter with skin like the shell of a gourd and eyes like dark seeds. Nneoma named her Obiageli.

Obiageli grew strong. She grew beautiful. She grew wise. She helped her mother in the compound. She fetched water. She swept the floor. She cooked the yams. And she brought joy to a house that had been silent for many years.

But the other women of the village were jealous. They said: "That girl is not a real child. She is a gourd. She will return to the water." They mocked Nneoma. They mocked Obiageli.

One day, Obiageli went to the stream alone. She did not return. Nneoma searched. She called. She wept. But Obiageli was gone.

Nneoma went to the stream and sat by the water. She said: "If you have taken my daughter, take me too. I have nothing left."

The water stirred. The gourd rose to the surface. It spoke: "I did not leave you. I returned to the water because the water is my home. But I will always be your daughter. Come to the stream every day, and I will speak with you. I will sing to you. I will be with you."

And so, every day, Nneoma went to the stream. She sat by the water. She spoke with her daughter. And the gourd sang to her, and she was not alone.

The Lesson: Love is not bound by form. A child is a child, whether born of the womb or of the water. The ancestors give in ways we do not always understand.

8. The Tortoise and the Billy Goat

The Tortoise and the Billy Goat were neighbours. They shared the same plot of land. Their compounds faced each other across a narrow path. And they were friends, in the way that neighbours are sometimes friends—not because they liked each other, but because they had learned to tolerate each other.

Now, the Tortoise was cunning. And the Billy Goat was proud. This was a dangerous combination.

One day, the Tortoise said to the Billy Goat: "Let us make a farm together. We will plant yams. We will share the harvest equally."

The Billy Goat agreed. He was proud, and he wanted to show the Tortoise that he was a hard worker. He cleared the land. He dug the mounds. He planted the yams. And the Tortoise watched from the shade.

But the Billy Goat did not know that the Tortoise was also planting—secretly, at night, in a plot of his own. And when the rains came, the Tortoise's plot flourished while the shared farm struggled.

When it was time to harvest, the Tortoise said: "Let us divide the shared farm. You take what grows above the ground, and I will take what grows below."

The Billy Goat agreed. He did not understand what the Tortoise was doing.

But the yams grew below the ground. The Billy Goat gathered the leaves—the yam vines—and carried them home. The Tortoise dug up the yams and filled his barn.

The Billy Goat's family went hungry that year. The Billy Goat was ashamed. He said: "You have tricked me."

The Tortoise said: "You agreed to the terms. It is not my fault if you did not understand the yam."

The Billy Goat was furious. But he was also humbled. And from that day forward, he did not trust the Tortoise again.

The Lesson: Read the terms before you agree. Trust without understanding is the first step toward loss.

9. Mgbafo-Ocha

Mgbafo-Ocha was a woman of extraordinary beauty. Her skin was the colour of fresh palm oil. Her eyes were like the surface of a still pool. Her voice was like the sound of a bell. Every young man in the village wanted to marry her. Every old man wished he were young again.

But Mgbafo-Ocha was proud. She refused every suitor. She laughed at the farmer. She mocked the hunter. She rejected the trader. She said: "I will only marry a man who can bring me the moon."

The young men laughed. They said: "The moon is not a thing that can be brought. You are asking for what is impossible."

But Mgbafo-Ocha was serious. She said: "If you cannot bring me the moon, you cannot have me."

One day, a stranger came to the village. He was tall and silent. His eyes were like the eyes of the night. He said: "I can bring you the moon."

Mgbafo-Ocha laughed. "Then bring it," she said.

The stranger went to the stream. He looked into the water. The moon was reflected there. He filled a calabash with the water and carried it to Mgbafo-Ocha.

"Here is the moon," he said.

Mgbafo-Ocha looked into the calabash. She saw the moon. She was astonished. She had asked for the moon, and the moon had been brought to her. She did not know what to say.

The stranger said: "You asked for the moon. I have brought it. Now you must marry me."

Mgbafo-Ocha agreed. But she did not know that the stranger was a spirit—a spirit of the water, who had tricked her with a reflection. She went with him to the river. She disappeared beneath the surface. She was never seen again.

And the elders said: "Be careful what you ask for. The moon is not a thing that can be held. And the one who promises you the moon may be promising you your death."

The Lesson: Pride asks for what cannot be given. And the one who promises the impossible may be promising your destruction.

10. The Ogbanje

After the animals, the people learned about the spirits. And of all the spirits, none were more mysterious and more feared than the ogbanje.

The ogbanje were spirit children. They were not born of human parents. They were born of the spirit world, and they came to earth for a time, but they were never meant to stay. A mother would give birth to a child, love the child, care for the child. And then, when the child grew strong, the child would fall sick. And then the child would die. And then the mother would mourn. And then the mother would give birth again. And the same child would come back.

The elders say that the ogbanje are bound to the spirit world by a pact made before birth. They have friends in the spirit world, and they long to return. They are like travelers who leave their homes for a time, but who always yearn to go back.

But the ogbanje do not travel alone. They travel in groups, and they bring sorrow to many families. When one ogbanje child dies, others often die at the same time, in other families. They leave behind parents who are broken, mothers who are blamed for the deaths. The mother is said to be "infested" by the ogbanje spirit, and she is often treated with suspicion and scorn.

The people learned to fight the ogbanje. They went to the dibia, the healer, who knew the secrets of the spirit world. The dibia would search for the child's Iyi-uwa, a secret object that the ogbanje hid in the earth. This object was the tie that bound the child to the spirit world. If the dibia found it and destroyed it, the child could stay. The ogbanje would become like any other child, no longer running away to the spirit world.

Other times, the dibia would cut a mark on the child's body—a small scar, a tiny brand. If the child died and returned, the mark would still be there. The parents would know that it was the same child, and they would know that the ogbanje was not gone. The mark was a trap, a way to catch the spirit and force it to stay.

The elders say that the ogbanje are not evil. They are simply spirits who are caught between two worlds. They belong to the spirit world, but they have been born into the human world. They do not know where they belong. They do not know where they are wanted. And so they come and go, come and go, causing pain with every departure and every return.

11. The River Goddess

Of all the spirits, the most beloved and the most feared were the water spirits—the mmiri goddesses who lived in the rivers and the lakes and the streams. The most famous of these was Idemmili, whose name means "pillar of water."

Idemmili was the patron goddess of Nnobi, a town in the Idemmili South region. She was a river spirit, a tributary of the Niger, and like most water spirits, she was a fertility goddess. She controlled the rain-clouds in the sky. She decided when they would fall and when they would hold back. She was the mother of the waters, and she was the mother of the people who lived beside her.

The people of Nnobi told many stories about Idemmili. They said that long ago, when human beings and spirits were still close, Idemmili would walk among the people. She would come to the market and buy food. She would sit by the river and talk with the women who came to fetch water. She was not a distant goddess. She was a neighbor, a friend, a mother.

But Idemmili was also a dangerous goddess. She did not tolerate disrespect. She did not tolerate greed. She did not tolerate the breaking of taboos. When people violated her rules, she withheld the rain. When people forgot her, she flooded the land. When people took more than they needed, she took back what she had given.

The people of Nnobi learned to honor Idemmili. They built shrines for her. They brought offerings to her. They gave their daughters to her service. They kept her laws. And in return, Idemmili kept them safe.

But the worship of Idemmili was not without cost. In some communities, the river goddess was believed to demand the lives of those who had wronged her. Those who drowned in the river were said to have been taken by Idemmili. Those who fell sick after swimming were said to have been warned. And those who became priests of Idemmili were often women, for the goddess favored women, and she gave them power in a world where power was often denied to them.

The elders say that Idemmili is still there. She is still in the river. She is still watching. And she still remembers every act of kindness and every act of cruelty.

12. The Kolanut and the Ancestors

After the sky moved away, after the animals and the spirits had taken their places in the world, the people learned about the ancestors. The ancestors were the Ndi Ichie, the living-dead, those who had left their bodies but who still watched over their families. They were not gone. They were simply in another room, waiting for the time when they would be reborn.

But how were the people to speak with the ancestors? How were they to know what the ancestors wanted? How were they to honor the dead and keep the balance between the visible and the invisible?

The answer was the kolanut.

In the beginning, the elders say, Chukwu created the kolanut as a gift to humanity. It was a small nut, round and pale, with three or four lobes like the fingers of a hand. But it was more than food. It was a bridge between worlds. It was the first thing offered to any guest, the first thing used in any prayer, the first thing placed on any shrine.

When the kolanut is presented, it is not simply eaten. It is blessed. The host holds the kolanut in his hand and speaks to the ancestors. He speaks to Ala, the earth goddess. He speaks to the spirits of the land. He asks them to come and eat, to accept the offering, to bless the gathering. He breaks the nut into pieces, and the pieces are shared among the people. The sharing of the kolanut is a communion between the living and the dead, between the human world and the spirit world.

The ancestors are called to the gathering. They are asked to "eat" the kolanut and to leave the living unmolested. Bits of the nut are waved over the head—a symbolism expressing the exchange of kolanut for life—and thrown out to the spirits. The invitation to the kolanut moves in hierarchies, from the creator to the divinities, from Ala to the ancestors, and then to friendly spirits.

The kolanut is a symbol of hospitality. It is a symbol of peace. It is a symbol of unity. When a man presents kolanut to a stranger, he is saying: "You are welcome here. You are safe here. You are one of us now." When a man refuses kolanut, he is saying: "I do not trust you. I do not accept you. We are not one."

The kolanut is also a symbol of respect. It cannot be presented by just anyone. It must be presented by the host, through the ranking male of his lineage. It must be broken by the eldest man present. It must be distributed in order of seniority. Women, no matter their status, may not present kolanut. This is the way of the ancestors, the way of omenala, the customs of the land.

And so, whenever the people gather, they bring kolanut. They speak to the ancestors. They share the nut. And they remember that they are not alone. They are surrounded by the living-dead. They are part of a great chain of being that stretches from Chukwu above to Ala below, and from the first ancestor Eri to the last child born in the village.

13. The Woodpecker's Boast

After all the great stories had been told—the story of the sky, the story of the sun and moon, the stories of the animals and the spirits and the ancestors—there remained one story. A small story. A story about pride.

The Woodpecker, whose name was Oturukpokpo, was known throughout the forest as a great borer. He could cut into the hardest trees with his long beak. He could make holes in the trunks of iroko and mahogany. His pecking echoed through the forest, a sound that was heard from far away.

But the Woodpecker was also a boaster. He liked to talk about what he could do. And one day, he boasted to all the other birds.

"You know," he said, "I am the greatest of all the birds. I can cut down any tree. I can bore through any trunk. When my mother dies, I will pull down the great iroko tree with my long beak. I will make her a coffin such as the world has never seen. I will sing songs of mourning that will be heard from one end of the forest to the other."

The other birds laughed. They did not believe him. But the Woodpecker continued to boast. He boasted for many days. He boasted until all the birds knew his promise.

And then, one day, his mother died.

The Woodpecker remembered his boast. He flew to the great iroko tree. He looked at the trunk, thick and ancient and hard as iron. He lifted his beak to strike.

But his beak was not strong enough. He struck the tree, and his beak bent. He struck again, and it cracked. He could not cut the tree. He could not pull it down. He could not make the coffin he had promised.

The other birds gathered around and mocked him. "Where is the great borer now?" they asked. "Where is the one who said he would pull down the iroko?"

The Woodpecker was ashamed. He flew away and hid in the forest. And from that day forward, he never boasted again.

But he did not stop pecking. He still pecks at trees, still bores into the bark, still searches for the food that lies within. But he does not peck for glory. He pecks for survival. He pecks because that is what he knows, and because he must.

And that is why, even today, the woodpecker is a humble bird. He does not sing great songs. He does not boast of his strength. He simply works, quietly, in the forest, remembering the day his pride was broken.

14. Amadioha, the Thunder That Judges

Of all the deities of the sky, none was more feared and more respected than Amadioha, the god of thunder and lightning. The elders say that Amadioha is the son of Chukwu, and that he sits at the right hand of the Great God, watching the world below with eyes that see everything.

Amadioha is not a cruel god. He is a god of justice. When a man tells a lie, Amadioha hears. When a man breaks an oath, Amadioha sees. When a man takes what is not his, or harms the innocent, or betrays a trust, Amadioha remembers. And when the time is right, Amadioha speaks.

His voice is thunder. His anger is lightning. And when he strikes, there is no escape.

The people learned to swear oaths before Amadioha. When two men had a dispute, and no one could determine the truth, they would go to the shrine of Amadioha and swear. "If I lie," they would say, "let Amadioha strike me down." The liar would be afraid. His tongue would tremble. His knees would shake. And often, he would confess, rather than risk the wrath of the thunder god.

Amadioha is depicted as a ram. White rams are sacred to him, and it is the custom to offer white rams during sacrifices to him. When a man takes an oath and offers a white ram, the ram is released. It roams the village freely. No one may harm it. And if the oath is broken, the ram's death is the sign of the oath-breaker's fate.

The elders tell a story of a man named Nwosu. Nwosu was a powerful man, but he was also a cheat. He stole land from his brother. He took a widow's yams. He lied to the elders. And then, when he was accused, he went to the shrine of Amadioha and swore that he was innocent.

The priest of Amadioha said: "You are swearing a lie. Do not do this. The thunder sees you. The thunder remembers."

But Nwosu laughed. He took the kolanut. He broke it. He ate it. And he said: "If I lie, let Amadioha strike me down."

Seven days later, Nwosu was walking through the market. The sky was clear. There was no cloud. There was no wind. And then, in the space of a heartbeat, a single lightning bolt came down from the empty sky and struck Nwosu dead. The people were terrified. They ran to the elders. And the elders said: "Amadioha has spoken. The lie is revealed. Let this be a lesson to all."

And from that day forward, no man in that village ever swore a false oath again.

The Lesson: The truth cannot be hidden from the gods. Do not swear a lie, for the thunder is always listening.

15. Mbe in a Cesspit

Mbe the Tortoise was always looking for an easy meal. He did not like to work. He did not like to plant. He did not like to hunt. He liked to eat, and he liked to eat what others had prepared.

One day, he smelled something delicious coming from the compound of the Leopard. The Leopard had killed a goat and was cooking it in a great pot. The smell of the roasting meat filled the air, and Mbe's belly ached with longing.

Mbe went to the Leopard and said: "My friend, I have come to help you. The meat smells wonderful. Let me taste it, to make sure it is properly cooked."

The Leopard, who was not foolish, said: "No. You will not taste my meat. You will steal it."

Mbe went away, but he did not give up. He returned the next day with a plan. He said: "My friend, I have found a great treasure in the cesspit behind your compound. Gold and cowries and fine cloth. But I am too small to reach it. You must help me."

The Leopard was greedy. He followed Mbe to the cesspit. He looked in. He saw nothing but filth. But Mbe said: "Look deeper. The treasure is at the bottom."

The Leopard leaned further and further over the edge. And then Mbe pushed him. The Leopard fell into the cesspit. He struggled. He roared. But the walls of the pit were too steep, and the filth was too deep.

While the Leopard struggled, Mbe ran back to the compound. He ate the goat. He ate every last piece. He licked the pot clean.

When the Leopard finally climbed out of the cesspit, he was covered in filth and shaking with rage. He found Mbe sitting by the empty pot, his belly round.

"You have stolen my meat," the Leopard roared. "You have pushed me into the cesspit. You will pay for this."

But Mbe said: "I did not push you. I tried to save you. You fell in by yourself. And the meat—the meat was eaten by the birds while you were gone. I was only guarding the pot."

The Leopard did not believe him. But he could not prove otherwise. And from that day forward, the Leopard never trusted Mbe again. And Mbe, for all his cleverness, was never again welcome in the Leopard's compound.

The Lesson: Cunning may win a meal, but it will lose a friend. Do not celebrate too soon, for the one you trick may one day trick you.

16. Ofo: The Staff of the Ancestors

After the oracles, the people learned about the ofo. The ofo is not a man. The ofo is not an animal. The ofo is a stick—a simple wooden staff, cut from the branch of the ofo tree. But it is more than a stick. It is the symbol of truth. It is the staff of the ancestors. It is the one thing that cannot lie.

The elders say that when a man holds the ofo, he is not alone. His ancestors are with him. His father, and his father's father, and his father's father's father—all those who came before him are standing behind him, and they will not allow him to speak falsehood. To swear on the ofo is to swear on the lives of the dead. To break an oath made on the ofo is to bring shame upon the entire lineage.

The ofo is kept by the head of the family. It is kept by the elders. It is kept by the titled men and the priests. It is passed down from generation to generation, from father to son, from mother to daughter. When a man dies, his ofo is inherited by his heir. When a woman marries, she takes her ofo with her to her new home. The ofo is never given away. It is never sold. It is a part of the family, as much as the blood that runs in the veins.

The ofo is used in many ways. It is used to bless. When a father blesses his child, he holds the ofo and speaks words of peace and prosperity. It is used to curse. When a man has been wronged, and there is no other recourse, he holds the ofo and speaks words of judgment. The curse of the ofo is the most terrible of all curses, because it comes not from the man himself but from the ancestors behind him.

The elders tell a story of a man named Okoro. Okoro was a wealthy man. He had many farms. He had many cattle. He had many children. But he also had a brother, Nnamdi, whom he despised. Nnamdi was poor. Nnamdi was humble. Nnamdi had nothing but his ofo and his good name.

One day, Okoro accused Nnamdi of stealing his yams. It was a lie. The yams had been stolen by a thief from another village. But Okoro wanted to be rid of his brother, and so he brought the matter before the elders.

The elders asked Nnamdi: "Did you steal your brother's yams?"

Nnamdi said: "I did not."

Okoro said: "He is a liar. Let him swear on the ofo."

The elders agreed. They brought the ofo. Nnamdi took it in his hand. He spoke: "I did not steal my brother's yams. If I lie, let the ancestors strike me down."

And then something strange happened. Nnamdi's ofo began to glow. It glowed with a light that had no heat, a light that was not of this world. The elders fell to their knees. Okoro's eyes went wide with fear. And the ofo spoke—not in words, but in a presence that filled the room.

The ancestors were there. They had come to witness. And they had seen the truth.

Okoro confessed. He wept. He begged for forgiveness. He returned Nnamdi's yams and gave him half of his own land as penance. And from that day forward, no man in that village ever dared to make a false accusation before the ofo.

The Lesson: The ofo cannot lie. When you swear on the ancestors, you swear on the truth itself.

17. Agwu: The Spirit That Calls the Dibia

After the ofo, the people learned about agwu. Agwu is the spirit of divination. Agwu is the spirit of healing. Agwu is the spirit that calls a man or a woman to become a dibia—a healer, a diviner, a keeper of secrets.

The elders say that agwu is not a god. Agwu is not an ancestor. Agwu is a spirit—a wandering spirit that moves from person to person, from generation to generation. It is agwu that gives the dibia the power to see the unseen, to hear the unspoken, to know the unknowable. It is agwu that allows the dibia to cast the stones and read the patterns. It is agwu that allows the dibia to speak with the dead.

But agwu is also a dangerous spirit. Agwu does not come quietly. Agwu comes with madness. When a person is chosen by agwu, they do not simply decide to become a healer. They are seized. They fall sick. They see visions. They hear voices. They speak in tongues that no one understands. They wander into the forest, naked and wild, and they do not return until agwu has claimed them.

The elders tell a story of a woman named Ebele. Ebele was an ordinary woman. She was married. She had children. She was happy. And then, one day, agwu came to her. She began to hear voices. She began to see spirits. She began to speak words that no one could understand.

Her family was terrified. They thought she was mad. They took her to the dibia in the next village. The dibia cast the stones. He read the patterns. And he said: "This woman is not mad. She is chosen. Agwu has called her. She must become a dibia, or she will die."

Ebele was afraid. She did not want to become a dibia. She did not want to leave her family. She did not want to spend her life in the shrine, tending to the sick and the dying.

But agwu would not let her go. She grew sicker. She grew weaker. She stopped eating. She stopped speaking. Her family begged her to accept the call. And finally, she agreed.

Ebele was taken to the shrine. She was taught the secrets of the dibia. She learned to cast the stones. She learned to read the patterns. She learned to speak with the dead. She learned to heal. And when she emerged, she was not the same woman. She was a dibia. She had the power to see the unseen. She had the power to heal. And she had a new name—a name that was given to her by agwu.

Ebele became the greatest dibia in the land. She healed the sick. She settled disputes. She spoke with the ancestors. She brought peace to families that had been broken. And she told her own children: "If agwu calls you, do not refuse. It is a gift, even when it feels like a curse."

The Lesson: The spirit world chooses whom it will. Do not resist the call, for the call is a gift—even when it comes in sorrow.

18. Ikenga: The Right Hand of a Man

After agwu, the people learned about ikenga. Ikenga is not a god. Ikenga is not a spirit. Ikenga is a shrine—a carved wooden figure, shaped like a man with horns. And yet, ikenga is more than wood. Ikenga is the symbol of a man's right hand. Ikenga is the symbol of his strength. Ikenga is the symbol of his chi—his personal god, the spirit that guides his destiny.

The elders say that every man has an ikenga. When a boy is born, his father gives him an ikenga. It is a small one at first—a simple figure, without much detail. But as the boy grows, so does his ikenga. When he becomes a man, and takes his place in the village, his ikenga is replaced with a larger, more elaborate figure. And when he takes a title, or achieves something great, his ikenga is replaced again.

The ikenga is kept in the man's obi—his personal hut, the place where he receives guests and conducts the business of his family. It is placed on a small altar, along with the ofo and the kolanut. Every morning, the man greets his ikenga. He offers it palm wine. He asks it for strength. He asks it for guidance. He asks it to help him succeed in his endeavors.

The ikenga is not a charm. It is not magic. It is a symbol. It is a reminder. It reminds the man that his strength comes not from himself alone, but from his chi—the spirit that has been with him since before his birth. It reminds him that his achievements are not his own. They are the result of his ancestors' blessings, his chi's guidance, and his own hard work.

The elders tell a story of a man named Emeka. Emeka was a strong man. He was a hard worker. He was a good farmer. But Emeka neglected his ikenga. He did not greet it in the morning. He did not offer it palm wine. He did not keep it clean. He thought it was just wood.

Emeka's farms began to fail. His yams withered. His palm trees stopped producing. His children grew sick. His wife left him. Emeka did not understand what was happening. He went to the dibia and asked for help.

The dibia cast the stones. He read the patterns. And he said: "Your ikenga is angry. You have neglected it. You have not honored your chi. You have not remembered the ancestors who gave you your strength."

Emeka returned home. He took his ikenga from its forgotten corner. He cleaned it. He offered it palm wine. He greeted it every morning. And slowly, his fortunes turned. His farms began to produce. His palm trees began to yield. His children recovered. His wife returned. And Emeka learned that strength is not a thing you possess. It is a thing you honor.

The Lesson: The right hand is a gift from the spirit world. Honor the source of your strength, and it will remain with you.

19. The Masquerade That Comes from the Spirit World

After ikenga, the people learned about the mmanwu—the masquerades. The masquerade is not a man. The masquerade is not a spirit. The masquerade is both. It is a man, dressed in a costume, who has been transformed. When a man puts on the mask, he is no longer himself. He becomes the spirit that the mask represents. He becomes the ancestor who has returned. He becomes the presence of the dead among the living.

The elders say that the masquerade is a sacred thing. It is not a performance. It is not a game. It is a ritual. When the masquerades come out, the village stops. The drums beat. The women sing. The children hide. And the masquerades dance.

But the masquerade is also a dangerous thing. There are rules. The rules must not be broken. Women must not touch the masquerade. Women must not see the masquerade unmasked. Children must not follow the masquerade into the forest. If a woman touches a masquerade, she will die. If a woman sees a masquerade unmasked, she will die. These are not idle warnings. These are the laws of the spirit world.

The elders tell a story of a woman named Ada. Ada was a curious woman. She was not content to stay in the compound while the masquerades danced in the square. She wanted to see. She wanted to know. She wanted to understand what the men did in the forest when they prepared the masquerades.

One night, while the masquerades were dancing, Ada crept out of her hut. She followed the sound of the drums. She followed the shadow of the masquerade into the forest. And there, in the clearing, she saw what no woman was supposed to see. She saw the men removing their masks. She saw the faces behind the spirits. And she saw the secret of the masquerade.

Ada was horrified. She ran back to the village. But she could not keep the secret. She told her friends. She told her sisters. The secret spread through the women's quarters. And when the elders discovered that the secret had been revealed, they were furious.

The masquerade is not a lie. The masquerade is a truth. The masquerade is the spirit world. The masquerade is the ancestors. When the secret is revealed, the spirit world is dishonored. The ancestors are insulted. And the punishment is death.

Ada was taken to the shrine. She was given a bowl of poison. She was told to drink it, to atone for her sin. She drank. She died. And the village was cleansed. And the masquerades danced again, and the spirit world was honored, and the secret was kept.

The Lesson: The spirit world is not a game. Honor its secrets, for the secrets are sacred, and the sacred is not to be profaned.

20. Ekwensu: The Bargain in Darkness

After the masquerades, the people learned about Ekwensu. Ekwensu is the spirit of chaos. Ekwensu is the spirit of trickery. Ekwensu is the spirit of war and of bargaining, of chance and of misfortune. The missionaries who came later called Ekwensu the devil, but the elders say that is not quite right. Ekwensu is not evil in the way the missionaries meant. Ekwensu is the spirit of the unpredictable. He is the one who stands at the crossroads. He is the one who offers you a bargain, and the bargain is always a trap, but the trap is always a choice.

The elders say that Ekwensu was once a great deity. He was worshipped by traders and by warriors. When a man wanted to succeed in business, he prayed to Ekwensu. When a man wanted to win a war, he sacrificed to Ekwensu. But Ekwensu was never satisfied. Ekwensu always wanted more. And the more a man gave to Ekwensu, the more Ekwensu took.

The elders tell a story of a man named Ikenna. Ikenna was a poor man. He had nothing. He had no land. He had no cattle. He had no wife. He lived in a hut at the edge of the village, and he ate what he could find in the forest.

One day, Ikenna was walking in the forest, and he came to a crossroads. At the crossroads stood a figure. The figure was tall. The figure was dark. The figure had no face.

"Ikenna," the figure said. "I know your name. I know your hunger. I know your despair. I can make you rich. I can make you powerful. I can give you everything you have ever wanted."

Ikenna was afraid. But he was also tired of being poor. He asked: "What must I give in return?"

Ekwensu said: "Nothing much. Just the first thing that comes to meet you when you return home."

Ikenna thought about this. He had nothing. He had no one. Nothing would come to meet him when he returned home. So he agreed.

Ekwensu gave him a bag of cowries. Ikenna was overjoyed. He ran home as fast as he could. But when he reached the door of his hut, the first thing that came to meet him was his mother. She had come from her village to visit him, because she had had a dream that he was in danger.

Ikenna wept. He had promised his mother to Ekwensu. He tried to break the bargain. He offered Ekwensu other things. He offered his own life. But Ekwensu refused. "The bargain was made," Ekwensu said. "The bargain must be kept."

And so Ikenna's mother died. And Ikenna became rich. He had everything he had ever wanted. But every time he looked at his wealth, he thought of his mother. And every time he thought of his mother, his heart broke.

Ikenna learned that a bargain with Ekwensu is never a bargain. It is a curse disguised as a gift. And he told his story to the young men of the village, warning them: "Do not bargain with the spirit of chaos. His gifts are traps. His bargains are traps. And his traps are always baited with what you love most."

The Lesson: Do not bargain with chaos. What is gained through trickery will always be lost through sorrow.

21. Mbari: The House Built for Ala

After Ekwensu, the people learned about Mbari. Mbari is not a god. Mbari is not a spirit. Mbari is a house—a great house built of mud and thatch, filled with carvings and paintings and sculptures. But Mbari is more than a house. Mbari is a gift to Ala, the earth goddess. Mbari is a prayer made visible. Mbari is a sacrifice made of art.

The elders say that when Ala is angry, or when Ala is sad, or when Ala has been neglected, she sends a message to the people. She sends sickness. She sends famine. She sends drought. And when these things happen, the people go to the diviner. The diviner casts the stones. The diviner reads the patterns. And often, the diviner says: "Ala demands a Mbari. You must build her a house of art."

The building of a Mbari is a great undertaking. The whole village must participate. The men go into the forest to gather the materials—the wood for the frames, the clay for the mud, the raffia for the roof. The women prepare the food. The children carry water. And the artists—the sculptors and the painters—begin their work.

The Mbari is filled with images. There are images of animals—leopards and elephants and crocodiles. There are images of humans—men and women and children. There are images of spirits—the mmụọ and the alusi. There are images of ancestors—the Ndi Ichie. There are images of things that are sacred. And there are images of things that are forbidden, things that no one talks about, things that are hidden. In the Mbari, everything is revealed. The sacred and the profane. The beautiful and the ugly. The seen and the unseen. Nothing is hidden from Ala.

The elders tell a story of a village that refused to build a Mbari. The rains had stopped. The crops had failed. The people were starving. The diviner said: "Ala demands a Mbari." But the people were tired. They were hungry. They said: "We cannot build a Mbari. We do not have the strength."

The rains stopped entirely. The rivers dried up. The animals fled. The people began to die.

Finally, the elders went to the diviner again. The diviner said: "Ala is patient. But Ala is not eternal. If you do not build her house, she will leave the land. And when Ala leaves, there will be no land at all."

The people were terrified. They began to build the Mbari. They built it with what little strength they had. They built it slowly, over many months. And when it was finished, they brought offerings. They sang songs. They prayed to Ala.

That night, the rain fell. The crops grew. The famine ended. And the people understood that the Mbari was not just a house. It was a promise. It was a covenant between humans and the goddess of the earth. As long as they remembered her, she would remember them. As long as they honored her, she would sustain them.

The Lesson: The earth is our mother. Honor her, and she will provide. Neglect her, and she will withdraw.

22. The Ozo Title and the Man Who Refused It

After Mbari, the people learned about the Ozo. Ozo is not a god. Ozo is not a spirit. Ozo is a title—the greatest title a man can take in the Igbo land. The Ozo title is given to a man who has proven himself. He has shown that he is honest. He has shown that he is generous. He has shown that he is brave. He has shown that he is wise. When a man takes the Ozo title, he becomes a living ancestor. He becomes a judge. He becomes a priest. He becomes a keeper of the laws of the ancestors.

The taking of the Ozo title is a great undertaking. The man must spend years preparing. He must gather the materials—the cloth, the cowries, the goats, the kola. He must learn the secrets of the title. He must learn the songs and the rituals. He must be secluded for many weeks. During this time, he is in a state of liminality. He is not yet Ozo. He is not yet a living ancestor. He is between worlds. He must be careful. He must not speak to his wife. He must not eat certain foods. He must not touch certain things. If he makes a mistake, he will die.

When the seclusion ends, the man emerges. He is no longer just a man. He is an Ozo. He is given a staff. He is given a title. He is given a new name. He is now a member of the most prestigious society in the land.

The elders tell a story of a man named Obi. Obi was a great man. He was wealthy. He was respected. He was wise. When he was old, his people came to him and said: "Obi, you must take the Ozo title. You are the greatest man in the village. You deserve this honor."

But Obi refused. He said: "I am not worthy. I have made mistakes. I have sinned. I have not always been honest. I have not always been generous. I cannot stand before the ancestors and claim to be a living Ozo."

The people were confused. They had never heard of a man refusing the Ozo title. They said: "But you are the greatest man in the village. If you are not worthy, then who is?"

Obi said: "The Ozo title is not about wealth. It is not about respect. It is about truth. I have not always told the truth. I have not always lived the truth. So I cannot take the title."

And Obi did not take the title. But something strange happened. Because Obi refused the Ozo title, he became more respected than ever. The people saw that he was honest. They saw that he was humble. They saw that he was willing to put truth above honor. And they said: "Obi is not just a great man. He is a holy man."

When Obi died, the whole village mourned. And the Ozo men of the village gathered at his grave and said: "Obi was more Ozo than any of us. He refused the title, but he lived the truth. He is a living ancestor."

The Lesson: The greatest title is not the one you take. It is the one you live. Truth is greater than honor.

23. The Umuada: The Daughters Who Judge

After the Ozo, the people learned about the Umuada. The Umuada are the daughters of the lineage. They are the women who were born in the village and who married outside the village. They are the women who married within the village and who stayed. They are the women who are connected by blood to the land. They are the daughters of the soil.

The elders say that the Umuada are the most powerful women in the Igbo world. They are not the wives of the village—the ndi nwunye—who come from other lineages. The wives are outsiders. They have no ancestral roots in the village. But the Umuada are the daughters. They have the blood of the founders. They have the right to speak. They have the right to judge.

When there is a dispute in the village, and the men cannot resolve it, they call the Umuada. When a wife is mistreated by her husband, she calls the Umuada. When a widow is denied her inheritance, she calls the Umuada. When a man is cruel to his children, they call the Umuada. And the Umuada come. They come with sticks. They come with songs. They come with the power of the ancestors. And they judge.

The Umuada are not gentle. They are not patient. They are fierce. They are righteous. They do not care about the wealth of a man, or the title of a man, or the strength of a man. They care about the truth. They care about justice. They care about the law of the ancestors. And when they have judged, their judgment is final.

The elders tell a story of a woman named Nneoma. Nneoma was a young wife who had been married to a cruel man named Chukwudi. Chukwudi beat her. He starved her. He took another wife and neglected her. Nneoma had no one to turn to. Her own family was far away. Her husband's family would not listen to her. She was alone.

Then Nneoma remembered the Umuada. She went to the leader of the Umuada of Chukwudi's village. She told her story. The leader listened. She called the other Umuada. They gathered in the village square. They summoned Chukwudi.

Chukwudi laughed. "What can you do to me?" he asked. "You are women. You are not warriors. You are not elders. You have no power."

The leader of the Umuada stood before him. She was an old woman, bent with age. But her eyes were bright. Her voice was strong. She said: "We are the daughters of this land. We are the blood of the founders. We are the mothers of the village. And we have the power to curse you."

She raised her hands. She began to sing. The other Umuada joined her. They sang a song that had not been heard in a hundred years. It was a song of judgment. It was a song of the ancestors. And as they sang, Chukwudi's goats fell dead. His yams withered. His house caught fire. His second wife left him. His children refused to speak to him. Everything he had, everything he loved, was taken from him.

Chukwudi fled the village. He was never seen again. And Nneoma was taken back by the Umuada, who found her a new husband—a kind man, a gentle man, a man who would honor her.

The Lesson: The daughters of the land have the power of the ancestors. Do not wrong a woman, for her sisters will come.

24. Ogbunabali: The One Who Kills at Night

After the Umuada, the people learned about death. Death is not a god. Death is not a spirit. Death is a presence. Death is a shadow. Death is the Ogbunabali—the one who kills at night. The elders say that Ogbunabali was created by Chukwu to keep the balance of the world. Without death, the world would be overcrowded. Without death, there would be no room for new life. Without death, there would be no ancestors. Ogbunabali is not cruel. Ogbunabali is necessary. But Ogbunabali is also terrifying, because Ogbunabali comes without warning. Ogbunabali comes at night, when you are sleeping. Ogbunabali takes the young and the old, the rich and the poor, the good and the bad. Ogbunabali does not discriminate. Ogbunabali simply takes.

The elders tell a story of a man named Eze. Eze was a great warrior. He had killed many men in battle. He had taken many slaves. He had won many victories. He was not afraid of death. He said: "I am a warrior. When I die, I will die with my sword in my hand. I will not go quietly. I will fight."

One night, Eze was sleeping in his hut. His sword was beside him. His shields were on the wall. He was ready for anything. And then, in the middle of the night, he felt a cold hand on his shoulder. He woke. He reached for his sword. But his arms would not move. He tried to shout. But his voice would not come. He looked up, and he saw a figure standing over him. The figure was tall. The figure was dark. The figure had no face.

"I am Ogbunabali," the figure said. "I have come for you."

Eze struggled. He tried to fight. But his body would not obey him. He tried to pray. But his words would not come. He tried to remember the names of the ancestors. But his mind was empty.

Ogbunabali said: "You were a warrior. You killed many men. You took many slaves. You thought you were strong. But you are not strong. You are just a man. And all men die."

Eze died that night. His sword was in his hand. His shields were on the wall. But he could not use them. Death does not care about your weapons. Death does not care about your strength. Death is death.

The elders say that this is why we must live well. We cannot fight death. We cannot bargain with death. We cannot escape death. But we can live in such a way that when death comes, we are ready. We can honor the ancestors. We can keep the laws. We can love our families. We can tell the stories. And when Ogbunabali comes—and he will come—we can go with him in peace.

The Lesson: Death comes for all. Do not boast of your strength, for death is stronger. Live in such a way that you are ready when the night comes.

25. The Ikoro Drum and the Voice of the Village

After Ogbunabali, the people learned about the ikoro. The ikoro is not a god. The ikoro is not a spirit. The ikoro is a drum—a great slit drum, carved from a single log, hollowed out and shaped so that it makes a voice. But the ikoro is more than a drum. The ikoro is the voice of the village. The ikoro is the voice of the ancestors. The ikoro is the voice of the community.

The elders say that the ikoro is the largest drum in the village. It is so large that it cannot be moved. It is kept in the ilo—the village square—under a shelter. It is beaten only on special occasions. When a great man dies, the ikoro is beaten. When a war is declared, the ikoro is beaten. When a festival begins, the ikoro is beaten. When an important message must be sent, the ikoro is beaten. The sound of the ikoro carries for miles. It carries across the fields. It carries across the rivers. It carries across the hills. And when the people hear it, they know that something important is happening.

The ikoro is not just a drum. The ikoro is a language. The ikoro speaks. It speaks in rhythms and tones. It speaks in patterns that the people have learned to understand. When the ikoro beats a certain rhythm, the people know that a great man has died. When it beats another rhythm, they know that a festival is beginning. When it beats a third rhythm, they know that the village is at war. The ikoro speaks, and the people listen.

The elders tell a story of a village that lost its ikoro. The village was called Ndi-Uno. It was a great village. It had many people. It had many farms. It had many warriors. But one day, a terrible thing happened. The ikoro was stolen. A neighboring village, jealous of Ndi-Uno's greatness, crept in at night and took the great drum. They carried it away to their own village, and they hid it.

Ndi-Uno was lost. They had no voice. They could not call their warriors. They could not announce their festivals. They could not mourn their dead. They could not speak to the other villages. They were silent.

The people of Ndi-Uno went to their diviner. The diviner cast the stones. He read the patterns. And he said: "The ikoro is in the village of Ndi-Agu. You must go and take it back."

The warriors of Ndi-Uno prepared for war. They sharpened their machetes. They took their shields. They marched to Ndi-Agu. But when they arrived, they did not attack. Instead, they stood at the edge of the village and began to sing. They sang the songs of Ndi-Uno. They sang the songs that the ikoro had always beaten. They sang the songs of the ancestors.

The people of Ndi-Agu were moved. They had never heard such songs. They had never felt such power. They brought out the ikoro. They returned it to the warriors. They said: "Take your drum. Your voice is too strong. We cannot keep it."

And so Ndi-Uno had its voice again. And from that day forward, the ikoro was never stolen again. Because the people understood that the ikoro is not just wood and skin. The ikoro is the soul of the village.

The Lesson: A people without a voice is a people without a soul. Guard the voice of your community, for it is the voice of the ancestors.

26. Nzu: The White Chalk of Truth

After the ikoro, the people learned about nzu. Nzu is not a god. Nzu is not a spirit. Nzu is white chalk—a soft, pale clay that is found in the riverbeds and in the earth. But nzu is more than chalk. Nzu is the symbol of purity. Nzu is the symbol of truth. Nzu is the symbol of the ancestors. Nzu is the sign that the spirit world is present.

The elders say that when a man is marked with nzu, he is marked with the blessing of the ancestors. The nzu is placed on the forehead. It is placed on the arms. It is placed on the chest. It is placed on the face. It is a sign of peace. It is a sign of welcome. It is a sign of truth.

When a man is about to tell the truth, he marks himself with nzu. When a man is about to swear an oath, he marks himself with nzu. When a man is about to make a sacrifice, he marks himself with nzu. When a woman is about to give birth, she is marked with nzu. When a child is born, it is marked with nzu. When a man dies, he is marked with nzu. The nzu is the thread that connects the living and the dead.

The elders tell a story of a man named Okafor. Okafor was a trader. He traveled from village to village, buying and selling. He was known for his honesty. He was known for his truthfulness. When he spoke, the people listened. When he made a deal, the people trusted him.

But one day, Okafor was tempted. He was offered a great sum of money to lie. He was offered a fortune to deceive. He was offered a treasure to cheat. Okafor was poor. He needed the money. He wanted the money. He took the money. He lied. He deceived. He cheated.

The people discovered the truth. They were shocked. They brought Okafor before the elders. The elders asked: "Did you lie?" Okafor said: "No." But his voice shook. His eyes would not meet theirs.

The elders said: "You must swear on the nzu. You must mark yourself with white chalk and speak the truth."

Okafor was afraid. He knew that the nzu would reveal him. He knew that the ancestors would strike him down if he lied with nzu on his face. He tried to refuse. But the elders insisted. And so, trembling, Okafor took the nzu. He marked his forehead. He marked his arms. He marked his chest. And then he said: "I did not lie."

And the nzu began to burn. It burned like fire. It burned on his skin. It burned into his flesh. Okafor screamed. He tore at his face. He tried to wipe away the nzu. But the nzu would not be wiped away. It kept burning. It kept burning until Okafor confessed. He wept. He told the truth. He returned the money. And then the burning stopped.

The elders said: "The nzu is the truth. It cannot be worn by a liar. Do not swear on the nzu unless you are ready to tell the truth."

The Lesson: The nzu is the white chalk of truth. Do not wear it with a lie in your heart, for the ancestors will burn it into your skin.

27. The First Yam and the Law of Work

After nzu, the people learned about the yam. The yam is not a god. The yam is not a spirit. The yam is a crop—a root vegetable that grows in the earth. But the yam is more than food. The yam is the king of crops. The yam is the symbol of work. The yam is the symbol of manhood. The yam is the symbol of the covenant between humans and the earth.

The elders say that when the sky moved away, the people did not know how to farm. They did not know how to plant. They did not know how to harvest. They were hungry. They were confused. They cried out to Chukwu for help.

Chukwu heard their cry. He sent his messenger to the earth. The messenger carried two seeds—one was the seed of the yam, and the other was the seed of the cocoyam. The messenger gave the seeds to the people. He said: "Plant these. Water them. Tend them. And when they are ready, harvest them and eat."

The people did not know how to plant. They did not know how to water. They did not know how to tend. But they learned. They learned to dig the earth. They learned to plant the seed. They learned to weed the fields. They learned to harvest. And in time, they had yams. They had cocoyams. They had food.

The first harvest was a celebration. The people gathered in the village square. They brought their yams. They brought their cocoyams. They roasted them over the fire. They ate until they were full. They sang songs of gratitude. They danced in honor of Chukwu and of Ala.

And so began the New Yam Festival—the Iri Ji. Every year, before the new yams could be eaten, a festival was held. The people gave thanks. They honored the earth. They remembered the ancestors. They shared their food with the poor. They danced. They sang. They feasted. And they remembered that food does not come from the sky anymore. Food comes from the earth, and it comes through work.

The elders say that this is why the yam is the king of crops. It is not because the yam is the most delicious. It is not because the yam is the most abundant. It is because the yam taught the people how to work. It taught them that work is sacred. It taught them that the earth is generous, but only to those who honor her. It taught them that food is not a gift—it is a covenant. You work, and the earth gives. You neglect the earth, and the earth withholds.

The Lesson: Work is sacred. The earth does not give to the idle. Plant, and you will harvest. Neglect, and you will starve.

28. Ogu: The Woman Who Kept the Peace

After the yam, the people learned about Ogu. Ogu is not a god. Ogu is not a spirit. Ogu is a woman. Ogu is the spirit of peace. Ogu is the spirit of justice. Ogu is the spirit of the women who keep the world from falling apart.

The elders say that Ogu was a woman who lived long ago. She was a woman of great wisdom. She was a woman of great courage. She was a woman of great peace. When the men went to war, Ogu went to the battlefield. She stood between the armies. She raised her hands. She spoke words of peace. And the warriors lowered their weapons. They could not fight in her presence. They could not kill in her presence. She was Ogu. She was the spirit of peace.

The elders say that when Ogu died, she did not leave the world. She became the spirit of peace. She became the spirit of justice. She became the spirit of the women who keep the peace. Whenever there is a dispute, and the men cannot resolve it, they call on Ogu. Whenever there is a war, and the women want it to end, they call on Ogu. Whenever there is injustice, and the poor have no one to speak for them, they call on Ogu.

Ogu is the voice of the women. Ogu is the voice of the mothers. Ogu is the voice of those who have no other voice. When Ogu speaks, the world listens. When Ogu rises, the world stops. When Ogu is angry, the world trembles.

The elders tell a story of a war between two villages. The war had lasted for three years. Many had died. Many had been taken as slaves. Many had been driven from their homes. The two villages hated each other. They would not make peace. They would not listen to the elders. They would not listen to the diviners. They would not listen to the ancestors.

Then a woman named Nnebue came forward. She was the eldest woman of her village. She had lost her husband in the war. She had lost her sons. She had lost her brothers. She was tired of death. She was tired of hatred. She was tired of war.

Nnebue called the women of both villages. She told them: "We are the mothers. We are the daughters. We are the sisters. We are the wives. We have borne the children. We have buried the dead. We have wept the tears. And now we must stop this war."

The women listened. They agreed. They gathered together. They went to the battlefield. They stood between the armies. They raised their hands. They began to sing. They sang the songs of Ogu. They sang the songs of peace. And as they sang, the warriors lowered their weapons. They could not fight in the presence of the women. They could not kill in the presence of the mothers.

The war ended that day. The two villages made peace. They shared their lands. They shared their markets. They shared their daughters. And they never fought again.

The Lesson: Peace is the gift of the women. When the world is broken, it is the mothers who mend it. Honor the women, for they are the keepers of the peace.
