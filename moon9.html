Moon 9: Echoes From Your Ancestors

1. The Tortoise and the Palm Fruits

In the days when the animals still held their markets and their kings sat upon stools of carved iroko, the Tortoise was known throughout the forest for his cunning. But cunning, as the elders say, is a knife that cuts the hand that holds it.

There came a season when the palm trees in the forest were heavy with fruit. The branches sagged under the weight of ripe nuts, and the air was thick with their sweet scent. Every animal in the forest desired those palm fruits. The monkeys climbed high to pluck them. The birds pecked at the soft flesh. Even the elephants stretched their trunks to reach the lowest branches.

But the Tortoise could not climb. His legs were short. His shell was heavy. And so he watched from the ground as the other animals feasted above him.

One day, the Tortoise gathered all the animals in the village square.

"My friends," he said, "I have been thinking. Why do we climb these trees and risk falling? Why do we compete with one another for the fruits that hang so high? Let us agree to wait. Let us allow the fruits to ripen fully and fall to the ground on their own. Then we will all share equally."

The animals, who trusted the Tortoise, agreed. They stopped climbing the palm trees. They waited.

But the Tortoise did not wait. Each night, while the other animals slept, he crept to the palm trees with his family. They gathered the fruits that had fallen during the day and stored them in a secret bunker hidden deep in the forest. Night after night, they filled their secret store. Night after night, they grew fat while the other animals grew thin.

Finally, the Monkey grew suspicious. He followed the Tortoise one night and discovered the secret bunker. He saw the mounds of palm fruits, the evidence of the Tortoise's deception.

The next morning, the Monkey called all the animals together. They went to the Tortoise's compound and found the bunker. They found the stolen fruits.

The animals were furious. They dragged the Tortoise before the King. The King listened to the charges. He listened to the Tortoise's excuses. And then he pronounced judgment.

"You have stolen from your neighbors," the King said. "You have lied to those who trusted you. You have taught your children to do evil. For this, you will be punished."

The Tortoise was beaten. His family was banished. And from that day forward, the Tortoise was never again trusted with the secrets of the community.

The Lesson: Do not steal, and do not teach your children evil, for the consequences of deceit fall upon the entire family.

2. The Lizard and the Wall Gecko

There was once a friendship between the Lizard and the Wall Gecko. They were brothers in spirit, though not in blood. They shared their meals. They shared their secrets. They shared their dreams.

In those days, there was a colony of lepers living in the forbidden forest. The lepers were kind and gentle, though their bodies were marked by disease. They lived apart from the village, as custom demanded, but they did not complain. They tended their gardens. They sang their songs. They welcomed any creature that came to them in peace.

The Wall Gecko went to the lepers one day. He saw their suffering. He saw their kindness. And he made them a promise.

"I know a cure for your disease," he said. "If you let me into your village, I will heal you."

The lepers were overjoyed. They welcomed the Wall Gecko into their village. They shared their food with him. They gave him a place to sleep. They treated him as a brother.

But the Wall Gecko had lied. He had no cure. He had come only to steal from the lepers. Each night, while they slept, he took their food. He took their tools. He took whatever he could carry.

The Lizard discovered what his friend had done. He was ashamed. He went to the lepers and confessed the Wall Gecko's crime. He returned what he could of the stolen goods.

The Earth Goddess, Ala, saw what had happened. She was angry. She punished the Wall Gecko by giving him leprosy. She cured the lepers. And she blessed the Lizard for his honesty.

The Wall Gecko, now marked by disease and shame, could no longer live in the forest. He fled to the village and hid in the walls of human homes. He stays indoors, out of shame, trying to win back the favor of humans by living close to them. His friendship with the Lizard ended forever.

The Lesson: Never repay good with evil. Treachery is a mortal sin, and always give thanks after acts of mercy.

3. Why Bats Don't Fly During the Day

There was once a mother sheep whose lamb was weak and small. The lamb could not run with the other lambs. It could not play in the fields. It stayed close to its mother, trembling in the shade.

The Bat, who was known for his wickedness, saw the lamb and desired to harm it. He went to the mother sheep and said: "Your lamb is useless. It will never grow strong. Why do you waste your milk on it?"

The mother sheep, who was gentle and meek, did not answer. She simply held her lamb closer.

The Bat returned the next day. And the next. Each day, he taunted the mother sheep. Each day, he tried to steal the lamb's food. Each day, he tried to hurt the lamb when the mother was not looking.

The Tortoise, who always had a way of showing off his wisdom, saw what was happening. He went to the Bat and said: "Why do you torment this poor creature? What have they done to you?"

The Bat laughed. "I do what I want. No one can stop me."

The Tortoise said nothing. But he made a plan.

He told the animals of the forest what the Bat had done. He told them of the mother sheep's suffering. He told them of the lamb's starvation.

The animals were angry. They gathered around the Bat's hiding place. They waited for him to emerge.

But the Bat was clever. He hid during the day and only came out at night.

And so, from that day forward, the Bat could not fly during the day. The animals watched for him. The people watched for him. He was forced to hide in caves and dark places, emerging only under the cover of darkness. The mother sheep's lamb survived, and the Bat was punished for his wickedness.

The Lesson: Wickedness will be punished. Do to others as you would have them do to you.

4. The Tortoise and the Dog's Race

The King had a beautiful daughter. Her skin was the color of fresh palm oil. Her smile was like the sun breaking through clouds. And every young man and every creature in the kingdom desired to marry her.

Among the suitors were the Tortoise and the Dog. The Dog was strong and fast. He could run like the wind. He was confident, some said arrogant, but he had reason to be. The Tortoise was slow and small. No one believed he had a chance.

The King, not wishing to be accused of favoritism, set a date for a race. The winner would marry the Princess.

The Dog laughed when he heard the news. "The Tortoise? Race me? It will be over before it begins."

The Tortoise said nothing. But he had a plan.

The night before the race, the Tortoise filled a bag with cow bones. He ran along the race course, dropping bones at different points. Then he returned home and slept.

The day of the race arrived. The whole village gathered to watch. The Dog and the Tortoise stood at the starting line. The King raised his hand. The drum sounded. The race began.

The Dog shot forward like an arrow. He was far ahead of the Tortoise in moments. But then he saw a bone lying on the path. He stopped. He cracked the bone and ate the marrow. Then he ran again.

A little further, he saw another bone. He stopped again. He ate again.

And another.

And another.

Each bone slowed him down. Each bone gave the Tortoise time to catch up. The Tortoise crawled steadily, never stopping, never distracted. And when the Dog finally reached the finish line, his belly full of bones, the Tortoise was already there.

The King had no choice but to hand over the Princess to the victorious Tortoise. The Dog, his pride broken, slunk away in shame.

The Lesson: Pride goes before a fall. The race is not always to the swift, but to the steady.

5. Mbe and the Singing Contest in the Sky

Mbe the Tortoise heard that the birds were having a singing contest in the sky. The prize was the hand of the beautiful daughter of the Sun. Mbe, who could not fly and could not sing, was determined to win.

He tricked the birds into lending him feathers, as he had done before. He tied the feathers to his shell and flew with them to the sky.

But when he arrived, he did not enter the singing contest. Instead, he went to the Sun's daughter and spoke to her privately.

"I have brought you a secret message from your mother, the Moon," he said. "She says you must not marry a singer, but a drummer. For the drum is the heartbeat of the world."

The Sun's daughter respected her mother deeply. She went to her father and told him she would only marry the best drummer.

All the birds were terrible drummers. They had come to sing, not to drum. They had no rhythm. They had no skill.

But Mbe had spent his whole life drumming on his own shell. He played a magnificent and complex rhythm. The drumming echoed through the sky. The Sun's daughter smiled. The Sun himself nodded in approval.

Mbe was declared the winner. He had won a singing contest without singing a single note, by changing the rules of the game to fit his own unique talent.

The Lesson: Wisdom is greater than strength. The clever mind finds a way where the strong body cannot.

6. The Long-Necked Woman

Chike was a young man who had grown tired of the women in his village. He had courted many of them. He had found them all lacking. They were too loud. They were too demanding. They were too ordinary.

One day, a stranger came to the village. She was tall and beautiful, with skin like polished mahogany. But there was something strange about her. Her neck was longer than any human neck should be. It stretched up and up, like the trunk of a palm tree.

Chike did not care. He was enchanted. He approached her. He spoke to her. He asked for her hand in marriage.

The woman smiled. "You do not know me," she said. "You do not know where I come from."

"I do not care," Chike said. "I love you."

They were married. They went to live in a house at the edge of the village. For a time, they were happy. The woman was kind. She was gentle. She cooked and cleaned and cared for Chike.

But at night, when the moon rose, she would disappear. Chike would wake and find her gone. He would search the house, the compound, the forest. But she was nowhere to be found.

One night, Chike pretended to sleep. He watched through half-closed eyes as his wife rose from the bed. He watched as she walked to the window. He watched as her neck began to stretch, longer and longer, until it reached the sky. Her head disappeared into the clouds.

Chike followed her. He climbed to the roof of the house. He looked up. And there, in the moonlight, he saw her. She was not a woman. She was a spirit. Her true form was a thing of bone and shadow, a creature of the spirit world who had taken human shape to walk among men.

Chike screamed. The spirit turned. Her eyes were empty. Her mouth was a hollow. And then she was gone.

Chike never saw her again. He returned to the village a broken man. And he warned all the young men: "Do not marry a woman you do not know. Do not love a face without knowing the spirit behind it."

The Lesson: Appearances deceive. True love requires knowledge, not just desire.

7. Mbe's Greed

There was a famine in the land, and Mbe the Tortoise was hungry. He went to the farm of the Monkey, who had a fine harvest of yams, and begged for a share. The Monkey, who was kind, gave him ten yams. But Mbe ate them in one night and returned the next day, begging for more. The Monkey gave him twenty. Mbe ate them and returned again. The Monkey gave him fifty. Mbe ate them and returned again.

Finally, the Monkey grew suspicious. He hid and watched Mbe. He saw Mbe take the yams not to his own compound but to a secret store he had built in the forest. The store was already full of yams Mbe had gathered from other animals—from the Goat, from the Antelope, from the Squirrel. All of them had given him food, believing him hungry. All of them had been deceived.

The Monkey told the other animals. They went to the secret store together. They found their own yams there, piled high, while Mbe's own barn was empty—for he had eaten everything he had been given and then stolen more.

Mbe was brought before the King. He tried to lie. He said the yams were his own. But the animals testified. The evidence was clear.

The King pronounced judgment. "You have disguised your greed as hunger. You have taken from those who trusted you. You have made hunger itself a lie. For this, you will be punished."

Mbe was made to return every yam he had stolen—twice over. His own compound was emptied as a fine. And he was driven from the market for a full year, so that he could not beg again.

And from that day forward, the animals knew that Mbe's hunger was not hunger at all—it was greed, and greed is never satisfied.

The Lesson: Greed disguises itself as need, but the ancestors see the truth. Do not take what is not yours, and do not lie to those who help you.

8. The Elephant and the Ant

The Elephant was the largest creature in the forest. He was strong. He was powerful. He was feared by all the other animals. When he walked, the ground trembled. When he trumpeted, the birds flew from the trees.

But the Elephant was not kind. He was arrogant. He believed that his size made him superior to all others. He trampled the ant hills without care. He drank from the streams without sharing. He took what he wanted and gave nothing in return.

The Ant was small. She was insignificant. The Elephant did not even notice her. He did not see her carrying food back to her colony. He did not see her working day and night. He did not see her building her home.

One day, the Elephant found a tree full of ripe fruits. He began to eat. He ate and he ate. He ate until his belly was full. And then he lay down to sleep beneath the tree.

The Ant saw her opportunity. She crawled onto the Elephant's trunk. She crawled into his ear. And there, in the soft darkness of the Elephant's ear, she began to bite.

The Elephant woke with a start. He trumpeted in pain. He shook his head. He stamped his feet. But the Ant would not leave. She bit and she bit, driving the Elephant mad.

The Elephant ran through the forest, crashing into trees, trampling bushes, desperate to escape the pain. He ran until he could run no more. He collapsed, exhausted, in a clearing.

The Ant crawled out of his ear and stood on his trunk.

"You are large," she said. "But size is not everything. I am small, but I am patient. I am small, but I am determined. And I have taught you a lesson you will never forget."

The Elephant never again trampled the ant hills. He never again drank from the streams without sharing. And he learned to respect even the smallest creatures, for they too have their place in the world.

The Lesson: Do not despise the small, for even the smallest creature can bring down the mighty.

9. Why the Woodpecker Pecks

The Woodpecker was once a beautiful bird. His feathers were bright and colorful. His song was sweet and melodious. He was the pride of the forest, the envy of all the other birds.

But the Woodpecker was also proud. He believed his beauty made him better than the other birds. He mocked the dull feathers of the Sparrow. He laughed at the harsh call of the Crow. He refused to share his food with anyone.

One day, a great famine came to the forest. The rains did not fall. The fruits did not grow. The streams dried up. The birds began to starve.

The Woodpecker, who had always stored more food than he needed, had enough to survive. But he refused to share. He watched the other birds grow thin. He watched them weaken. He watched them die.

When the famine ended, the other birds gathered. They went to the Woodpecker and asked: "Why did you not share with us? We would have shared with you."

The Woodpecker laughed. "Why should I share? I am beautiful. I am special. I do not need you."

The birds were angry. They went to Chukwu and told him what the Woodpecker had done.

Chukwu summoned the Woodpecker. "You have been given beauty and abundance," he said. "But you have used them for selfishness. You will be punished."

And so Chukwu took away the Woodpecker's beautiful feathers. He made them dull and brown. He took away the Woodpecker's sweet song. He made it a harsh, repetitive sound. And he gave the Woodpecker a new task: to peck at the trees, searching for the insects that would be his food. For the rest of his days, the Woodpecker would work hard for every meal.

And that is why, even today, the Woodpecker pecks at the trees. He is still searching. He is still working. And he remembers what he lost when he refused to share.

The Lesson: Greed and selfishness lead to loss. Share what you have, for abundance is meant to be shared.

10. The Girl and the Mami Wata

There was once a girl named Adaeze who lived in a village by the river. She was beautiful and kind, but she was also poor. Her parents had died, and she had no one to care for her. She spent her days washing clothes in the river, her hands red and raw from the water.

One day, as she washed, she saw something glinting in the water. She reached in and pulled out a mirror. It was made of silver and gold, and its surface was smooth as glass.

Adaeze looked into the mirror. And in the mirror, she saw a woman. The woman was not her reflection. The woman was beautiful beyond description. Her skin was the color of moonlight. Her hair was long and flowing. Her eyes were deep as the river.

"I am Mami Wata," the woman said. "I am the spirit of the waters. You have found my mirror, and for that, you will be rewarded."

Adaeze was afraid. She had heard stories of Mami Wata. She knew that the water spirit could be generous or cruel. She knew that those who accepted her gifts often paid a terrible price.

"I do not want a reward," Adaeze said. "I only want to return your mirror."

Mami Wata smiled. "You are different from the others. They always want something. You only want to do what is right. For this, you will be blessed."

And so Mami Wata blessed Adaeze. She gave her wealth and beauty. She gave her a home and a family. But she also gave her a warning: "Never forget where you came from. Never forget the river. And never forget that the water remembers everything."

Adaeze never forgot. She became a wealthy woman, but she never forgot the river. She brought offerings to Mami Wata every year. She taught her children to respect the waters. And she lived a long and happy life, always remembering the day she found the mirror and the spirit who gave her everything.

The Lesson: Kindness and honesty are rewarded. Respect the spirits, and they will respect you.

11. The Ogbanje Who Stayed

There was once a woman named Nneka who had given birth to five children. All five had died. Each had lived only a few years. Each had been beautiful. Each had been loved. And each had been taken.

Nneka was heartbroken. She went to the diviner and asked: "What is happening to my children? Why are they dying?"

The diviner cast the stones and read the patterns. "Your children are ogbanje," he said. "They are spirit children. They come to earth for a time, but they always return to the spirit world. They make a pact before birth. They have friends in the spirit world. They cannot stay."

"What can I do?" Nneka asked.

The diviner told her to find the Iyi-uwa, the secret object that bound the child to the spirit world. "If you find it and destroy it," he said, "the child will stay."

Nneka searched. She dug in the compound. She searched the forest. She looked everywhere. But she could not find the Iyi-uwa.

Then she became pregnant again. She gave birth to a daughter. She named her Obiageli. And she watched over her day and night, terrified that the child would die.

But Obiageli did not die. She grew strong. She grew healthy. She laughed and played and filled the compound with joy.

Nneka went back to the diviner. "She is still here," she said. "Why is she still here?"

The diviner cast the stones again. "The child has chosen to stay," he said. "She has broken the pact. She has chosen the human world over the spirit world. She loves you, and she does not want to leave."

Nneka wept with joy. She held her daughter close. And from that day forward, Obiageli stayed. She grew into a woman. She married and had children of her own. And she told her children the story of how she chose to stay.

The Lesson: Love can break even the strongest bonds. The spirit world and the human world are not so far apart, and love can bridge the gap between them.

12. The Drum That Called the Rain

There was once a village that had no rain. For three years, the sky had been empty. The crops had withered. The rivers had dried. The animals had fled. The people were dying.

The elders gathered. They consulted the diviner. They prayed to Chukwu. They made sacrifices to Ala. But nothing worked. The sky remained silent.

Then an old woman spoke. She was the oldest person in the village, and she remembered things that others had forgotten.

"I remember a story," she said. "A story my grandmother told me. There was once a drum that could call the rain. It was hidden in the forest, guarded by the spirits. Whoever found it could bring the rain back to the land."

The young men of the village were eager to find the drum. They gathered their weapons and their torches. They prepared to enter the forest.

But the old woman stopped them. "The drum cannot be taken by force," she said. "It must be earned. Only the pure of heart can find it. Only the humble can play it."

The young men laughed. They were strong. They were brave. They did not believe in such things.

They entered the forest. They searched for days. They fought the spirits. They were defeated. They returned empty-handed.

Then a young girl named Ada spoke. "Let me go," she said. "I am not strong. I am not brave. But I am pure of heart. I will find the drum."

The elders agreed. Ada entered the forest alone. She did not carry a weapon. She did not carry a torch. She carried only her faith.

She walked for many days. She faced many dangers. But she did not fight. She did not run. She simply walked, trusting that the spirits would guide her.

Finally, she found the drum. It was hidden in a cave, guarded by an old spirit. The spirit looked at her and smiled.

"You are the first to come without anger," the spirit said. "You are the first to come without fear. Take the drum. Play it. And the rain will return."

Ada took the drum. She played it. And the sky opened. The rain fell. The rivers filled. The crops grew. The people rejoiced.

Ada returned to the village a hero. But she did not boast. She did not claim credit. She simply said: "The rain came because we trusted. The rain came because we believed."

The Lesson: Faith and humility can achieve what strength and force cannot.

13. The Kolanut and the Broken Promise

There was once a man named Okafor who was known throughout the village for his wisdom. People came to him for advice. They came to him for judgment. They trusted him because he always spoke the truth.

But Okafor had a secret. He had made a promise to his brother many years ago. He had promised to care for his brother's children if anything happened to him. And then his brother had died. And Okafor had taken the children into his home.

But Okafor did not care for them as he had promised. He treated them as servants. He gave them the worst food. He made them sleep on the floor. He beat them when they made mistakes.

The children grew thin. They grew sad. They grew silent. And the village began to whisper.

One day, a stranger came to the village. He was an old man with a white beard and eyes that saw everything. He asked for Okafor.

"I have heard of your wisdom," the stranger said. "I have come to learn from you."

Okafor was flattered. He welcomed the stranger into his home. He presented the kolanut.

But the stranger refused. "I will not eat kolanut with you," he said. "Not until you have told me the truth."

Okafor was confused. "What truth?"

"The truth about your brother's children," the stranger said. "The truth about the promise you broke. The truth about the kolanut you present to the ancestors while you betray their blood."

Okafor was ashamed. He fell to his knees. He confessed. He wept.

The stranger was not a stranger at all. He was the spirit of Okafor's brother, returned from the spirit world to judge him.

"You broke your promise," the spirit said. "You betrayed your blood. You dishonored the kolanut. For this, you will be punished."

And so Okafor was punished. He lost his wealth. He lost his reputation. He lost his place in the village. But he did not lose the children. They stayed with him. They cared for him. They forgave him.

And Okafor learned that forgiveness is the greatest gift, and that even the most broken promise can be mended by love.

The Lesson: Promises are sacred. The kolanut binds the living to the dead, and breaking a promise dishonors both.

14. The Food Drum

There was once a young man named Okafor who was poor but honest. He had no land. He had no cattle. He had only his two hands and his good name. And he was content, for he had learned that contentment is greater than wealth.

One day, while walking in the forest, Okafor came upon a clearing where seven spirits were seated around a drum. The spirits did not harm him. They welcomed him. They invited him to sit with them.

"We have watched you," they said. "You are poor but honest. You are humble but brave. We will give you a gift."

They gave him the drum. "Beat it once," they said, "and it will give you food. Beat it twice, and it will give you drink. Beat it three times, and it will give you cloth. But use it wisely, for the drum remembers."

Okafor took the drum home. He beat it once, and food appeared—yams and cocoyams and honey and roasted fish. He beat it twice, and palm wine flowed into calabashes. He beat it three times, and fine cloth unrolled at his feet.

Okafor shared with his neighbours. He fed the hungry. He clothed the naked. He gave wine to the thirsty. The village rejoiced, and Okafor became known as the most generous man in the land.

But Okafor's brother, Ekwe, was jealous. He wanted the drum for himself. One night, he crept into Okafor's hut and stole the drum. He ran to a distant village and beat it seven times in a row, demanding more and more and more.

The drum grew angry. It had been made for generosity, not for greed. Instead of food and drink, it produced scorpions and snakes and fire. Ekwe was stung and burned. He fled into the forest, screaming, and was never seen again.

Okafor found the drum abandoned in the forest. He returned it to the seven spirits and thanked them. The spirits blessed him. They said: "You have used the drum as it was meant to be used. The drum will always remember you."

And Okafor lived a long and peaceful life, and when he died, the drum was buried with him, so that no one else would ever abuse it.

The Lesson: A gift shared in gratitude multiplies. A gift abused destroys the abuser. The ancestors see how you use what they give you.

15. How the Tortoise Got a Wife

There was once a King who had a daughter so beautiful that suitors came from every corner of the land. Princes brought cattle. Warriors brought elephant tusks. Even the Leopard, the strongest of all the animals, laid his finest kill at the King's feet.

But the Princess would look at each suitor and shake her head. "I will only marry the man who can bring me an elephant," she said. "I want to ride on its back through the village, for the elephant is the symbol of royalty."

The strongest men tried to capture an elephant. They dug pits and set traps. But the elephant was clever and strong, and they all failed.

Now, Mbe the Tortoise heard of the Princess's wish. He was small and slow. He had nothing to offer. But Mbe had something greater than strength—he had cunning.

He went to the Elephant and said: "My great friend, the King has invited you to a festival. He wishes to appoint you as the chairman of the occasion. It is a great honour."

The Elephant was flattered. "I will go at once," he said.

On the day of the festival, Mbe arrived at the Elephant's house. They walked together toward the King's compound. But Mbe was too slow to keep up.

"My friend," Mbe said, "let me sit on your back. From there, I can direct you. And we will arrive faster."

The Elephant agreed. But Mbe could not climb onto his back. His legs were too short. His shell was too heavy. He tried and he tried, and each time he fell to the ground.

"I cannot climb," Mbe said. "But I have a rope. Let me tie it around your neck. I will hold on to the other end, and you can pull me along behind you."

The Elephant, eager to reach the festival, agreed. Mbe tied the rope around the Elephant's neck and held the other end tight.

As they approached the King's compound, Mbe began to sing:

"Nnam eze akpatam enyi! My King, I have brought the Elephant!"

The Elephant heard him and asked: "What are you saying, Tortoise?"

"I said, let the earth protect and guide the Elephant," Mbe replied. "Did I say I brought the Elephant?"

And he sang again: "Nnam eze akpatam enyi!"

The people of the village heard the song and came running. They saw the Tortoise leading the great Elephant by a rope. They cheered. The Elephant, confused and ashamed, could not free himself without trampling the crowd.

Mbe led the Elephant to the King's palace and handed the rope to the King. The Princess, true to her word, had no choice. She climbed onto the Elephant's back and rode through the village. And Mbe, the smallest of all creatures, had won the hand of the King's daughter.

The Lesson: Wisdom is greater than strength. The clever mind finds a way where the strong body cannot. Do not judge a suitor by his size, but by his wit.

16. The Tortoise and the Palm Wine

There was once a great palm wine tapper named Ekwe. He tapped the finest palm trees in the forest and collected the sweetest wine in the land. Every morning, he climbed the trees and hung his calabashes to catch the sap. Every evening, he gathered the calabashes and carried them to the village, where they were sold at the market.

Mbe the Tortoise loved palm wine. He loved it more than food, more than sleep, more than anything. But Mbe was poor. He had no cowries to buy the wine. So he watched from the edge of the forest as Ekwe carried the calabashes past him, and his mouth watered.

One day, Mbe had an idea. He went to Ekwe and said: "My friend, you work so hard. Let me help you. I will guard your palm trees at night, so that no thief steals your wine. In return, you will give me one calabash of wine each day."

Ekwe was grateful. He agreed. And so, every night, Mbe went to the palm trees and guarded them. Every morning, Ekwe gave him a calabash of wine.

But Mbe did not guard the trees. Instead, he drank the wine that dripped from the trees. He drank until he was drunk. He drank until he could not walk. And then he slept under the trees until morning.

One night, the Leopard came to the palm trees. He saw Mbe lying drunk on the ground. He saw the wine dripping from the trees. And he drank his fill.

In the morning, Ekwe came to gather his wine. The calabashes were empty. The wine was gone. He found Mbe asleep under the trees, his shell stained with palm wine.

Ekwe was furious. He dragged Mbe to the village and told the elders what had happened. The elders listened. They looked at Mbe's stained shell. They looked at the empty calabashes.

"You were trusted," the elders said. "You were given wine as payment. But you stole more. You drank until you could not stand. And you allowed a thief to take what was not yours. For this, you will be punished."

Mbe was beaten and driven from the village. He was never again trusted with the wine. And from that day forward, the Tortoise was known as a drunkard and a thief.

The Lesson: Do not abuse trust. A gift received with gratitude is a blessing. A gift stolen is a curse.

17. Mbe and the Dog's Funeral

The Dog's mother died. All the animals gathered in the Dog's compound to mourn, for the Dog was well-loved in the village. They came with yams and palm oil and cloth. They came with pots of wine and baskets of kola nut. They came to honour the dead and to comfort the living.

Mbe the Tortoise, who was the Dog's closest friend, was appointed chief mourner. He wept louder than anyone. He wailed and tore at his clothes. He threw himself on the ground and called out to the ancestors. "The mother of my friend! The mother of my brother! How can the world continue without her?"

The animals were moved by his grief. "Mbe truly loved the Dog's mother," they said. "See how he mourns."

But Mbe's grief was a lie. While the animals slept that night, Mbe crept into the Dog's yam barn and stole every single yam that had been given as a funeral gift. He carried them, one by one, to his own compound and hid them in his secret store.

In the morning, the Dog went to his barn to distribute the yams to the poor, as custom demanded. The barn was empty. The yams were gone.

The Dog wept. He went to the elders and told them what had happened. The elders summoned the animals and asked: "Who has stolen from the dead?"

No one answered. But the Monkey, who had been watching, stepped forward. "I saw Mbe go to the barn last night," he said. "I saw him carry the yams away."

The animals went to Mbe's compound and found the yams. They dragged Mbe before the elders. Mbe tried to deny it. He said the yams were his own. He said the Monkey was lying. He said the Dog had given them to him.

But the evidence was clear. The elders pronounced judgment: "You have stolen from the dead. You have betrayed your friend. You have mocked the ancestors with your false tears. For this, you will be punished."

Mbe was beaten and driven from the village. His compound was burned. His family was scattered. And from that day forward, the Tortoise was never again trusted with the secrets of the community.

The Lesson: False grief is an abomination. Do not weep with your mouth while your hands are busy stealing. The ancestors see all, and they will not be mocked.

18. Mbe and the Talking Frog

Mbe the Tortoise was walking by a swamp one day when he heard a voice say: "The world is a strange place."

Mbe looked around. He saw no one. He looked under a leaf. He looked behind a rock. And then he saw a Frog, sitting quietly at the edge of the water.

"Was that you?" Mbe asked.

"Yes," said the Frog. "I was just thinking."

Mbe was astounded. A frog that could talk? A frog that could think? This was a wonder. A miracle. And Mbe, being Mbe, immediately saw an opportunity.

"You must come with me to the King," Mbe said. "He will reward me richly for bringing him a talking frog."

The Frog shook his head. "I do not wish to go to the King. I am happy here in my swamp."

But Mbe would not listen. He picked up the Frog and carried him to the palace. The King was amazed. He had never seen a talking frog. He gave Mbe a bag of cowries and a plot of land.

But the Frog was not happy. He sat in the King's courtyard and said nothing. The King asked him questions. The Frog did not answer. The King commanded him to speak. The Frog did not answer. The King grew angry.

"This frog does not talk," the King said. "The Tortoise has deceived me."

Mbe was summoned. He tried to make the Frog speak. He poked him. He pinched him. But the Frog remained silent.

"You have brought me a dumb frog and claimed it was a miracle," the King said. "You have wasted my time and stolen my reward. For this, you will be punished."

Mbe was beaten and thrown out of the palace. He limped home, carrying the Frog, who had still not spoken a word.

When they reached the swamp, the Frog finally spoke. "You see," he said, "the world is a strange place. A frog who talks is a miracle. A tortoise who steals a miracle is a fool."

And the Frog jumped back into the water and was never seen again.

The Lesson: Do not try to profit from the gifts of others. A miracle shared freely is a blessing. A miracle stolen is a curse.

19. The Gift of Fire

In the beginning, the people did not have fire. They ate their food raw. They shivered in the cold at night. They feared the darkness, for the darkness was full of things they could not see.

The people prayed to Chukwu for fire. But fire belonged to the spirit world, and the spirits did not wish to share it. They kept it in their own hearths, and they guarded it carefully, for they knew that fire, once given, cannot be taken back.

A woman named Ada heard the people's prayers. She was not a chief. She was not a dibia. She was a simple woman, but she was brave. She said: "I will go to the spirit world. I will bring fire back to the people."

Ada crossed the great river. She walked through the forest of shadows. She reached the village of the spirits. And there, in the center of the village, she saw the fire burning.

She approached the fire and begged the spirits to give her a coal. But the spirits refused. They said: "Fire is not for humans. Go back to your world."

But Ada did not give up. She waited until the spirits slept. Then she crept to the fire, and she hid a coal inside a hollow reed. She carried the reed across the river. She returned to the earth.

When she reached her village, she blew on the coal and lit a small fire. The people gathered around and warmed their hands. They cooked their food. They lit their homes at night.

The spirits were angry at first. They came to the village and demanded the fire back. But the elders of the spirit world saw how the people prospered with fire. They said: "Let her keep it. Fire is a gift of the spirit world, and now it belongs to humans too."

And so fire came to humanity, brought by a woman who was brave enough to cross the river between the worlds.

The Lesson: The greatest gifts come from those who are willing to risk everything for others. Fire warms us because a woman dared to steal it from the spirits.

20. The Tortoise and the Pepper Soup

There was a great feast in the animal kingdom. All the animals were invited. The main dish was pepper soup, prepared with the hottest peppers in the forest. The aroma filled the air, and every animal's mouth watered.

But there was a rule: no animal could eat the pepper soup without first washing his hands. The King had decreed it, for the pepper was so hot that it could burn the eyes and the mouth of anyone who touched it carelessly.

The animals lined up at the stream to wash their hands. The Monkey washed. The Antelope washed. The Goat washed. But Mbe the Tortoise, who was always looking for a shortcut, did not wash.

He went to the pot of pepper soup and began to eat. The soup was hot. It burned his mouth. It burned his throat. Tears streamed down his face. But Mbe did not stop. He ate and he ate, for he was greedy and he did not want to lose his share.

The other animals saw what he was doing. They saw the tears on his face. They saw the sweat on his brow. And they knew he had not washed.

"Mbe has broken the rule!" the Monkey cried. "He has eaten without washing!"

The animals dragged Mbe before the King. The King looked at Mbe's red, tear-stained face. He looked at his greasy hands. And he pronounced judgment.

"You have broken the law," the King said. "You have eaten without washing, and you have stolen the share of those who obeyed. For this, you will be punished."

Mbe was beaten. He was driven from the feast. And from that day forward, the Tortoise was never again invited to eat with the other animals.

The Lesson: Rules are made for a reason. Do not break them for your own gain, for the consequences will fall upon you.

21. Ile-Mgba, the Sacred Grove

Ile-Mgba was a sacred grove at the edge of the village. It was not large. It was not beautiful. It was a thicket of ancient trees, wrapped in shadow, silent except for the whisper of leaves. But it was the home of the ancestors. The people did not cut its trees. They did not hunt its animals. They did not enter it without permission. The elders said: "Ile-Mgba is not a forest. It is a shrine. The ancestors dwell there, and they do not forget."

A young man named Nwosu did not believe in the old ways. He had been to the city. He had learned the white man's books. He laughed at the elders. He said: "A grove is a grove. Trees are trees. I will cut them. I will sell the wood. I will build a house."

One day, Nwosu entered Ile-Mgba with an axe. He raised it against a great iroko tree. But when the blade struck the trunk, the tree bled. Red sap poured from the wound, and Nwosu saw it and dropped his axe.

The grove fell silent. The birds stopped singing. The leaves stopped rustling. A voice spoke from the earth—deep and slow, like the sound of a drum: "You have entered what is not yours. You have harmed what is sacred. Go home and wait."

Nwosu ran home. That night, his house caught fire. His barn was struck by lightning. His children fell sick. His wife left him. Everything he touched turned to dust.

He went to the dibia. The dibia cast the stones. He read the patterns. He said: "You have offended Ile-Mgba. The ancestors are angry. You must make a sacrifice. You must beg for forgiveness."

Nwosu did as he was told. He offered a white ram and a white cock. He poured palm wine at the edge of the grove. He knelt on the earth and confessed. He said: "I was foolish. I did not understand. Please forgive me."

Slowly, his fortune returned. His house was rebuilt. His children recovered. His wife came home. And from that day forward, Nwosu never again entered Ile-Mgba. He told his children the story. He told them: "Some places are not for the living. Some places belong to the dead."

The Lesson: Sacred places are not empty. The ancestors dwell in them, and they do not forget. Honor the groves, or the groves will take from you.

22. An Orphan's Udala

There was once an orphan girl named Nneka who lived with her wicked stepmother. The stepmother gave her no food. She gave her no cloth. She gave her no rest. The girl worked from dawn to dusk, fetching water, sweeping the compound, tending the goats. At night she slept on the floor, while the stepmother's own children slept on soft mats.

One day, Nneka sat beneath an udala tree and wept. She had been beaten for dropping a calabash. She had not eaten in two days. She was tired. She was hungry. She was alone.

The tree spoke to her. Its voice was soft, like the rustle of leaves in a gentle wind. "Why do you weep, child?"

"I have no mother," Nneka said. "I have no father. I have no one. My stepmother hates me. My stepbrothers mock me. I am nothing."

The udala tree said: "You have me. Come to me every morning, and I will give you food. Come to me every evening, and I will give you rest. I will be your mother, and I will be your father."

And so Nneka came to the tree every day. The tree gave her fruit and honey and shade. She grew strong again. She grew beautiful again. She laughed again.

The stepmother discovered this. She was jealous. She came to the tree herself, demanding gifts. But the tree gave her nothing. It said: "You have no sorrow. You have no hunger. You are not an orphan. Go away."

The stepmother grew angry. She tried to cut down the tree. But the axe bounced back and struck her on the leg. She fell to the ground and was never able to walk again.

And Nneka lived happily beneath the udala tree. She grew up. She married a kind man. She had many children. And she told them the story of the tree that had loved her when no one else would.

The Lesson: The spirits protect the orphan, and the wicked harm only themselves. When no one else loves you, the earth itself will be your mother.

23. Why a Hawk Kills Chickens

In the olden days, there was a very fine young hen who lived with her parents in the bush. She was beautiful, with feathers that shone like copper and a voice that was sweet as the morning. All the birds admired her.

One day, a Hawk was hovering round, about eleven o'clock in the morning, as was his custom, making large circles in the air and scarcely moving his wings. His keen eyes were wide open, and he saw the pretty Hen picking up some corn near her father's house.

The Hawk was struck by her beauty. He closed his wings slightly and swooped down. "I must marry you," he said. "You are the most beautiful creature I have ever seen."

The Hen was flattered. But she was also cautious. "You are a hawk," she said. "You eat chickens. How can I marry you?"

"I will not eat you," the Hawk said. "I will protect you. I will provide for you. I will love you until the end of my days."

The Hen agreed. They were married. And for a time, they were happy. The Hawk brought her food. He built her a nest. He sang to her in the evenings.

But the Hawk's father had not given his blessing to the marriage. And when the Hawk's father died, the Hawk was told that he must pay a dowry to the Hen's family. He had no cattle. He had no yams. He had nothing to give.

He went to the Hen's father, the Cock, and pleaded: "I have nothing. But I love your daughter. Please, let me pay the dowry in another way."

The Cock, who was proud and greedy, said: "You will pay with your children. Every child that is born to you and my daughter will be mine. You will bring them to me as payment."

The Hawk was horrified. But he had no choice. He agreed.

And so, from that day forward, whenever a Hawk sees a chicken, he swoops down and carries it off—in part-payment of his dowry. He takes the children of the Hen, for they are the price he must pay for his love.

The Lesson: A promise made under desperation is a trap. And a debt unpaid will haunt the generations to come.

24. Obaledo

Obaledo was a beautiful girl. Her skin was the colour of fresh palm oil, and her smile was like the sun breaking through clouds. She was the pride of her village, and everyone said she would marry a great man.

One day, Obaledo's parents had to travel to a distant village for a funeral. Before they left, they called her to them and said: "Obaledo, we are leaving you alone. Do not leave the house. Do not go into the forest. And when you are hungry, eat only the yam and the snail we have prepared for you. Do not eat them together. Roast the yam first, and then the snail."

Obaledo nodded. "I will obey," she said.

But Obaledo was not obedient. No sooner had her parents left than she began to feel hungry. She took the yam and put it on the fire. Then she took the snail and put it on the fire before the yam was cooked. The snail released its water, which put out the fire.

Obaledo was angry. She needed fire. She looked out of the window. She saw a light in the forest—a small fire burning in the distance. She knew she should not leave the house. She knew her parents had warned her. But her hunger was stronger than her obedience.

She left the house and walked into the forest. She followed the light until she came to a clearing. There, sitting by a fire, was a spirit—a creature with no nose, with eyes that glowed like embers. It was a demon, one of the spirits that roamed the villages in those days.

The demon looked at Obaledo and smiled. "Welcome, pretty one," it said. "I have been waiting for you."

Obaledo screamed. She tried to run, but the demon was faster. It caught her. It carried her into the forest, deep into the land of the spirits, where no human could follow.

Her parents returned the next day and found the house empty. They searched the forest. They called her name. But Obaledo was gone. She was never seen again.

And that is why, to this day, the elders tell children: "Obey your parents. Do not leave the house when you are told to stay. For the spirits are always watching, and they are always hungry."

The Lesson: Disobedience leads to destruction. The rules of your parents are not chains—they are shields.

25. The Calabash Child

There was once a King who had no children. He had wealth and power. He had lands and servants. But he had no heir. His wife, the Queen, wept every night, for a woman without a child is like a tree without fruit.

One day, the Queen went to her farm to work. As she weeded the ground around her calabashes, she saw a little girl sitting among the gourds. The child was beautiful, with skin like the inside of a calabash and eyes like dark seeds.

"Who are you?" the Queen asked.

"I am your daughter," the child said.

The Queen was astonished. She took the child home and presented her to the King. The King rejoiced. He named the child Adaeze, which means "the King's daughter." And he loved her more than anything in the world.

But the King had other wives—wives who had borne him no children and were jealous of the Queen. They plotted against the child. They told the King: "That girl is not your daughter. She is a spirit. She will bring misfortune to your house."

The King did not believe them. He loved Adaeze too much.

One day, the King's other wives took Adaeze into the forest and abandoned her. They told the King that she had wandered off and been eaten by wild animals. The King wept and tore his clothes. He mourned for his daughter.

But Adaeze did not die. She was found by an old woman who lived in the forest. The old woman raised her as her own. Adaeze grew strong and beautiful. She learned the secrets of the forest. She learned the songs of the spirits.

Years later, a hunter from the King's village passed through the forest. He saw Adaeze and recognized her. He returned to the King and told him: "Your daughter is alive. I have seen her."

The King sent for her. Adaeze returned to the palace. She was no longer a child, but a woman. She stood before the King and his wives. The King's other wives trembled, for they knew they had been caught.

Adaeze spoke: "I am not a spirit. I am your daughter. And these women—these women who abandoned me in the forest—they are the ones who bring misfortune to your house."

The King banished his other wives. He welcomed Adaeze back to the palace. And from that day forward, the Calabash Child ruled by her father's side, bringing wisdom and peace to the kingdom.

The Lesson: Jealousy destroys the jealous. The child you try to harm may be the one who saves you.

26. Mbe's Help

The Leopard was trapped. A heavy log had fallen on his leg, and he could not free himself. He called out for help, but the other animals were afraid of him. The Elephant passed by and said, "You would eat me if I came close." The Antelope passed by and said, "You would kill me." The Monkey passed by and said, "You would chase me up the tree." The Goat passed by and said, "You would devour my children."

Finally, Mbe the Tortoise came by. He saw the Leopard in pain. He did not run. He said, "I will help you."

The Leopard laughed. "You? You are the smallest and slowest of the animals. How can you help me? The Elephant could not move this log. The Antelope could not move this log. What can you do?"

Mbe said nothing. He crawled under the log. He planted his feet firmly on the ground. He pushed with his strong shell. He pushed and pushed. Slowly, the log began to move. The Leopard pulled his leg free. He stood up, trembling, and shook himself.

The Leopard was ashamed. He had laughed at Mbe. He had doubted him. He said: "You have helped me when no one else would. You have done what the strong could not do. From this day forward, I will not harm you. And if any animal tries to harm you, I will protect you."

And so Mbe the Tortoise gained the friendship of the Leopard. He was safe in the forest forever. The other animals were amazed. They said: "Mbe is small, but he is not weak. Mbe is slow, but he is not useless."

And from that day forward, the animals learned that help can come from the most unexpected places, and that kindness is repaid in ways we cannot foresee.

The Lesson: Even the smallest can save the greatest. Do not despise help because it comes from a humble source. Kindness is repaid in unexpected ways.

27. How Death Came to Be

In the beginning, there was no death. Men lived forever. They grew old, but they did not die. They became tired, but they did not leave the world.

But the world was not happy. The old men and the old women filled the villages. There was no room for the young. There was no room for new life. The world was crowded and restless.

The people went to Chukwu and said: "We are tired of living. We want to leave this world. We want to rest."

Chukwu heard their cry. He sent a messenger to the world—a dog—with a message: "When a man dies, he will be restored to life. He will live again in the body of his son."

The dog carried the message. He ran through the villages, announcing: "Death will not be permanent. The dead will return."

But along the way, the dog met a sheep. The sheep asked: "Where are you going in such a hurry?"

And the dog, eager to deliver his message, said: "I am carrying word from Chukwu. When a man dies, he will live again."

But the sheep did not hear him correctly. The sheep heard: "When a man dies, he will not live again." And the sheep ran back to the villages and announced: "Death is permanent! The dead will not return!"

And so, when the first man died, the people waited for him to return. But he did not return. The dog came and saw them waiting. He asked: "Why are you waiting? I told you the dead would return."

But the people said: "No. The sheep told us that death is permanent."

The dog was furious. He chased the sheep, and from that day forward, dogs have chased sheep. But the damage was done. Death had entered the world. And it has remained with us ever since.

The Lesson: A message must be carried carefully. A word misheard can change the world forever. Listen with both ears, and speak with one tongue.

28. Why the Crocodile Lives Alone

The Crocodile was once a social creature. He lived with his family—his wife and his children—in a great house by the river. He had many friends. He was invited to every feast and every gathering. He was respected and loved.

But the Crocodile had a secret. He was greedy. And his greed was not for food or for wealth. His greed was for meat—the meat of his own kind.

One night, when his wife was asleep, the Crocodile crept out of the house and killed his youngest child. He ate the child and buried the bones in the riverbank. In the morning, he wept and said: "A wild beast has taken my child!"

The village mourned with him. They brought him gifts of food and cloth. They said: "The Crocodile is a good father. His grief is our grief."

But the Crocodile did not stop. He killed his second child. He killed his third. Each time, he wept and blamed a wild beast. Each time, the village mourned with him.

Finally, the Crocodile's wife grew suspicious. She watched him at night. She saw him creep out of the house. She saw him go to the riverbank. She saw him dig up the bones of their children and eat them.

She screamed. She ran to the village and told the elders: "The Crocodile is a murderer! He has eaten his own children!"

The village gathered. They found the bones. They found the evidence of the Crocodile's crime. They were horrified. They drove the Crocodile from the village. They said: "You are not one of us. You are a beast. You will live alone."

And so the Crocodile was banished. He went to the river and made his home in the water. He hid in the reeds and the mud. He never again lived with his family. He never again had friends. He lived alone, as he lives to this day.

The Lesson: Greed destroys the greedy. A man who devours his own family will be devoured by his own loneliness.
