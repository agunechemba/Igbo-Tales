Stories of the Women Who Ruled

1. STORY ONE: The Omu, Mother of Society

In the Igbo communities west of the Niger, in the area called Anioma, there was an institution older than memory: the Omu. She was not a queen consort. She was not the wife of a king. She was the "mother of society," a woman chosen by the oracle, installed with the Obi title, and given authority over the marketplace and the spiritual welfare of the town .

The Omu dressed like a king. She had her own palace. She sat with the male elders and chiefs in council, for her installation qualified her to take the Obi title and belong to their rank . But her power was not the same as theirs. Her power was over the women. Her power was over the market. Her power was over the rituals that kept the community in balance with the spirits.

The Omu Nwagboka of Onitsha was a signatory to an 1884 treaty with the British—a woman whose signature bound her people to an agreement with a foreign empire . She was not a figurehead. She was a ruler.

The Lesson: Women's power in Igbo society was not borrowed from men. It was a separate domain, with its own authority, its own rituals, and its own voice.

2. STORY TWO: The Omu Who Opened the Market

In Asaba, the market could not open until the Omu arrived. She would come to the marketplace with her calabash. She would take her seat. And only then, after she had declared the market open, could trading begin. If any woman sat down before her, the Omu could seize that woman's calabash or fine her .

The Omu did not just control trade. She controlled the conduct of women. Husbands brought complaints about their wives to her. She settled marital disputes. She punished women for non-attendance at the market, for going to other markets in other towns. She and her cabinet made medicines to protect the town from disease. If sickness was in a nearby town, they would place charms at the entrance to their own town to keep it out .

When there was drought and sickness and people were dying, the Omu and her cabinet would walk naked in the night with live brands to curse whoever had brought the disease. These were not empty rituals. They were acts of spiritual warfare, performed by women, for the protection of the community .

The Lesson: The Omu's power was economic, judicial, and spiritual. She was the guardian of the market, the regulator of women's conduct, and the protector of the town's health.

3. STORY THREE: The Umuada, Daughters of the Lineage

In every Igbo village, there was another source of women's power: the Umuada. They were the daughters of the lineage—women who had been born into the family, whether they still lived there or had married into other villages. Their authority was unique. They were not wives. They were not outsiders. They were blood .

The Umuada had the power to intervene in the affairs of their natal village. If a man mistreated his wife, the Umuada could summon him. If there was a dispute between families, the Umuada could mediate. If the male elders made a decision that the women opposed, the Umuada could organize collective action to resist it .

The senior Ada, the oldest daughter of the lineage, represented the women of her natal village in the Omu's palace. The Omu and the Umuada discussed matters of public importance. They discussed affairs concerning the women in the town. They worked together, a dual authority of women, one elected and one inherited .

The Lesson: The Umuada were the conscience of the lineage. Their power came not from marriage or wealth, but from blood. They were the daughters who never left, even when they left.

4. STORY FOUR: The Isi Ada, the Oldest Daughter

In many Igbo communities, the Isi Ada—the oldest daughter of a lineage—held a place of honor and authority. She was not just the firstborn female. She was a political and judicial figure. Her reports to the women of her group could lead to collective action opposing the decisions of the male political leaders .

The Isi Ada was the voice of the daughters. When the men made decisions that affected the women, the Isi Ada was the one who spoke. When the women needed representation, the Isi Ada was the one who stood before the elders. She was a bridge between the male and female spheres, a woman with a seat at the table because of her birth, not her marriage.

The Lesson: In Igbo society, authority was not only male. The oldest daughter held a power that was hers by right of birth.

5. STORY FIVE: The Women Who Fought Alongside Men

The oral histories of the Igbo are filled with stories of women who went to war. There was a queen who defied the odds to search for her husband in battle, entering enemy territory to rescue him. There was a mother who composed a famous battle song to protect her only son from going to war .

These were not fairy tales. They were heroic legends, passed down through generations, telling of women who held the fates of their husbands and children in their hands. They were activists, supernatural supporters, strategists. They were not passive observers of war. They were participants.

The Lesson: The Igbo woman was not confined to the compound. When the community was threatened, she could take up the fight alongside the men.

6. STORY SIX: The Ahebi Ugbabe, the Female King

Ahebi Ugbabe was born around 1880 in Enugu-Ezike. She was exiled from her community as a young woman. She became a prostitute. She traveled widely. She learned to speak many languages. She became a close companion of the Igala kings and the British officers .

And then she returned home. And she became a man.

She was appointed a warrant chief. And then she became a king—the Eze. She took the title of King Ahebi. She married many wives, as a man would. She sat in judgment over cases. She ruled her community with an iron will .

The British supported her claim because she was useful to them. The Igala kings supported her because she was their companion. But her own people, the men of her community, resented her. They said she was "governing too much." She faced constant opposition and conflict. But she never bowed .

The Lesson: A woman could become a king in colonial Nigeria. The sex and gender did not coincide in northern Igboland. Women could occupy positions that in other societies were exclusively male .

7. STORY SEVEN: The Women's War of 1929

In 1929, the British colonial administration began to count women, children, and livestock—a prelude to taxing women, a thing unheard of in Igbo custom. The women of Oloko knew what this meant. It was not just about money. It was about their independence, their dignity, and their place in the world.

The protest began in Oloko and spread like wildfire. Tens of thousands of women rose up. They danced, they sang, and they marched. They demanded the removal of the warrant chiefs. They destroyed the Native Courts and the European factories that symbolized their oppression .

The British fired on the women. In Aba, in Abak, in Utu Etim, women were killed. The police fired, causing eighteen casualties, all of them women . But the movement did not stop.

The Women's War, known in Igbo as Ogu Umunwanyi, forced the British to abandon the census and the taxation of women. It led to the end of the warrant chief position in British colonial Africa . The women had won.

The Lesson: When women unite, they possess a power that no empire can suppress. The Women's War was the first time that women in Nigeria organized on such a massive scale to confront colonial power.

8. STORY EIGHT: Madam Nwanyerewa, the Voice of the Revolt

Among the women who led the Women's War was Madam Nwanyerewa of Oloko, an ancient town in what is now Abia State. She played an important role not only in triggering the revolt but also in gaining recognition for her articulate testimony and intelligence .

She was not a queen. She was not a chief. She was a woman who spoke the truth and refused to be silenced. Her words, her courage, and her intelligence made her a leader. The colonial governor-general of Nigeria, Lord Frederick Lugard, was forced to observe of the Igbo woman: "She is ambitious, self-reliant, hard-working, and independent... she claims full equality with the opposite sex and would seem indeed to be the dominant partner" .

The Lesson: Leadership does not always come from titles. Sometimes it comes from the courage to speak.

9. STORY NINE: The Women Who Held the Economy

Before 1900, Igbo women were the backbone of the economy. They were active in agricultural cultivation and food processing. They manufactured and marketed palm oil and cassava. They produced salt, pottery, cloth, and mats. They dominated trade and commerce in the Igbo region .

Sylvia Leith-Ross, a British observer, was so impressed with the role women played in the Igbo economy that she concluded: "their women, because of their economic importance both as mothers, farm cultivators and traders, have rather more power than is generally thought" .

The market was a woman's domain. The Omu controlled it. The women traded in it. The wealth of the community flowed through their hands. And because they held the economy, they held power.

The Lesson: Economic power is political power. The Igbo woman's control of the market gave her a voice that could not be ignored.

10. STORY TEN: The Otu Omu, the Women's Cabinet

The Omu did not rule alone. She had a cabinet—the Otu Omu—a council of women who advised her, supported her, and helped her govern. Together, they decided on matters concerning theft, adultery, and other violations of traditional laws and customs in the market. They settled complicated marital conflicts. They maintained the traditional religion through public enlightenment campaigns .

The Otu Omu were the spiritual and political heart of the women's constituency. They performed rituals. They made medicines. They acted as spies for the town, especially in the area of security. They called the Umuada and the Inyemedi to order if there was any problem between them .

The Lesson: Women's power was not solitary. It was collective. The Omu was the head, but the Otu Omu was the body.

11. STORY ELEVEN: The Dual-Sex System

The Igbo society was not a patriarchy in the simple sense. It was a dual-sex system. There was a male sphere and a female sphere, each with its own authority, its own institutions, and its own leaders .

The male sphere had the Obi, the Eze, the Umunna. The female sphere had the Omu, the Umuada, the Otu Omu. Each was sovereign in its own domain. The men did not rule over the women. They ruled alongside them.

The British did not understand this. They saw only the male chiefs. They appointed warrant chiefs—all men. They ignored the Omu. They ignored the Umuada. They ignored the women's institutions. And in doing so, they disrupted the balance .

The Lesson: Colonialism did not just take land and resources. It took women's power. The dual-sex system was dismantled, and women were pushed out of the political sphere they had once occupied.

12. STORY TWELVE: The Mkpuke and the Obi

Ife Amadiume, a scholar of Igbo society, described the structure of the Igbo world as a balance between the Mkpuke and the Obi. The Obi is the male-focused house. The Mkpuke is the female-focused, matricentric unit .

These two units are not enemies. They are partners. The Mkpuke brings compassion, love, peace, and the spirit of common motherhood. The Obi brings competitiveness, valour, force, and the spirit of the warrior. Each is necessary. Each balances the other .

The Igbo world is not a world of male dominance. It is a world of balance. The feminine principle is not subordinate. It is equal. It is essential.

The Lesson: The Igbo cosmos is centered around women. The earth goddess Ala is the moral authority. The Omu is the mother of society. The Umuada are the conscience of the lineage. The feminine is not secondary. It is foundational .

13. STORY THIRTEEN: The Women Who Survived the War

When the Nigerian Civil War came, and Biafra was blockaded and bombed, it was the women who held the communities together. They took on the role of primary caregivers. They ensured that children and older people received what little food and medical care were available. They organized local food-sharing systems. They secretly preserved crops. They improvised nutritional supplements to save children suffering from malnutrition .

The writer Flora Nwapa documented their experiences in stories like "Wives at War" and "Daddy Don't Strike the Match." She wrote of the risks women faced in the "attack trade"—the dangerous journeys to find food—and the persistent efforts to ensure families' survival amid dangerous conditions. Women managed the evacuation of civilians whenever Nigerian forces threatened to take over a town .

Far from being passive victims, Igbo women stepped into roles of survival, care, and leadership. They ensured the continuity of their families and communities in the face of destruction.

The Lesson: The power of Igbo women is not just in the past. It is in the present. When the world falls apart, it is the women who hold it together.

These are the stories of the women who ruled. The Omu who opened the market. The Umuada who spoke for the lineage. The Ahebi who became king. The Nwanyerewa who led a revolt. The women who traded and farmed and fought and survived. Their power was not given to them. It was theirs by right. And as long as the Igbo people remember, it will never be forgotten.
