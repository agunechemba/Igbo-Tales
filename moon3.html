Moon 3: Ancient Igbo Communities

1. The Priest-King of Nri

Long before the British came, before the wars and the treaties, there was a kingdom that did not fight with swords. It fought with the ofo—the staff of ritual authority. It was called Nri, and it was the oldest kingdom in what would one day be called Nigeria.

The people of Nri did not conquer with armies. They conquered with purity. The Eze Nri, the priest-king, was not a warrior. He was a ritual specialist, a man so sacred that he could not be looked upon by ordinary eyes. His face was never seen in public. When he traveled, he was hidden behind cloth. When he spoke, his words were law.

According to the migration history, the founders of Nri left Aguleri on the River Anambra and settled in a place they called Nri, which comprised three villages: Agukwu, Diodo, and Akamkpisi. But their influence did not stay in those three villages. Nri men traveled to the farthest reaches of Igbo land, carrying their otonsi—the spear of peace and diplomatic immunity—in their left hand, and their ofo—the staff of ritual and political authority—in their right hand.

They were considered sacred people. They could not be molested or killed. They were the symbols of inter-village peace. They carried the ritual power of cleansing abomination, of making and dissolving taboos, of arbitrating between warring villages, of consecrating chiefs and title holders.

The power of Nri was the power of the spirit, not the sword. And for centuries, it held Igbo land together.

The Lesson: True authority is not taken by force. It is granted by the people, and it is maintained by wisdom, purity, and the trust of the ancestors.

2. The Bronze Casters of Igbo-Ukwu

In the 9th century, long before the kingdoms of Europe had mastered the art of bronze casting, there was a community in the heart of Igbo land that had perfected it. It was called Igbo-Ukwu, and its bronze works were the finest in all of West Africa.

In 1939, a man named Isaiah Anozie was digging a cistern in his compound. His hoe struck something hard. He pulled it out and found a bronze vessel, intricately decorated with insects and animals. He showed it to his neighbors. Some were afraid. They thought it was a thing of the spirits. But others were curious, and the news spread.

When archaeologists finally came, they uncovered a treasure trove: cast bronze sculptures, chieftaincy paraphernalia, glass pendants, and thousands of beads. The bronze objects were decorated with a style that had never been seen before—deep channelling, projecting bosses, and a geometric precision that spoke of an advanced civilization.

The Igbo-Ukwu findings dated to the 9th century CE. They were the earliest known bronzes in West Africa. They proved that the Igbo people were not a primitive people waiting to be discovered. They were the builders of an advanced civilization, participating in long-distance trade, connected to the wider world.

The Lesson: The Igbo past is not a void. It is filled with bronze and gold and the evidence of genius. The history of the Igbo people is a history of civilization.

3. The Founding of Arochukwu

In the tropical rain forest of what is now Abia State, there was a town that was not founded by one people, but by three. The Igbo, the Ibibio, and the Akpa came together to create a kingdom that would reshape the history of the entire region.

The first Igbo settlers were the Eze Agwu group, led by Agwu Inobia. They arrived in the land that the Ibibio had already settled. They were not welcomed. The Aro-Ibibio wars began, a long and bitter conflict that seemed to have no end.

But there was a man among the Igbo named Nnachi. He saw that the war could not be won by force alone. He went to the Ibibio king and spoke of peace. He offered a pact, a union of peoples. The king agreed, and the Kingdom of Arochukwu was born.

Arochukwu was not just a town. It was a state. It had a king, the Eze Aro. It had a council of chiefs. It had the Oracle of Ibini Ukpabi, the Long Juju, which drew pilgrims from across Igbo land and beyond. It had wealth from trade, from slaves, from the palm oil that flowed down the rivers to the coast.

The date of its foundation is calculated to be around 1690/1720. But the legacy of Arochukwu lasted far longer than its years. It was an empire built not on land, but on influence.

The Lesson: A kingdom can be built not just by conquest, but by diplomacy and the union of peoples.

4. The Long Juju of Arochukwu

The Oracle of Ibini Ukpabi was the most powerful oracle in all of Igbo land. It was called the Long Juju by the British, but to the Igbo, it was the voice of Chukwu himself. Pilgrims came from distant lands to seek its judgment. They descended into a gorge, where the oracle spoke. Those found guilty walked into dark tunnels and were never seen again. Those found innocent returned to their families.

But the priests of the oracle grew greedy. They discovered that they could sell the guilty into slavery instead of sacrificing them. The verdicts were falsified. Hundreds of people visited the oracle and never returned. Their communities believed the oracle had devoured them. The red water that flowed from the shrine was taken as proof that the condemned were dead.

The Long Juju became the center of the slave trade in Igbo land. The Aro people grew wealthy and powerful, controlling the oracle and the trade routes. But the blood of the innocent cried out from the tunnels. And eventually, the British came with guns and cannon, and the Long Juju was destroyed.

The Lesson: Even the most sacred oracle can be corrupted by greed. The voice of the gods can be twisted by human hands.

5. The King Who Crossed the River

In the 16th or early 17th century, there was a great upheaval in the Kingdom of Benin. A dispute arose between the Oba and a man named Chima. Some say Chima was an Igbo man who had been taken to Benin as a slave. Others say he was a prince of the royal house. But all agree that he was forced to flee.

Chima gathered his followers—men, women, and children—and left Benin. They moved through western Igbo land, founding towns along the way. These towns became known as Umu Ezechima, the children of Chima.

When they reached the River Niger, Chima died. His followers divided into two groups. One group, led by Oreze, crossed the river and founded a new state. They called it Onitsha. The other group, led by Esumai, went south and founded Aboh.

Onitsha grew into a great kingdom. It was an Igbo state in language and custom, but it retained elements of Benin influence, most evident in the institution of the Obi, or king. The Onitsha people remembered their origin. They told stories of their migration. They honored the memory of Chima.

The Lesson: A people can lose their home and find a new one. Migration is not a loss; it is a journey to a new destiny.

6. The Mixed Kingdom of Aboh

Aboh was a kingdom of two peoples. The Edo immigrants from Benin had conquered the earlier Igbo settlers. But the conquered were more numerous, and in time, they imposed their language on the conquerors. The Edo may have been stronger, but the Igbo tongue prevailed.

The Obi of Aboh was not a hereditary king. When an Obi died, anyone from the Umude section—the descendants of the Edo—could aspire to the throne. The Odua, the oldest man from the Umude section, consulted with the elders and announced the new king.

The Iyasere was the chief counsellor and commander-in-chief. He had to be of mixed parentage—one parent from the Umude, one from the Ndiche, the descendants of the non-Edo. This was the genius of Aboh. It was a kingdom that balanced the two peoples, that gave each a place, that forged a new identity from the union of two.

Aboh controlled the river trade. Its influence extended over the riverain clans and some upland clans. It was powerful enough to command the loyalty of Ogume, Ashaka, Amai, Ossissa, and many others.

The Lesson: Unity in diversity is not a weakness. It is a strength. A kingdom that embraces all its peoples is a kingdom that endures.

7. The Blacksmiths of Awka

Awka was a town of iron. The Awka blacksmiths were renowned throughout Igbo land, and even beyond its borders. They were the ones who forged the tools that cleared the farms, the weapons that defended the villages, the hoes that dug the yam mounds.

The Awka area was the site of the Nri civilization that produced the earliest documented bronze works in Sub-Saharan Africa around 800 AD. The Umueri blacksmith clan traced their origin to Awka. The received wisdom was that Igbo ironworking technology originated with Awka blacksmiths, at least as far back as the 9th century.

Before the British came, Awka was governed by titled men known as Ozo and Ndichie. The town was divided into seven Igbo groups sharing common blood lineage, divided into two sections: Ifite and Ezi. Half of the men of Awka were itinerant artisans and traders, traveling to distant lands, plying their trade, returning with wealth and stories.

The Lesson: A people who master a craft will never be poor. The blacksmiths of Awka proved that skill is the greatest wealth.

8. The Ancient People of Nsukka

Nsukka is one of the oldest settlements in Igbo land. Archaeology has shown that human activity in the area dates back to 3000 BCE, and pottery has been found that is dated to about 2500 BCE. The people of Nsukka are an ancient people, and they know it.

Nsukka Asadu Ideke Arumona was the original settlement. It was made up of four quarters: Nkpunano, Nru n'ato Ezke Ekwensi, Ihe, and Owere Dialoke Ndidiaba. Nine autonomous communities were created out of it, and in all, there are 117 villages.

The social organization of Nsukka was based on kinship. The nnaanyi was the father of the family. The okpara umunna was the eldest son of the kindred. The onyishi was the head of the village, and he possessed the arua, the symbol of authority with which he piloted the affairs of the village.

The ndishi n'akpruarua, the council of elders, and the nd oha, the council of titled men, governed the town. The nzemaa, the council of maskers, guarded the traditions. The umuda, the council of first daughters, spoke for the women. The nd igwe, the council of traditional rulers, stood at the top.

The Lesson: A community is governed not by one voice, but by many. The wisdom of the elders, the strength of the titled men, the spirit of the masquerades, and the voice of the daughters—all are needed to keep the village in balance.

9. The Heartland of the Igbo

The elders say that the heart of Igbo land is not in one place. It is in three places: Awka, Orlu, and Okigwe. These three towns form a belt, and the people in this area have no tradition of coming from anywhere else. They are the original Igbo.

Scholars have long debated this. Some say the Igbo migrated from the north, from the Benue valley. Some say they came from the west, from the Benin kingdom. But the people of Awka, Orlu, and Okigwe say they have always been here. The archaeology supports them. The Nsukka-Udi-Okigwe cuesta has witnessed human activity from very early times.

The Nri-Awka-Orlu complex was probably the earliest center of Igbo settlement in southern Nigeria. The Nri-Awka and Isuama (Orlu) area has been described as "the Igbo center or core". It was from this core that the Igbo people spread across the land, by waves of migration, carrying their language, their customs, and their spirit.

The Lesson: The Igbo people are not newcomers to their land. They are the original inhabitants, the children of the soil, the descendants of the first settlers.

10. The Sacred Land of Aguleri

Aguleri is a town on the River Anambra. It is a sacred town, for it is here that the Igbo people trace their origin to Eri, the first man, the one whom Chukwu lowered from the sky onto an anthill.

According to oral tradition, Eri's first son was named Agulu, and it was he who settled in the land that would become Aguleri. The town still celebrates a festival called Olili Obibia Eri, held in the seventh native month, to honor Eri, the father of the Igbo race.

Other Igbo communities come to Aguleri to offer sacrifices in the sacred temples. They come to request favors, to seek blessings, to connect with the source of their ancestry. The sacred place of Obu-gad is used for social and religious occasions. It is a place of pilgrimage, a place of origin, a place where the spirit of Eri still lives.

The Lesson: To know where you come from is to know who you are. Aguleri is the root of the Igbo tree.

11. The Cradle of the Igbo

There is a controversy among the Igbo people. Two towns claim to be the cradle of the Igbo: Aguleri and Nri. Aguleri says that Eri, the father of all Igbo, landed there. Nri says that the founders of Nri left Aguleri and founded the settlement of Nri, which became the center of Igbo civilization.

But some say the dispute is unnecessary. Umueri, a town near Aguleri, claims to be the ancestral home of all Igbos. A team of Igbo researchers confirmed Umueri as the ancestral home, saying that all Igbos can trace their ancestral home from the ancient town founded by the children of Eri. It was the oldest human settlement established by early Igbo founders, the earliest human settlement east of the Niger.

The controversy continues. But the elders say that the dispute is a sign of love, not division. The Igbo people love their history. They love their origins. They want to know where they come from.

The Lesson: The search for origins is a search for identity. The Igbo people are still searching, still remembering, still honoring the ancestors.

12. The Fishermen of Oguta

Oguta is a town on the east bank of Oguta Lake, in what is now Imo State. The people of Oguta trace their origins to the great Benin kingdom, during the reign of Oba Ewuare the Great. They migrated from Benin and settled in their present abode due to the exploits of three great brothers: Osu, and others.

In the beginning, Oguta was a fishing community. The people lived on the fish of the lake. But then the palm oil trade came. European and African merchants established stations at Oguta. The town was transformed from a predominantly fishing community to a relatively prosperous and cosmopolitan town.

Oguta was one of the first territories used by the British to advance into the Igbo hinterland. It was a place of encounter, of trade, of change. The people of Oguta adapted. They learned the ways of the merchants. They learned the ways of the British. But they did not forget their origins. They did not forget the great Benin kingdom from which their forefathers came.

The Lesson: Change is not a loss of identity. A people can adapt to new circumstances while still honoring their past.

13. The Legacy of Eri

The stories of the ancient Igbo communities all lead back to one man: Eri. Whether it is Nri, Aguleri, Umueri, or any of the other towns that claim descent from him, the memory of Eri is the memory of the beginning.

Eri was the first man. He was lowered from the sky by Chukwu. He lived in the age when the sky was close and the people did not have to work. He was the father of the Igbo race.

When Eri died, the sky moved away. The age of abundance ended. The people had to learn to farm, to hunt, to build, to trade. They had to learn to live in a world that was no longer easy. But they did not forget Eri. They did not forget the lessons he taught. They did not forget the covenant between the human world and the spirit world.

And so, the ancient communities of Igbo land are not just towns on a map. They are the living memory of Eri. They are the children of the first man. They are the keepers of the covenant. They are the heirs of the sky that once fed us.

The Lesson: The history of the Igbo people is not just a story of the past. It is a living tradition, passed down from generation to generation, from the time of Eri to the present day. The ancestors are not gone. They live in the towns they founded, in the customs they established, in the stories they told. And as long as the Igbo people remember, they will never be lost.

14. The Ancient Walls of Udi

In the land of Udi, in what is now Enugu State, there are walls. They are not the walls of a palace. They are not the walls of a shrine. They are the walls of a people—great earthworks that stretch across the land, built by hands that have long since turned to dust.

The elders say that the walls of Udi were built in the time of the ancestors. They were built to protect the villages from invaders. They were built to mark the boundaries of the clans. They were built to honor the gods. And they were built with the sweat and blood of the people who called this land their home.

Archaeologists have studied the walls of Udi. They have found that they were built over many centuries, by many generations. They have found that they were built with a sophistication that speaks of a well-organized society. They have found that they were built by a people who understood the land and knew how to shape it.

The walls of Udi are a testament to the genius of the Igbo people. They are a reminder that the ancient Igbo were not simple villagers. They were engineers. They were architects. They were builders of civilizations.

The Lesson: The greatness of a people is not measured by their monuments of stone, but by the works of their hands and the vision of their minds.

15. The Salt Makers of Uburu

In the land of Uburu, in what is now Ebonyi State, there was a people who made salt. They did not mine it from the earth. They did not extract it from the sea. They made it from the brine of the salt lakes, the iyi aja, which were sacred to the goddess of the waters.

The salt makers of Uburu were women. They were the keepers of the salt. They were the ones who boiled the brine in great clay pots. They were the ones who watched the water evaporate, leaving behind the white crystals that were the wealth of the land.

The salt of Uburu was famous throughout Igbo land. It was traded in the markets of the north and the south. It was used to preserve food. It was used in medicine. It was used in ritual. It was a gift of the goddess, and the women of Uburu were her priestesses.

There was once a woman named Oma who was the greatest salt maker in Uburu. She knew the secrets of the brine. She knew the songs that pleased the goddess. She knew the prayers that made the salt pure. And when she died, the women of Uburu mourned for a year, for they had lost their mother.

The Lesson: The wealth of a community is not always in gold or in land. Sometimes it is in the skill of its women and the gifts of the goddess.

16. The Kingdom of Nsukka and the Sacred Pottery

The people of Nsukka were among the first potters in Igbo land. They shaped the clay of the earth into vessels of beauty and utility. They made pots for cooking. They made pots for storing water. They made pots for ritual. They made pots for the dead.

The pottery of Nsukka was decorated with intricate patterns—lines, spirals, triangles, and dots. Each pattern had a meaning. Each pattern told a story. Each pattern connected the living to the ancestors.

The women of Nsukka were the potters. They were the ones who dug the clay. They were the ones who shaped the vessels. They were the ones who fired them in the open fires. They were the ones who decorated them with the symbols of the lineage.

There was once a woman named Ada who was the finest potter in Nsukka. Her pots were sought after throughout the land. Her patterns were copied by other potters. Her skill was legendary. And when she died, her pots were buried with her, so that she would have them in the spirit world.

The Lesson: The craft of the hands is a sacred thing. The potter is not just a maker of vessels. She is a keeper of tradition, a storyteller of the lineage, a priestess of the earth.

17. The Ancient City of Opi

In the Nsukka area, there is a town called Opi. It is one of the oldest settlements in Igbo land. The people of Opi trace their origins to the first settlers of the Nsukka region, the children of Eri who spread across the land.

Opi was a city of traders. It was located at the crossroads of the trade routes that connected the north and the south. Its market was famous throughout the land. Its people were known for their honesty and their hospitality.

The elders of Opi remember the old days. They remember the caravans that came from the north, carrying salt and horses. They remember the traders who came from the south, carrying cloth and beads. They remember the days when Opi was the center of the world.

And they remember the laws of the ancestors. The laws that said: welcome the stranger, for the stranger may be an ancestor in disguise. The laws that said: trade fairly, for the gods are watching. The laws that said: honor the ancestors, for they are the foundation of the city.

The Lesson: A city is not just a place. It is a community of people who share a history, a tradition, and a covenant with the ancestors.

18. The Warrior Communities of Abam

In the land of Abam, in what is now Abia State, there was a people who were known for their courage. They were warriors. They were hunters. They were the defenders of the Aro Confederacy, the mercenaries who fought for the Long Juju.

The men of Abam were trained from boyhood in the arts of war. They learned to use the machete. They learned to use the spear. They learned to use the bow and arrow. They learned to move silently through the forest. They learned to strike without warning.

The Abam warriors were feared throughout the land. Their name was a warning. Their approach was a death sentence. They were the leopard of the forest, the eagle of the sky, the python of the river. They were the Agu of the Aro.

But the Abam warriors were not just fighters. They were also farmers. They were also fathers. They were also keepers of the traditions. They fought for their families. They fought for their land. They fought for their gods.

The Lesson: Courage is not the absence of fear. It is the willingness to fight for what you love, even when the odds are against you.

19. The Town of Umuahia and the Oracle

Umuahia is a town in what is now Abia State. It was once a group of villages, bound together by kinship and by covenant. The people of Umuahia trace their origins to the migrations that shaped the Igbo world—some from the north, some from the south, some from the west.

Umuahia was known for its connection to the Oracle of Ibini Ukpabi. The Aro people, who controlled the oracle, had settlements in Umuahia. They used the town as a base for their trading and their slave raids. The people of Umuahia learned the ways of the Aro. They learned the arts of trade. They learned the arts of diplomacy.

But Umuahia was also a center of resistance. When the British came, the people of Umuahia fought. They fought with the weapons they had. They fought with the courage of their ancestors. And when they were defeated, they did not surrender their identity. They kept their language. They kept their customs. They kept their stories.

The Lesson: A people can be defeated in battle, but they cannot be defeated in spirit. As long as they keep their traditions, they will rise again.

20. The Kingdom of Onitsha and the Obi

Onitsha was founded by the followers of Chima, the prince who fled from Benin. They crossed the River Niger and settled on the eastern bank. They built a kingdom that was Igbo in language and custom, but retained the memory of their Benin origin.

The Obi was the king. He was not a warrior. He was a ritual specialist. He was the link between the people and the ancestors. He was the guardian of the covenant. He was the one who sat in judgment. He was the one who made the sacrifices. He was the one who spoke to the gods.

The Obi of Onitsha was bound by many taboos. He could not leave his palace. He could not see a corpse. He could not sit on the ground. He could not eat in the house of another. The taboos were not punishments. They were protections. They preserved his ritual purity. They kept him separate from the contamination of daily life.

The Obi was the heart of Onitsha. He was the center of the kingdom. He was the link between the living and the dead. And as long as the Obi lived, Onitsha lived.

The Lesson: A king is not just a ruler. He is a vessel of the gods, a keeper of the covenant, a bridge between the human world and the spirit world.

21. The Anioma Communities of the West

In the land west of the River Niger, there is a people who are Igbo, but who are also different. They are called the Anioma. They are the "children of the earth," the "good land," the people of the west.

The Anioma are divided into many communities: Ika, Ndokwa, Isoko, Aniocha, Oshimili. Each has its own customs, its own traditions, its own stories. But they share a common identity. They share a common language. They share a common history.

The Anioma were influenced by the Benin kingdom, by the Igala, by the Ijaw, and by the European traders who came to the coast. They learned to trade. They learned to adapt. They learned to survive.

But they never forgot their origins. They never forgot that they were Igbo. They never forgot the language of their ancestors. They never forgot the gods of their fathers.

The Lesson: Identity is not a fixed thing. It is a living thing. It is shaped by history, by geography, by the encounters of a people. But as long as the language is spoken and the traditions are kept, the identity remains.

22. The Spirit of the Oguta Lake

Oguta Lake is one of the most sacred lakes in Igbo land. It is a place of beauty. It is a place of mystery. It is a place of the spirits. The people of Oguta say that the lake is the home of the goddess, the mother of the waters, the one who watches over the town.

There is a story that the lake is connected to the River Niger by a tunnel. The waters of the lake flow into the river, and the waters of the river flow into the lake. The tunnel is the path of the spirits. It is the way between the worlds.

The people of Oguta honor the lake. They bring offerings to the goddess. They do not pollute the water. They do not fish in certain parts of the lake. They do not disturb the spirits who dwell within.

There was once a young man named Okeke who did not respect the lake. He fished in the forbidden waters. He threw his refuse into the lake. He laughed at the warnings of the elders. One day, as he swam, the water began to churn. The goddess rose from the depths. She seized him. He was never seen again.

The Lesson: The waters are sacred. The goddess is real. Honor the lake, and it will bless you. Defile it, and it will take you.

23. The Isuama Communities of Orlu

Orlu is a town in what is now Imo State. It is one of the oldest settlements in Igbo land. It is the heart of the Isuama, a subgroup of the Igbo people who have no tradition of migration. They say they have always been here.

The Isuama are the keepers of the old ways. They are the ones who preserved the customs, the rituals, the stories of the ancestors. They are the ones who remember the time before the British came. They are the ones who know the names of the gods.

The Isuama communities of Orlu are many: Orlu, Amaigbo, Umuowa, Nkwerre, and others. Each has its own traditions. Each has its own festivals. Each has its own sacred groves. But they share a common identity. They share a common ancestry. They share a common language.

The Isuama are the root of the Igbo tree. They are the original people. They are the children of the soil. And as long as they remember their traditions, the Igbo world will never be lost.

The Lesson: The old ways are not dead. They live in the hearts of the people who keep them. The Isuama are the keepers of the flame.

24. The Ancient Market of Nkwo

In the town of Umuduruoha, there was a market. It was called Nkwo. It was one of the oldest markets in Igbo land. It was the heart of the community. It was where the people gathered. It was where they traded. It was where they celebrated.

The market of Nkwo was held on the fourth day of the Igbo week. It was a day of rest. It was a day of gathering. It was a day of the ancestors. On that day, the people came from the surrounding villages. They brought their yams. They brought their palm oil. They brought their cloth. They brought their stories.

The market was not just a place of trade. It was a place of justice. It was a place of ritual. It was a place of community. The elders sat in judgment. The priests made sacrifices. The women traded. The children played. The ancestors watched.

And so, the market of Nkwo was not just a market. It was the center of the world. It was the place where the visible and the invisible met.

The Lesson: The market is the heart of the community. It is not just a place of trade. It is a place of gathering, of justice, of ritual, of community.

25. The Ancient City of Nnewi

Nnewi is a town in what is now Anambra State. It is one of the oldest settlements in Igbo land. The people of Nnewi trace their origins to Eri, the first man, the father of the Igbo race.

Nnewi was a kingdom. It had a king, the Obi. It had a council of chiefs, the Ndi Ichie. It had a council of elders, the Ndi Ozo. It had a council of women, the Umuada. It had a council of masquerades, the Nze na Ozo.

The people of Nnewi were known for their wisdom. They were known for their courage. They were known for their industry. They were farmers. They were traders. They were warriors. They were priests.

And they were keepers of the covenant. They remembered Eri. They honored the ancestors. They kept the laws of the land. And as long as they remembered, Nnewi endured.

The Lesson: A city is not just a place. It is a people. It is a tradition. It is a covenant with the ancestors. As long as the covenant is kept, the city will stand.

26. The Sacred Grove of the Aro

In Arochukwu, there was a grove. It was sacred. It was the home of the oracle. It was the place where the voice of Chukwu spoke. It was the place where the guilty were condemned and the innocent were set free.

The grove was guarded by the priests of Ibini Ukpabi. They were the keepers of the oracle. They were the ones who spoke for the god. They were the ones who decided the fate of those who came to consult. They were the ones who grew wealthy from the gifts of the pilgrims.

But the grove was also a place of darkness. The tunnels that led from the oracle were the tunnels of death. Those who entered never returned. Their bones lay in the darkness, unburied, unremembered. Their spirits wandered, unable to find rest.

The grove of Arochukwu was destroyed by the British in 1901. The oracle was silenced. The tunnels were sealed. The priests were hanged. And the grove, once sacred, became a place of silence, a place of memory, a place of shame.

The Lesson: A sacred place can become a place of darkness. When the guardians of the sacred become corrupt, the sacred itself is destroyed.

27. The Ancient Craft of the Nkwerre Gunsmiths

In the town of Nkwerre, in what is now Imo State, there was a people who were known for their skill in making guns. They were the gunsmiths of Igbo land. They made the guns that armed the warriors. They made the guns that defended the villages. They made the guns that won the wars.

The Nkwerre gunsmiths learned their craft from the Europeans, who came to the coast in the 17th and 18th centuries. But they did not simply copy the European guns. They improved them. They adapted them. They made them their own.

The Nkwerre gunsmiths were also traders. They traveled to the coast to buy the materials they needed—the iron, the gunpowder, the flint. They traveled to the interior to sell their guns to the communities that needed them. They became wealthy. They became powerful. They became respected.

And they remembered the covenant. They remembered the ancestors. They kept the laws. They honored the gods. And as long as they remembered, Nkwerre endured.

The Lesson: Skill is a gift. The Nkwerre gunsmiths used their skill to serve their people. They did not use it for greed. They used it for the community.

28. The Legacy of the Ancient Communities

The ancient communities of Igbo land are many. Nri. Aguleri. Arochukwu. Onitsha. Aboh. Awka. Nsukka. Udi. Opi. Abam. Umuahia. Orlu. Nnewi. Nkwerre. Oguta. Uburu. Each has its own story. Each has its own traditions. Each has its own covenant with the ancestors.

But they are not separate. They are connected. They are connected by the blood of Eri, the first man. They are connected by the language of the ancestors. They are connected by the gods they worship. They are connected by the covenant that binds the living to the dead.

The ancient communities are the foundation of the Igbo world. They are the roots of the tree. They are the source of the river. They are the beginning of the story.

And as long as the Igbo people remember, the ancient communities will never be forgotten. They will live in the stories we tell. They will live in the customs we keep. They will live in the names of the towns, the rivers, the markets, the shrines. They will live in the hearts of the children of the soil.

The ancestors are not gone. They are with us. They are watching. They are waiting. And as long as we remember, they will never be lost.

The Lesson: The history of the Igbo people is a living tradition. It is passed down from generation to generation, from the time of Eri to the present day. As long as the stories are told and the traditions are kept, the ancient communities will never be forgotten.
