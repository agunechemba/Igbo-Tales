THE SKY THAT FED US

Traditional Igbo Stories of Humans, Animals, and Spirits

CHAPTER ONE: The Voice Before the World

Before there was earth, before there was sky, before there was river or forest or the small red soil that stains the feet of farmers, there was Chukwu.

Chukwu was not a man. Chukwu was not a woman. Chukwu was the Great God, the One Who Created All Things, the source from which everything came and to which everything returns. The elders say that Chukwu existed alone, in a vast and silent darkness that had no beginning and no end. There was no sound. There was no light. There was only Chukwu, and Chukwu's thought.

And then Chukwu thought: Let there be something.

The thought became a word. The word became a breath. The breath became light, and the light spread through the darkness like palm oil poured on still water. And where the light touched, things began to exist.

Chukwu created the sky first. Not the sky as we know it now—far away, distant, untouchable—but a sky that lay close to the earth, like a blanket spread over a sleeping child. The sky was called Igwe, and Igwe was given the task of watching over the heavens, of holding the clouds and the stars and the moon in their proper places.

Then Chukwu created the earth. The earth was called Ala, and she was given the task of holding all things that live and grow. Ala was the ground beneath the feet, the soil that receives seeds, the womb that receives the dead. She was the goddess of fertility and morality, the one who would judge human actions and uphold the laws of the land. The elders say that Ala is so pervasive in Igbo consciousness that in traditional morning prayers, she is mentioned second only to Chukwu himself.

Chukwu created the waters—the rivers and the streams and the great ocean that surrounds the world. Chukwu created the forests and the trees and the animals that would dwell within them. Chukwu created the mmụọ, the spirits, who move between the visible and the invisible, carrying messages and watching over the balance of things. Chukwu created the alusi, the deities, who would serve as intermediaries between the human world and the divine.

And then Chukwu created light.

The light was called Anyanwu, the sun, and it was given the task of illuminating the day. The moon was called Mmanwu, and it was given the task of illuminating the night. In those early days, the Sun and the Moon were the closest of companions, living side by side in the sky, their light blending together in harmony. Wherever they went, joy followed. The earth below bloomed with plenty, the rivers sparkled with life, and all creation looked up with gratitude.

But the greatest creation was yet to come.

Chukwu took a handful of earth—the red earth of Ala—and shaped it into the form of a man. Chukwu breathed into the man's nostrils, and the man became a living being. His name was Eri, and he was the first of all humans, the father of the Igbo people. Eri was lowered down from the heavens onto an anthill in a land that was still waterlogged from the great flood that had covered the earth. The land was soft and wet, and Eri sat upon the anthill, not knowing what to do.

Then Chukwu spoke to Eri.

"You are the first," Chukwu said. "From you will come many. But you must learn to live with the sky, for the sky is my gift to you. As long as you honor it, it will feed you."

And so it was.

CHAPTER TWO: Eri and the Sky That Fed Us

In the days of Eri, the sky did not sit far above the earth as it does now. It rested just above the heads of men, close enough to touch, close enough to eat. When Eri was hungry, he reached up and broke off a piece of the sky. It tasted like whatever his heart desired—sometimes like roasted yam, sometimes like fresh fish, sometimes like the sweetness of palm wine that has not yet fermented.

Eri did not need to plant. He did not need to harvest. He did not need to cook. The sky provided everything. And the sky did not only feed Eri—it fed all the people who came after him, the children of Eri, the Umu-Eri, who spread across the land of the rising sun, the land that would one day be called Igbo land.

The elders say that during Eri's time, there was no sickness. There was no hunger. There was no war. The sky fed everyone, and no one took more than they needed. When a child was born, the sky provided. When an elder grew weak, the sky sustained them. When the people gathered to give thanks, they did not bring offerings of food, because food was always available. They brought offerings of gratitude.

Eri himself did not sleep as we sleep. He did not grow old as we grow old. The sky sustained him in a way that we can no longer understand. He walked among the spirits. He spoke with the animals, who in those days spoke the same language as men. He listened to the whispers of Chukwu, which came not from the sky alone but from the earth, from the trees, from the rivers, from the wind.

Eri had a wife, and together they bore children. The children bore children, and the world began to fill. The people did not build houses, for the sky was their roof. They did not dig wells, for the sky gave them drink. They did not hunt, for the animals were their companions. They did not fight, for there was nothing to fight over. Everything was shared. Everything was given.

But something changed.

The elders do not always agree on what it was. Some say that the people began to forget. They ate from the sky and did not give thanks. They took and took and did not remember the hand that gave. Others say that Eri himself grew proud. He began to think that the abundance was his own doing, that he had earned the sky's gifts, that he was the source of his own sustenance.

Still others say that it was not pride but death that ended the age of plenty. When Eri died—for even Eri had to die—the sky stopped feeding the people directly. Chukwu withdrew his hand. The sky rose, slowly at first, then faster, until it settled where it now sits, far above the reach of human hands.

And the people wept, because they had never known hunger before. They had never known work.

CHAPTER THREE: Why the Sky Moved Away

The elders say that the sky did not leave in anger. The sky left in sorrow.

For many seasons after Eri's death, the people continued to eat from the sky. They reached up and broke off pieces, as they had always done. But they had forgotten the lesson of gratitude. They took more than they needed. They broke off large pieces and threw away what they could not finish. They let the children trample on what had fallen to the ground.

The sky watched. The sky felt the waste. And the sky grew heavy with grief.

One day, a woman was pounding yam. She had taken a large piece of the sky and was preparing it for her family. But she was careless. She let pieces fall to the ground. She let the children play with what she had dropped. She did not stop them when they threw the sky's gift into the fire.

The sky saw this, and the sky spoke. Not in words that human ears could hear, but in a language that the earth itself understood. The sky said: If you cannot respect what is given, you will learn to work for what you eat.

And then the sky began to rise.

Slowly at first. The people reached up, and their fingers could no longer touch it. They stretched, and they stretched, and they cried out, but the sky kept moving. It rose above the trees. It rose above the hills. It rose until it was so far away that no human hand could ever reach it again.

And there the sky has remained, my child, far above us, where it watches but does not feed.

That is why we farm. That is why we cook. That is why we sweat under the sun, because the sun, Anyanwu, is the eye of the sky, and he sees everything we do. We work for our food now, and we must not waste it. The sky taught us that lesson.

But the sky did not abandon us entirely. When the rains fall, they come from the sky. When the sun shines, it warms the earth. When the moon rises, it lights the night. The sky is still there, still watching, still waiting. And every time we look up, we remember.

The elders say that this is not just a story about the sky. It is a story about everything. It is about the relationship between human beings and the cosmos. We are not separate from the sky, or from the earth, or from the spirits. We are part of one great whole. When we act with greed, we disturb that whole. When we act with respect, we keep it in balance.

CHAPTER FOUR: The Quarrel Between Earth and Sky

After the sky moved away, the people learned to farm. They learned to plant yam and cocoyam, to harvest and to store. They learned to build houses with mud walls and thatched roofs, to dig wells and to make fire. They learned to live without the direct nourishment of the sky.

But the sky was not always kind.

There was a time, long after the age of Eri, when Earth and Sky quarreled. The elders say that the quarrel began over something small—a matter of pride, a matter of words spoken in anger. But whatever the cause, the result was terrible. Sky withheld rain for seven years.

Seven years without rain.

The rivers dried up. The streams became dust. The crops withered in the fields, and the yams that had been planted with hope yielded nothing but shriveled roots. The animals grew thin and weak. The people grew thin and weak. And because the earth was too hard to dig, the dead could not be buried. The hoes broke on the stony ground, and the bodies of the departed lay unburied, their spirits unable to find rest.

The people cried out to Chukwu. They cried out to Ala. They cried out to the ancestors. But the sky did not answer.

Finally, the people sent Vulture to plead with Sky. Vulture was chosen because he could fly high, higher than any other bird, high enough to reach the dwelling place of the sky. Vulture was given a message of suffering, a song of sorrow, a plea for mercy.

Vulture flew and flew. He flew until the earth was a small brown circle below him. He flew until he reached the gates of the sky. And there he sang. He sang of the withered crops and the dying animals. He sang of the broken hoes and the unburied dead. He sang of the hunger and the thirst and the grief of the sons of men.

Sky was moved to pity. Sky's heart softened. And Sky gave Vulture rain wrapped in leaves of coco-yam.

"Take this to the earth," Sky said. "Let it fall gently, so that the soil may drink and the crops may grow."

Vulture took the rain in his talons and began the long journey back to earth. But as he flew, his long talon pierced the leaves, and the rain fell. It fell not gently, but in torrents. It fell as it had never fallen before, flooding the rivers and drowning the fields. And so heavily did it rain on Vulture that he could not return to deliver his message. He flew to a distant land, where he saw a fire, and he warmed himself and ate the entrails of a sacrifice.

And that is why, whenever sacrifices are being offered, vultures are to be seen hovering in the sky, often descending to eat the substance of the sacrifice. And that is why Nigeria has two seasons: a rainy season and a dry season, to describe the effect of seasonal changes on migratory birds like the vulture.

CHAPTER FIVE: The Sun and the Moon

In the beginning, the Sun and the Moon were the best of friends.

The Sun was called Anyanwu. He was fiery and bold, always eager to shine with strength. The Moon was called Mmanwu. She was calm and gentle, offering cool light to balance the Sun's heat. Together they made a perfect pair. The animals of the forest sang songs in their honor, and the people told stories of their friendship.

But the Sun was not content with friendship. The Sun wanted more. He wanted to be greater than the Moon. He wanted the praise and the glory for himself.

One day, the Sun invited the Moon to a grand feast in his fiery palace.

"Come, my friend," said the Sun. "Let us share a meal and strengthen our bond."

The Moon accepted with joy, for she trusted the Sun completely. She did not know that the Sun had secretly prepared a trick. He wanted the feast for himself, to enjoy the praise and the glory without sharing.

When the day of the feast arrived, the Sun welcomed the Moon with smiles and music. But when the food was served, the Sun used his burning heat to spoil the Moon's portion. The Moon could not eat, while the Sun feasted heartily on roasted yam, sweet palm wine, and tender meat. The Moon sat in silence, her heart heavy with betrayal.

At last she spoke.

"Friend Sun, why have you done this? Did we not promise to walk together in unity?"

But the Sun laughed loudly, his flames crackling with pride.

"I am the brighter one," he said. "The sky belongs to me. You are but a pale shadow beside my greatness."

The Moon's face darkened with sorrow. She left the feast and returned to her cool dwelling in the night. From that day forward, the friendship between Sun and Moon was broken. They no longer walked together in the sky. Instead, the Sun ruled the day, blazing with fiery pride, while the Moon ruled the night, shining gently in solitude.

Sometimes, in rare moments, they cross paths during an eclipse. When that happens, the people say it is the Sun and the Moon meeting again, quarreling over the past, and reminding the world of the danger of betrayal.

The people looked up and learned an important lesson: once trust is broken, harmony is lost. That is why even today, the Sun and the Moon never share the sky as they once did in the beginning.

CHAPTER SIX: The Tortoise and the Birds

After the time of Eri, the animals still spoke the same language as men. The Tortoise, whose name was Mbeku, was the most cunning of all the creatures. He was greedy and shrewd, always looking for a way to trick others and fill his own belly.

In those days, there was a great famine in the land. The crops had failed, and the animals had nothing to eat. But the birds of the sky had a secret. They had discovered a way to reach Skyland, the home of the sky spirits, where food was abundant. Every year, the birds flew up to Skyland for a great feast, and they returned fat and happy.

The Tortoise heard about this feast, and his mouth watered. He wanted nothing more than to go to Skyland and eat his fill. So he went to the birds and begged them to take him with them.

"Please," he said, "let me come with you. I am hungry, and I have no wings to fly."

The birds laughed at him. "How can a tortoise fly? You have no wings. You belong on the ground."

But the Tortoise was clever. He made a plan. He asked each bird to give him one feather. "With enough feathers," he said, "I can build myself wings and fly with you."

The birds were amused by this idea. They each gave the Tortoise a feather, and the Tortoise tied them together with string and stuck them to his shell. He flapped his new wings and, to the surprise of all the birds, he rose into the air.

But the Tortoise had another trick. He told the birds that, in Skyland, each of them should take a new name. He said that the sky spirits would not recognize them otherwise. The birds, being simple creatures, agreed. They each chose a new name. But the Tortoise chose a name that was a title of honor.

"I shall be called All of You," he said.

The birds did not understand what he was doing.

When they arrived in Skyland, the sky spirits welcomed them and laid out a great feast. The food was piled high—roasted yam, pounded cocoyam, palm wine, and honey. The Tortoise looked at the feast and smiled.

"And who is this feast for?" asked the chief of the sky spirits.

"For All of You," replied the Tortoise.

And so the sky spirits served the food to the Tortoise, because his name was All of You. The birds watched in anger as the Tortoise ate and ate, stuffing himself until his belly was round and tight. They got nothing.

The birds were furious. They waited until the Tortoise had finished eating, and then they took back their feathers. Each bird plucked the feather it had given him, and the Tortoise was left without wings. He could not fly. He could not return to earth.

The Tortoise begged the birds to help him. He asked them to carry him down. But the birds refused. They flew back to earth and left him in Skyland.

Then the Tortoise asked the sky spirits to help him. They told him to jump. "If you jump from the edge of the sky, you will fall to the earth. But you must tell your wife to prepare soft things for your landing—mats, blankets, piles of grass."

The Tortoise sent a message to his wife, but the message was garbled. Instead of telling her to prepare soft things, the messenger told her to prepare hard things—stones, rocks, iron pots. And so, when the Tortoise jumped from the sky and fell to the earth, he landed on hard things, and his shell cracked into many pieces.

That is why the tortoise's shell is checkered and cracked. And that is why, even today, the tortoise is known as the trickster, the one who schemes and plots, but who often falls victim to his own plans.

CHAPTER SEVEN: Why the Bat Flies at Night

After the Tortoise's fall, the animals learned to be careful of tricksters. But the Bat, whose name was Emiong, was a trickster of a different kind. He did not scheme with words. He schemed with his body.

In those days, the Bat lived among the birds. He had wings and feathers, and he flew during the day like all the other birds. But he was not content to be a bird. He wanted to be something more. He wanted to be special.

The Bat was friends with the Bush Rat, whose name was Oyot. They always fed together, and Oyot trusted Emiong completely. But the Bat was jealous of the Bush Rat. The Bush Rat had soft fur and a pleasant face, while the Bat was ugly and strange. The Bat wanted the Bush Rat's beauty for himself.

One day, the Bat said to the Bush Rat: "Let us go and bathe together. We will wash ourselves clean, and we will both be beautiful."

The Bush Rat agreed, for he trusted his friend. They went to the river and bathed. But while the Bush Rat was washing, the Bat secretly boiled a pot of water. When the water was hot, the Bat said: "Now let us jump into the water. It will make us clean."

The Bush Rat jumped in first. The water was scalding hot, but he did not know it until it was too late. He was burned alive.

The Bat watched the Bush Rat die, and then he took the Bush Rat's skin and wore it as his own. He went among the animals, pretending to be the Bush Rat. But the other animals were not fooled. They saw the Bat's eyes, which were not the eyes of a rat. They saw his strange walk, which was not the walk of a rat.

The animals confronted the Bat. They accused him of killing the Bush Rat. The Bat was afraid. He flew away and hid in a dark cave. All day long the people tried to catch him, so he had to change his habits. He only came out to feed when it was dark.

And that is why you never see a bat during the daytime. Only at night will you see him flying around, hiding from the eyes of those who know what he did.

CHAPTER EIGHT: The Mosquito and the Ear

Not all the stories of the animals are about tricksters and villains. Some are about love, and the foolish things that love can make us do.

In those days, the Mosquito was a handsome creature—or so he believed. He was small and quick, with long legs and a delicate body. He had been watching the Ear for a long time, for the Ear was a beautiful woman, and she was ready for marriage. Many creatures came to woo her—the big creatures with strength, the rich creatures with gifts. But the Mosquito came with something else.

He came with words.

Every day, the Mosquito would fly to the Ear and buzz his love into her. He would tell her how beautiful she was, how perfect she was, how he would do anything for her. He would whisper sweet things into her ear, hoping to win her heart.

But the Ear rejected him. She laughed at him. "You are too small," she said. "You are nothing. Why would I marry a creature who is no bigger than a speck of dust?"

The Mosquito was humiliated. He was angry. He was hurt. And he swore that he would not forget the insult. "I will make you pay," he said. "Every day, I will come to you. Every day, I will remind you of what you did to me. You will never have peace."

And so, from that day forward, the Mosquito has flown close to the Ear and buzzed. He buzzes to remind the Ear that he is still alive, that he has not forgotten, that he will never forgive. And the Ear, in turn, slaps at him, trying to drive him away. But the Mosquito always comes back. He will always come back.

That is why, even today, the mosquito buzzes in your ear. And that is why you slap your ear when you hear him, trying to kill the love that was lost.

CHAPTER NINE: Why the Hawk Kills Chickens

The Hawk was once a proud bird. He was a hunter, fierce and strong, and he was known throughout the land for his courage. But the Hawk was also lonely.

One day, the Hawk saw a Hen. She was beautiful, with feathers that shone like copper in the sun. The Hawk fell in love.

He went to the Hen and said: "I have been watching you. You are the most beautiful creature I have ever seen. Will you be my wife?"

The Hen was flattered. She had never been courted by a hunter before. She agreed to marry the Hawk. And for a time, they were happy. The Hawk brought her food from his hunts, and the Hen kept his nest warm.

But then the Rooster came.

The Rooster was a farmer's bird, fat and lazy and full of pride. He saw the Hen and desired her. He went to her and said: "Why do you waste your time with the Hawk? He is a hunter. He will always be a hunter. I am a farmer. I have grain and corn and a warm coop. I can give you a better life."

The Hen was tempted. She left the Hawk and went to live with the Rooster.

The Hawk was heartbroken. He had given his heart to the Hen, and she had betrayed him. He flew to the Rooster and said: "You have taken my wife. You must pay for what you have done."

The Rooster laughed. "What can you do to me? You are a hunter, but I am protected by the farmer. I have a coop and a fence. You cannot touch me."

The Hawk said: "You are right. I cannot touch you. But I will take what is yours. From this day forward, I will kill your chicks. I will take them from under your nose. You will never know peace."

And that is why the Hawk kills chickens. Whenever the hawk attacks, the hen says to her chicks: "Keep close to me, for my talons are soaked in blood." And the hawk circles in the sky, waiting, watching, remembering what he lost.

CHAPTER TEN: The Ogbanje

After the animals, the people learned about the spirits. And of all the spirits, none were more mysterious and more feared than the ogbanje.

The ogbanje were spirit children. They were not born of human parents. They were born of the spirit world, and they came to earth for a time, but they were never meant to stay. A mother would give birth to a child, love the child, care for the child. And then, when the child grew strong, the child would fall sick. And then the child would die. And then the mother would mourn. And then the mother would give birth again. And the same child would come back.

The elders say that the ogbanje are bound to the spirit world by a pact made before birth. They have friends in the spirit world, and they long to return. They are like travelers who leave their homes for a time, but who always yearn to go back.

But the ogbanje do not travel alone. They travel in groups, and they bring sorrow to many families. When one ogbanje child dies, others often die at the same time, in other families. They leave behind parents who are broken, mothers who are blamed for the deaths. The mother is said to be "infested" by the ogbanje spirit, and she is often treated with suspicion and scorn.

The people learned to fight the ogbanje. They went to the dibia, the healer, who knew the secrets of the spirit world. The dibia would search for the child's Iyi-uwa, a secret object that the ogbanje hid in the earth. This object was the tie that bound the child to the spirit world. If the dibia found it and destroyed it, the child could stay. The ogbanje would become like any other child, no longer running away to the spirit world.

Other times, the dibia would cut a mark on the child's body—a small scar, a tiny brand. If the child died and returned, the mark would still be there. The parents would know that it was the same child, and they would know that the ogbanje was not gone. The mark was a trap, a way to catch the spirit and force it to stay.

The elders say that the ogbanje are not evil. They are simply spirits who are caught between two worlds. They belong to the spirit world, but they have been born into the human world. They do not know where they belong. They do not know where they are wanted. And so they come and go, come and go, causing pain with every departure and every return.

CHAPTER ELEVEN: The River Goddess

Of all the spirits, the most beloved and the most feared were the water spirits—the mmiri goddesses who lived in the rivers and the lakes and the streams. The most famous of these was Idemmili, whose name means "pillar of water."

Idemmili was the patron goddess of Nnobi, a town in the Idemmili South region. She was a river spirit, a tributary of the Niger, and like most water spirits, she was a fertility goddess. She controlled the rain-clouds in the sky. She decided when they would fall and when they would hold back. She was the mother of the waters, and she was the mother of the people who lived beside her.

The people of Nnobi told many stories about Idemmili. They said that long ago, when human beings and spirits were still close, Idemmili would walk among the people. She would come to the market and buy food. She would sit by the river and talk with the women who came to fetch water. She was not a distant goddess. She was a neighbor, a friend, a mother.

But Idemmili was also a dangerous goddess. She did not tolerate disrespect. She did not tolerate greed. She did not tolerate the breaking of taboos. When people violated her rules, she withheld the rain. When people forgot her, she flooded the land. When people took more than they needed, she took back what she had given.

The people of Nnobi learned to honor Idemmili. They built shrines for her. They brought offerings to her. They gave their daughters to her service. They kept her laws. And in return, Idemmili kept them safe.

But the worship of Idemmili was not without cost. In some communities, the river goddess was believed to demand the lives of those who had wronged her. Those who drowned in the river were said to have been taken by Idemmili. Those who fell sick after swimming were said to have been warned. And those who became priests of Idemmili were often women, for the goddess favored women, and she gave them power in a world where power was often denied to them.

The elders say that Idemmili is still there. She is still in the river. She is still watching. And she still remembers every act of kindness and every act of cruelty.

CHAPTER TWELVE: The Kolanut and the Ancestors

After the sky moved away, after the animals and the spirits had taken their places in the world, the people learned about the ancestors. The ancestors were the Ndi Ichie, the living-dead, those who had left their bodies but who still watched over their families. They were not gone. They were simply in another room, waiting for the time when they would be reborn.

But how were the people to speak with the ancestors? How were they to know what the ancestors wanted? How were they to honor the dead and keep the balance between the visible and the invisible?

The answer was the kolanut.

In the beginning, the elders say, Chukwu created the kolanut as a gift to humanity. It was a small nut, round and pale, with three or four lobes like the fingers of a hand. But it was more than food. It was a bridge between worlds. It was the first thing offered to any guest, the first thing used in any prayer, the first thing placed on any shrine.

When the kolanut is presented, it is not simply eaten. It is blessed. The host holds the kolanut in his hand and speaks to the ancestors. He speaks to Ala, the earth goddess. He speaks to the spirits of the land. He asks them to come and eat, to accept the offering, to bless the gathering. He breaks the nut into pieces, and the pieces are shared among the people. The sharing of the kolanut is a communion between the living and the dead, between the human world and the spirit world.

The ancestors are called to the gathering. They are asked to "eat" the kolanut and to leave the living unmolested. Bits of the nut are waved over the head—a symbolism expressing the exchange of kolanut for life—and thrown out to the spirits. The invitation to the kolanut moves in hierarchies, from the creator to the divinities, from Ala to the ancestors, and then to friendly spirits.

The kolanut is a symbol of hospitality. It is a symbol of peace. It is a symbol of unity. When a man presents kolanut to a stranger, he is saying: "You are welcome here. You are safe here. You are one of us now." When a man refuses kolanut, he is saying: "I do not trust you. I do not accept you. We are not one."

The kolanut is also a symbol of respect. It cannot be presented by just anyone. It must be presented by the host, through the ranking male of his lineage. It must be broken by the eldest man present. It must be distributed in order of seniority. Women, no matter their status, may not present kolanut. This is the way of the ancestors, the way of omenala, the customs of the land.

And so, whenever the people gather, they bring kolanut. They speak to the ancestors. They share the nut. And they remember that they are not alone. They are surrounded by the living-dead. They are part of a great chain of being that stretches from Chukwu above to Ala below, and from the first ancestor Eri to the last child born in the village.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN: The Woodpecker's Boast

After all the great stories had been told—the story of the sky, the story of the sun and moon, the stories of the animals and the spirits and the ancestors—there remained one story. A small story. A story about pride.

The Woodpecker, whose name was Oturukpokpo, was known throughout the forest as a great borer. He could cut into the hardest trees with his long beak. He could make holes in the trunks of iroko and mahogany. His pecking echoed through the forest, a sound that was heard from far away.

But the Woodpecker was also a boaster. He liked to talk about what he could do. And one day, he boasted to all the other birds.

"You know," he said, "I am the greatest of all the birds. I can cut down any tree. I can bore through any trunk. When my mother dies, I will pull down the great iroko tree with my long beak. I will make her a coffin such as the world has never seen. I will sing songs of mourning that will be heard from one end of the forest to the other."

The other birds laughed. They did not believe him. But the Woodpecker continued to boast. He boasted for many days. He boasted until all the birds knew his promise.

And then, one day, his mother died.

The Woodpecker remembered his boast. He flew to the great iroko tree. He looked at the trunk, thick and ancient and hard as iron. He lifted his beak to strike.

But his beak was not strong enough. He struck the tree, and his beak bent. He struck again, and it cracked. He could not cut the tree. He could not pull it down. He could not make the coffin he had promised.

The other birds gathered around and mocked him. "Where is the great borer now?" they asked. "Where is the one who said he would pull down the iroko?"

The Woodpecker was ashamed. He flew away and hid in the forest. And from that day forward, he never boasted again.

But he did not stop pecking. He still pecks at trees, still bores into the bark, still searches for the food that lies within. But he does not peck for glory. He pecks for survival. He pecks because that is what he knows, and because he must.

And that is why, even today, the woodpecker is a humble bird. He does not sing great songs. He does not boast of his strength. He simply works, quietly, in the forest, remembering the day his pride was broken.

EPILOGUE: The Sky That Watches

The elders say that all these stories are one story. The story of Eri, who came from the sky. The story of the sky, which moved away. The story of the sun and moon, who quarreled and parted. The story of the tortoise, who tricked and was tricked. The story of the ogbanje, who came and went. The story of the kolanut, which binds the living to the dead.

These are not separate stories. They are different parts of the same whole. They are the way the ancestors understood the world. They are the way they explained why things are the way they are. They are the way they taught their children what it means to be human.

The sky is far away now. But it is not gone. It is still there, still watching. The spirits are still among us, though we cannot always see them. The animals still speak, though we have forgotten how to listen. The ancestors are still with us, waiting to be remembered.

And so, when we look up at the night sky, we remember Eri. When we farm, we remember the age of abundance. When we eat, we remember the kolanut. When we see the tortoise, we remember the trickster. When we hear the mosquito, we remember the love that was lost. When a child is born, we remember the ogbanje, who may come and go, but who is always welcome.

We are part of the whole. We are held within the cosmic order that Chukwu set in motion. And as long as we remember the stories, we will never be alone.

The sky is far away now, my child. But it is not gone. It is still there. And every time we look up, we remember.
