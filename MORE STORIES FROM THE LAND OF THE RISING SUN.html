MORE STORIES FROM THE LAND OF THE RISING SUN

Twenty-Eight Traditional Igbo Tales

STORY ONE: Why the Dog Is the Friend of Man

Long ago, before the villages grew around the compound fires, the Dog and the Tortoise were great friends in the wild. They shared the same hunting grounds. They drank from the same streams. They slept beneath the same trees. But a famine came, one of those terrible famines that the elders still speak of in whispers. The rains refused to fall. The yams shriveled in the ground. The streams turned to dust. The Dog and the Tortoise grew thin, their ribs pressing against their skin like the bars of a cage.

"We cannot continue like this," said the Dog. "We must go to the village of Man and seek help."

The Tortoise, who was always thinking, agreed. "You go first," he said. "You are faster. You will find help sooner."

So the Dog went to the compound of a man. He did not beg. He did not steal. He made himself useful. He barked at strangers who approached the gate. He played with the children, letting them pull his ears and ride on his back. He helped the man hunt in the forest, tracking the antelope and the bush pig. In return, the man gave him food from his own plate. He gave him a warm place to sleep by the fire. He gave him a name and a home.

The Tortoise went next. But the Tortoise did not make himself useful. He crawled into the man's yam barn and began to secretly eat the yams. He ate and he ate, filling his belly while the man slept. When the man discovered the theft, he was furious. He set a trap and caught the Tortoise. He was about to kill him when the Dog saw what was happening.

"He is my friend," the Dog said. "He does not understand your ways."

The man respected his loyal Dog. He let the Tortoise go, but he banished him from the compound forever.

And so the Dog stayed with Man, having learned that loyalty and service provide a better life than theft and cunning. The Tortoise returned to the wild, forever wary of Man's world.

The Lesson: Loyalty and honest service bring lasting reward, while cunning and theft lead to exile and shame.

STORY TWO: The Cat and the Rat

In ancient times, when the land was blind, the Rat and the Cat were friends. They lived together in a mud hut at the edge of the village. They traveled together to other lands to see their friends and family. They were so close that people mistook them for siblings.

The day of the great festival came. The two friends decorated their hut with nzu, white clay, and unyi, charcoal. They replaced the roof with fresh raffia palm leaves. They rested from the tedious work and thought about the food they would prepare for their guests.

"It must be a delicious meal," the Cat said.

"Indeed," the Rat replied. "Let us prepare the soup e richaa e chefuo uwa—the soup that makes you eat and forget the world."

They agreed to contribute ingredients. The Rat promised to go into town to steal from the old woman's spice basket. The Cat decided to kill a fowl in the barn house down the village road. Soon the firewood was roaring, and the soup pot was gathering steam. The two friends salivated as the smell filled the compound. They began to pray for the soup to be ready.

Their prayers were answered. The soup was done. To let it cool, they decided to take a stroll.

But on their way, the only thing in the Rat's mind was the delicious pot of soup. He was greedy. He started thinking about what he would do with the whole pot for himself. An idea came to him.

"Ewoo! Ewoo!" the Rat screamed.

The Cat asked why he was screaming.

"I forgot my smoking pipe!" the Rat cried. He begged the Cat to let him go and fetch it.

The Cat refused at first, but the Rat would not relent. He continued begging, and the Cat warned him to fetch it and return at once.

When the Rat got back to the hut, he jumped on the pot and started eating the soup as fast as he could. While he was eating and struggling with the pot, it fell over and trapped him. He was shivering, thinking of how angry the Cat would be. But he kept chewing on the pieces of fish and meat that fell on the floor.

The Cat became impatient. He ran home to look for the Rat. When he arrived, he saw the pot upturned.

"Ewoo! What happened to our soup?" the Cat yelled. He started calling the Rat, but the Rat did not answer.

Slowly, the Cat lifted the pot. And there was the Rat, covered in soup, his belly round, his paws greasy, his eyes wide with guilt.

The Cat did not speak. He simply looked at the Rat with a gaze that held all the betrayal in the world. And from that day forward, the Cat vowed to chase and kill every Rat he found.

The Lesson: Greed destroys friendship, and betrayal leaves a wound that time cannot heal.

STORY THREE: Mbe and the Squirrel's Granary

Mbe the Tortoise was a lazy farmer. His yam barn was empty. His fields were overgrown with weeds. His neighbors whispered about him, but he did not care. He preferred to sleep in the shade and dream of food he had not planted.

His neighbor, the Squirrel, was hardworking. His barn was full of nuts. His fields were neat and thriving. He rose with the sun and worked until the sun set. He had enough for himself and enough to share.

Mbe went to the Squirrel one day and said: "Friend, let us have a contest. We will see who can shout the loudest."

The Squirrel, who was simple and trusting, agreed.

Mbe told him to go inside his own granary to get the best echo. When the foolish Squirrel went inside and began to shout, Mbe quickly barred the door, trapping him inside. Mbe then began to leisurely eat the Squirrel's nuts.

After a few days, the Squirrel's wife came looking for her husband. Mbe, disguising his voice, shouted from inside the granary: "Go away! I am meditating!"

Finally, the wife grew suspicious. She got help from the other animals. They broke open the granary and found the skinny, weak Squirrel, surrounded by the shells of his own stolen nuts. They saw that Mbe had eaten everything.

Mbe was forced to repay the Squirrel twice over. He was shamed for his wicked trickery. And from that day forward, the Tortoise was never again trusted with the secrets of the hardworking.

The Lesson: Those who take from the labor of others will be exposed and forced to repay, and their shame will follow them.

STORY FOUR: The Hornbill and the Yams

The Hornbill gave his wife some yams. "Cook these!" he said.

After she cooked the yams, he said: "You eat them. I will get better food at my mother-in-law's house."

Yet when he reached his mother-in-law's house, they had already finished eating. The Hornbill got nothing. He flew back home, angry and hungry.

"Bring me those yams!" he told his wife.

But his wife had eaten the yams, as he had told her to do.

And that is why Hornbill is always hungry. He is never content with what he has, and so he ends up with nothing at all.

The Lesson: Contentment with what you have is better than chasing what you do not need.

STORY FIVE: The Chameleon That Revealed a Murder

A man was murdered in the forest. There were no witnesses. The murderer went back to the village and proclaimed his innocence. He wept with the family. He helped with the burial. He spoke loudly of justice and the wickedness of the unknown killer.

The village elders consulted a diviner. The diviner cast the stones and read the patterns. He said: "There was a witness. It was the Chameleon who sat on the branch above the crime."

The elders brought the Chameleon to the village square during the trial. They placed it on a white cloth. The Chameleon stayed white. It did not move. It did not change.

They asked the accused man to touch it.

When the murderer touched the Chameleon, its skin instantly turned blood red. The man's guilt was revealed for all to see. He confessed. He was punished. And the Chameleon was honored.

The Chameleon, in Igbo belief, is a sacred creature with a deep connection to Ala, the earth goddess. It is seen as a keeper of secrets and an agent of truth. Its ability to change color is not just camouflage—it is a magical ability to reflect the truth of a situation.

The Lesson: No secret is hidden from the eyes of the earth. Truth will always find a way to reveal itself.

STORY SIX: Why the Chameleon Changes Colour

Before the first man pressed his foot upon the earth, before the forests learned the names of birds, Ogwurugwu the Chameleon was one of Chukwu's chosen. Of all the creatures He had made, Chukwu found him wise and trustworthy. When a message had to travel from heaven to earth, it was Ogwurugwu who carried it. When he was sent, he returned. When he was questioned, he spoke the truth.

And because of this, Chukwu loved him dearly. Before sending him away, Chukwu gave Ogwurugwu something by which all creatures would know him: a colour of his own. A rich bronze, glowing like metal fresh from the blacksmith's fire. At sunrise, it shone warmly, and at dusk, it carried the colour of embers resting beneath ash.

Whenever the animals gathered in the village square beneath the moon, they admired him.

"Look at him," they would say. "He must be so lucky."

And Ogwurugwu would look up to the sky and say: "It is my Creator who has given me such beauty. He is the one who should be praised."

But praise is like palm wine left too long in the sun. What begins sweet in the morning can turn sour in the evening. It was not long before Ogwurugwu began to wonder why he remained only Chukwu's messenger while others sat upon thrones on earth. Pride crawled into his heart.

One day, he climbed a tree high enough that he could almost see the sky in its detail and called out to Chukwu.

"You have given me beauty that turns heads," Ogwurugwu said. "And in return I have served you faithfully. I have carried your messages. I have done all you asked of me. But today I come to you asking only one favour."

He told Chukwu of his desire to rule over the other animals as their king.

Chukwu was surprised. Not because a chameleon ruling over other animals had never been heard of, but because never before had a messenger asked for a throne.

"A crown is heavier than it appears," Chukwu said. "You are beautiful, Ogwurugwu. You are wise. You are loved. But you are not content. And so you must learn the lesson of contentment."

And Chukwu took back the bronze colour. He took back the solid hue. He gave Ogwurugwu instead the ability to change, to shift, to blend. From that day forward, the Chameleon could never hold a colour of his own. He could only reflect the colours of others. And when he moves, he sways uncertainly, tilting left and right, as if still searching for the beauty he once had.

The Lesson: A person who despises what they have may spend a lifetime wearing the gifts of others.

STORY SEVEN: The Origin of Death

During Uga Chi, the second age, death came to the world for the first time.

The earth dwellers, who were still able to see and speak face to face with Chukwu, were perplexed and disturbed by this new phenomenon. They conferred among themselves and elected the Dog and the Chameleon to meet God immediately on their behalf. They were to tell him what they had witnessed and plead with him that whoever died must wake up. Death should be strictly like sleep—ula bulu onye ma ya e buna uche ya. At the end of the time allotted to death, the dead would wake up and resume normal appearance and life.

True to the Igbo love of dualities, two messages were sent to God. One said: "When someone dies, the person should not wake up and live again." The other said: "When someone dies, the person should wake up and live again."

They gave the latter message to the fast-running Dog to deliver. The former message was given to the slow-moving Chameleon.

The earth dwellers were optimistic. The fast Dog would deliver the positive message with speed and bring back God's favorable grant of their request.

But the Dog, on his way to God's residence, chanced upon a woman pounding and extracting fresh, appetizing palm oil. He descended to help himself to the palatable fresh palm oil. He ate and he ate, forgetting his important message.

In the meantime, the slow-moving Chameleon crawled to God's residence and delivered the shattering message. By the time the Dog remembered and raced to God's residence to deliver the positive message, the Chameleon had already gone back to the earth dwellers with the negative grant.

God said to the Dog: "I regret that I cannot grant the second request. The first message that reaches me is the one I grant. The grant has already been made on the negative choice."

God, being merciful, did not send the Dog back empty-handed. He informed the Dog that the phenomenon of death now carried with it ozu na ola iwi—corpse, staleness, and great loss. He therefore gave Oku (fire) to the Dog to send back to humanity. Direct contact with God would also fizzle out to a trickle, so God would send many spirits and intermediaries to help humanity communicate with Him.

And so death became permanent. But so did fire. And so did prayer.

The Lesson: Great consequences can hinge on small distractions, and what is sent in haste may be lost forever.

STORY EIGHT: The Tortoise and the Pepper Soup

Once upon a time, the animals of the forest decided to have a great feast. There was all kinds of food—roasted yam, pounded cocoyam, palm wine, and honey. But the main dish was pepper soup. The pot was huge, filled with fish and meat and spices that made the air itself taste delicious.

The animals gathered around the pot. They were hungry. They were eager. But the Tortoise, who was always thinking, noticed something. The pot was too hot. The steam was rising. Whoever tried to eat first would burn their mouth. Whoever waited would get the best part.

The Tortoise spoke up. "My friends," he said, "this soup is very hot. We should let it cool. But we must also be fair. Let us each take a turn. I will go first, since I am the smallest and the most patient."

The animals, who did not see through his trick, agreed.

The Tortoise dipped his bowl into the pot. He ate and he ate. He ate the fish. He ate the meat. He ate the best parts, leaving only the bones and the watery broth. The other animals watched in growing anger, but they had agreed to the rule. When the Tortoise was finished, he stepped back, his belly round and full.

The other animals rushed forward. But there was nothing left but scraps.

And from that day forward, the animals learned a lesson. They never again let the Tortoise go first.

The Lesson: Cleverness may win a meal, but it will lose trust and friendship.

STORY NINE: The Tortoise and the Seven Magical Creatures

Once, when the Tortoise was hungry and his barn was empty, he wandered deep into the forest, farther than he had ever gone before. He came upon a clearing where seven magical creatures sat in a circle. They were not human. They were not animal. They were spirits of the underworld, the mmụọ who dwell between the worlds.

The Tortoise, though afraid, was also hungry. He greeted them respectfully.

The magical creatures looked at him. They saw his thin body and his desperate eyes. They took pity on him. They gave him a magic drum.

"When you tap this drum," they said, "all kinds of fruits will fall out. You will never be hungry again."

The Tortoise was overjoyed. He thanked them and carried the drum home. He tapped it, and indeed, fruits fell out—mangoes, bananas, pawpaw, and more. He ate until he was full. He shared with his family. He became the richest creature in the forest.

But the magical creatures had other surprises for the Tortoise. They had given him the drum to test him. They wanted to see if he would use it for good or for greed.

The Tortoise, drunk with power, began to boast. He invited all the animals to a feast and tapped the drum. Fruits fell in abundance. But then he tapped it again, and again, demanding more. He wanted to hoard. He wanted to sell. He wanted to become king.

Then the magical creatures appeared. They took back the drum. They gave him instead a second drum, one that they told him to tap only in times of true need.

The Tortoise, angry and greedy, tapped the second drum immediately, without need.

And from that drum came three hundred fierce Igbo warriors carrying sticks and whips. They beat the Tortoise and all his guests. They destroyed his compound. They scattered his family.

The Tortoise learned that day that magic is not a toy, and that greed turns even a gift into a curse.

The Lesson: Power given for good becomes a curse when used for greed.

STORY TEN: The Wrestler and the Ghost

There was once a wrestler whose back never touched the ground. He was the greatest wrestler in all the villages. He had defeated every challenger. He had won every contest. His fame spread from the Niger to the sea.

But the wrestler grew proud. He began to boast that there was no one—man or spirit—who could defeat him.

One day, a stranger came to the village. He was tall and silent, with eyes that did not reflect the light.

"I have heard of your boasts," the stranger said. "I challenge you to a match."

The wrestler laughed. "I have defeated the strongest men in the land. What can you do?"

The stranger did not answer. He simply stepped into the wrestling ring.

The match began. The wrestler charged. But the stranger did not move. The wrestler pushed. The stranger did not yield. The wrestler lifted. The stranger was heavier than stone.

And then the stranger spoke.

"I am a ghost," he said. "I have come from the land of the dead. I have come because you boasted that you could defeat anyone. Now you will learn that there are things beyond your strength."

The wrestler's back touched the ground. And the ghost disappeared.

The wrestler never boasted again. And the people learned that pride is a weakness that even the strongest cannot hide.

The Lesson: Pride goes before a fall, and the strongest man is weak before the spirit world.

STORY ELEVEN: The Girl Who Married a Ghost

Oglisa was the most beautiful girl in the village. She was also the most proud. She refused every suitor who came to her father's compound. She laughed at the farmer. She mocked the hunter. She rejected the trader.

"I will only marry a man who is perfect," she said. "A man who is rich. A man who is handsome. A man who has no flaws."

One day, a stranger came to the village. He was tall and handsome. His clothes were rich. His voice was smooth. He spoke of lands far away and treasures beyond imagination.

Oglisa fell in love immediately.

"Who is this man?" she asked her father.

"I do not know," her father said. "No one knows where he comes from."

But Oglisa did not care. She married the stranger.

They went to his home, far from the village. It was a beautiful house, but it was silent. There were no servants. There were no animals. There were no sounds of children.

One night, Oglisa woke and saw her husband standing by the window. He was not touching the ground. He was floating. And his face was not the face of a man. It was the face of a ghost.

She screamed.

The ghost turned to her. "You wanted a perfect man," it said. "I am perfect. I have no body to decay. I have no heart to break. I have no life to end. Is this not what you desired?"

Oglisa fled. She ran through the night, through the forest, back to her village. She fell at her father's feet and wept.

And from that day forward, she never judged a man by his appearance. She learned that pride blinds the eyes, and that what glitters may not be gold.

The Lesson: Pride goes before a fall, and appearances can deceive even the wisest.

STORY TWELVE: Why the Agama Lizard Bobs Its Head

In the beginning, when Chukwu was creating the world, the earth was soft. It was not firm. It was not stable. It was like mud that has not been pressed. The animals walked upon it and sank. The trees planted their roots and they wavered. Nothing could stand.

Chukwu called all the creatures together.

"The earth is not firm," He said. "Someone must press it down. Someone must make it hard enough for life to grow."

The animals looked at one another. Who would do this work? Who would press the whole earth with their body?

The Agama Lizard stepped forward.

"I will do it," he said.

And so the Lizard began to press down on the ground with his head. He bobbed up and down, up and down, packing the earth until it was hard and stable. He worked for days. He worked for weeks. He worked until his head ached and his neck was sore. But he did not stop.

When the earth was finally firm, Chukwu called the animals again.

"The earth is ready," He said. "Who will be honored for this work?"

But the animals had forgotten. They were busy with their own affairs. They did not praise the Lizard. They did not thank him.

The Lizard was hurt. He had given his strength to the earth, and no one remembered.

And so, even today, the Agama Lizard still bobs his head. He bobs it up and down, up and down. He is still testing the firmness of the earth. He is still doing the work he was never thanked for. And when you see him, you should remember the lesson: those who build the world are often forgotten, but their work remains.

The Lesson: The greatest work is often done by those who receive the least recognition, and we should honor them while we can.

STORY THIRTEEN: The Lazy Dog and the Tortoise

In a certain village, there was a Dog who refused to work. He would not help dig the well. He would not help clear the path. He would not help build the market stalls. When the other animals gathered to work, he lay in the shade and watched.

"If I work," he said, "I will sweat. If I sweat, I will be tired. If I am tired, I will be unhappy. Why should I be unhappy when I can be comfortable?"

The other animals complained, but the Dog did not care. He slept while they dug. He ate while they built. He drank from the well they had dug, and he walked on the path they had cleared.

The Tortoise, who was also lazy but clever, saw an opportunity. He went to the Dog.

"Friend Dog," he said, "you are the wisest of all the animals. You know that work is for the foolish. Let us make a pact. We will not work. We will simply enjoy the fruits of others' labor."

The Dog agreed.

But the Tortoise had a plan. He told the other animals to stop giving the Dog food. He told them to stop letting the Dog drink from the well. He told them to stop letting the Dog walk on the path.

The Dog grew hungry. He grew thirsty. He grew weak.

"Tortoise," he cried, "help me! The animals have turned against me!"

The Tortoise smiled. "You refused to work with them," he said. "Now you must learn to work alone."

But working alone is hard. The Dog could not dig a well by himself. He could not clear a path by himself. He could not build a market by himself.

And so the Dog went back to the animals. He apologized. He asked to join them. And from that day forward, he worked alongside them, remembering that no one can survive alone.

The Lesson: Everyone must contribute to the community, for no one can thrive in isolation.

STORY FOURTEEN: Why the Sun and the Moon Live in the Sky

Long ago, the Sun and the Moon lived on the earth. They were husband and wife, and they were the best of friends with Water. Water came to visit them often, and they would sit together in the compound and share stories and palm wine.

One day, the Sun said to Water: "My friend, you visit us so often, but we have never visited you. Why do you not invite us to your home?"

Water sighed. "I would like to invite you," he said. "But my home is very large. If you come, you must build a house big enough to hold all of me and all of my people."

The Sun and the Moon were determined. They built a great house, larger than any house in the world. They cleared a compound wide enough for a whole village. Then they sent word to Water: "Come. Our home is ready."

Water came. He flowed into the compound. He filled the first room. Then he said: "Are you sure you want me to come in?"

"Yes," said the Sun. "Come in."

Water rose to their knees. He rose to their waists. He rose to their chests. He rose to their necks. And still he asked: "Are you sure you want me to come in?"

The Sun and the Moon climbed onto the roof of the house. The water rose higher and higher. It filled the compound. It filled the house. It covered the roof. It covered the trees. It covered the whole land.

The Sun and the Moon climbed higher still, up into the sky, where the water could not reach them. And they have stayed there ever since. When the Sun rises, he looks down and remembers the friend who filled his home with water. When the Moon rises, she remembers too, and her light is pale and gentle, for she still loves the Water who drove her from the earth.

The Lesson: Be careful what you invite into your house, for even a friend may fill it beyond your capacity to hold.

STORY FIFTEEN: Why the Cock Crows at Dawn

In the beginning, the world was dark. Chukwu had made the sun, but the sun did not know when to rise. It slept in the belly of the sky. There was no morning. There was no evening. There was only one long, endless night.

The animals grew confused. They did not know when to sleep. They did not know when to wake. They did not know when to hunt. They did not know when to rest. The world was in chaos.

Chukwu called the animals together. "Someone must wake the sun," He said. "Someone must be the herald of the morning."

The Elephant stepped forward. "I am the strongest," he said. "I will wake the sun with my trumpet."

But the Elephant's trumpet was too loud. It frightened the sun, who hid deeper in the sky. The morning did not come.

The Lion stepped forward. "I am the bravest," he said. "I will wake the sun with my roar."

But the Lion's roar was too fierce. It angered the sun, who refused to rise. The morning did not come.

The Monkey stepped forward. "I am the cleverest," he said. "I will wake the sun with my tricks."

But the Monkey's tricks were too silly. They amused the sun, who wanted to see more. The morning did not come.

Then the Cock stepped forward. He was small and humble. "Let me try," he said.

The animals laughed. "You are the smallest of us all. What can you do?"

But the Cock stood upon the wall of the compound and crowed: "Kukuruku!"

It was a clear, bright sound—neither too loud nor too soft, neither too fierce nor too silly. It was the sound of a new day. The sun heard it and remembered its duty. It rose from the belly of the sky. Light spread across the world. And for the first time, there was morning.

Chukwu blessed the Cock. "You will be the herald of the dawn," He said. "Every morning, you will crow, and the sun will rise. And the people will know that a new day has come."

And so, even today, the Cock crows at dawn. He is not the strongest, nor the bravest, nor the cleverest. But he is faithful, and faithfulness is the greatest gift of all.

The Lesson: Great tasks are not always given to the strong, but to the faithful. The smallest voice may carry the greatest message.

STORY SIXTEEN: How the Tortoise Lost His Hair

In the beginning, the Tortoise was a handsome creature. He had a head of hair—thick, black, and shining like the feathers of the crow. When he walked through the forest, the other animals turned to look. The women admired him. The men envied him.

But the Tortoise was proud. He spent hours combing his hair. He rubbed it with palm oil until it gleamed. He refused to go anywhere without first admiring himself in the stream.

"There is no one as beautiful as me," he said. "Not the Leopard, not the Antelope, not even the Peacock."

One day, an invitation came to the forest. The spirits of the river were holding a great festival on the other side of the water. All the animals were invited. But to reach the festival, they had to cross the river, and the Tortoise could not swim.

The Duck offered to carry him across. "Climb on my back," he said. "I will take you to the festival."

The Tortoise agreed. But as they crossed the water, the Duck bobbed and dipped. The water splashed over the Tortoise's head. His beautiful hair was soaked. It clung to his skull, wet and heavy.

"My hair!" the Tortoise cried. "My beautiful hair!"

He was so ashamed that he could not bear to be seen. When they reached the shore, he hid behind a rock. He refused to enter the festival. He refused to be seen. And when his hair dried, it fell out in clumps, leaving his head bare and smooth.

From that day forward, the Tortoise had no hair. He could not grow it back. He could not hide his shame. And so he learned to live without vanity, remembering that pride in appearance is a fragile thing, washed away by a single splash of water.

The Lesson: Vanity is a borrowed garment. Pride in appearance is washed away by the smallest wave.

STORY SEVENTEEN: Why the Wasp Has a Thin Waist

The Wasp was once a round and comfortable creature. His belly was full. His body was broad. He was not handsome, but he was content.

Then came the great feast of the ants. The Ants had gathered food for a whole season—grains and nuts and sweet honey and ripe fruit. They invited the Wasp to eat with them.

The Wasp was delighted. He sat at the feast and ate. He ate the honey. He ate the nuts. He ate the fruit. He ate and ate, and his belly grew rounder and rounder. He could not stop. He did not want to stop.

"Eat more," the Ants said, for they were generous. "There is plenty."

So the Wasp ate. He ate until he could not stand. He ate until he could not breathe. His belly swelled and swelled, and a great pain filled his body.

"Something is wrong!" the Wasp cried. "I have eaten too much!"

The Ants ran to help. They found a rope and tied it around the Wasp's waist. They pulled it tight—tighter, tighter—until the swelling stopped. The pain eased. The Wasp breathed again.

But when they removed the rope, the mark remained. The Wasp's waist had been pinched. From that day forward, his body was divided into two parts, joined by a thin, narrow waist.

And that is why, even today, the Wasp has a thin waist. He remembers the feast. He remembers the pain. And he remembers that eating without restraint is the beginning of sorrow.

The Lesson: Gluttony brings its own punishment. Eat to live, and do not live to eat.

STORY EIGHTEEN: The Origin of Palm Wine

In the days before the villages knew the taste of palm wine, there lived a hunter named Okeke. He hunted in the deep forest, far from the compound, where the trees grew tall and the spirits walked among them.

One day, Okeke wounded an antelope, and it fled deep into the forest. He followed it for many hours, until he lost his way. Night fell. He could not find the path home. He built a small fire and lay down beneath a palm tree, hoping to sleep until morning.

But sleep would not come. The forest was alive with sounds—the hoot of owls, the rustle of leaves, the whisper of the wind. And then, above him, he heard a sound he had never heard before. It was like weeping—soft, musical weeping, dripping down from the tree.

He looked up. The palm tree was crying. From the top of its trunk, a sweet liquid fell, drop by drop. Okeke caught some in his hand and tasted it. It was sweet. It was warm. It was the most delicious thing he had ever tasted.

He drank and drank, and as he drank, the tree spoke to him.

"I was once a woman," the palm tree said. "I was a woman named Akwa, who loved a spirit. When the spirit returned to the other world, I followed him. But I could not enter. I was turned into a tree and planted here, at the edge of the world, so that I might weep for him forever."

Okeke was moved. "Is there nothing I can do for you?" he asked.

"Yes," the tree said. "When you return to your village, cut my trunk and let my tears flow into calabashes. Let the people drink and be merry. For joy is the greatest gift, and my tears will turn into joy."

Okeke did as the tree asked. He cut the trunk and collected the sweet liquid in calabashes. He carried it home to the village. And from that day forward, the people knew the taste of palm wine. They offered it at feasts, at funerals, and at ceremonies. And each time they drank, they remembered Akwa, the woman who loved a spirit and became a tree.

The Lesson: Sorrow can be transformed into joy, and the gift of the spirit world is meant to be shared.

STORY NINETEEN: Why the Bush Fowl Scratches the Ground

Long ago, the Bush Fowl was the servant of the King. She was a faithful servant, and the King trusted her with his most important tasks.

One day, the King called her to him. "Bush Fowl," he said, "I am sending you to my mother's village. Take this calabash of palm oil. It is sacred. It must not be spilled. It must not be opened. It must arrive safely."

The Bush Fowl took the calabash. "I will not fail you," she said.

She walked through the forest, careful with each step. She crossed the stream, holding the calabash high. She avoided the roots and the rocks. She kept the calabash safe.

But then she came to a clearing, and in the clearing she saw a field of ripe corn. She was hungry. She thought: "I will just take a little. I will set the calabash down for a moment."

She set the calabash on the ground and began to peck at the corn. And as she pecked, a great wind blew through the clearing. It knocked the calabash over. The palm oil spilled onto the ground. It sank into the earth. It disappeared.

The Bush Fowl was horrified. She scratched at the ground. She tried to gather the oil. But it was gone. The earth had swallowed it. She scratched and scratched, but she could not recover it.

She returned to the King, empty-handed. The King was angry. "You have failed me," he said. "You have lost what was sacred. From this day forward, you will scratch the ground forever, searching for what you have lost."

And so, even today, when you see the Bush Fowl in the forest, you will see her scratching the ground. She is still searching for the oil she spilled. She is still trying to make amends.

The Lesson: Do not let small desires distract you from great responsibilities. What is lost through carelessness may never be recovered.

STORY TWENTY: The King's Daughter and the Python

There was once a King whose daughter was named Nneka. She was beautiful and kind, but she was also very sick. No medicine could cure her. The diviners cast their stones. The herbalists brewed their potions. But she only grew weaker.

One night, a Python came to the King's compound. It was a great python, as thick as a tree trunk and as long as a river. The guards tried to kill it, but it spoke.

"I have come to heal the King's daughter," it said. "But I ask for one thing in return. She must become my wife."

The King was horrified. "A python? My daughter marry a snake?"

But Nneka was desperate. "I do not want to die," she said. "I will marry the python."

The Python opened its mouth. It spat out a small stone—a stone that glowed like the moon. "Swallow this," it said. "And you will be healed."

Nneka swallowed the stone. That night, the sickness left her. She grew strong. She grew healthy. She was herself again.

But the Python had not lied. It came to claim her. Nneka, true to her word, went with the Python to the river. She lived in the water, in the Python's home beneath the riverbed. The village mourned, for they thought she had been taken by a spirit.

But Nneka did not mourn. For the Python, in the water, was not a python at all. He was a prince—a prince who had been cursed by an evil spirit and could only be released by a true vow. When Nneka kept her promise, the curse was broken. The Python became a man.

They returned to the village. The King wept with joy. The Prince and Nneka were married in the village square. And they lived together for many years, remembering that even a python can be a prince if you keep your promise.

The Lesson: A promise kept, even to the most unlikely creature, can break the strongest curse.

STORY TWENTY-ONE: The Kind Brother and the Jealous Brother

There were once two brothers. The elder was named Emeka, and the younger was named Chinedu. They shared the same mother and the same father, but they were as different as day and night.

Emeka was kind. He helped the poor. He shared his food with the hungry. He never turned away a stranger. Chinedu was jealous. He wanted what his brother had. He resented Emeka's goodness. He wished him harm.

One day, a famine came to the land. The brothers had only one field of yams between them. Emeka suggested they divide the harvest equally. Chinedu agreed, but in his heart he planned to steal his brother's share.

That night, Emeka was walking in the forest. He came upon an old woman who was starving. She was a spirit, though Emeka did not know it. She asked him for food.

Emeka gave her his own share of the yams. He gave her his water. He gave her his cloth. She was grateful.

"You are a good man," she said. "For your kindness, I will give you a gift."

She gave him a small gourd. "When you shake this gourd," she said, "you will have everything you need. But use it wisely, for it can only be used for good."

Emeka thanked her and returned home. He shook the gourd, and food appeared. He shared it with his brother. Chinedu was amazed.

"Where did you get this?" he demanded.

Emeka told him the story. Chinedu was jealous. That night, he went into the forest and found the old woman.

"Give me a gourd too," he demanded.

The old woman looked at him. "You are not kind," she said. "Your heart is full of envy. But I will give you a gourd."

She gave him a gourd. "Shake it," she said. "And you will get what you deserve."

Chinedu shook the gourd eagerly. But instead of food, out came snakes and scorpions and fire. Chinedu ran screaming into the forest. He was never seen again.

And Emeka lived a long and happy life, sharing his gifts with all who needed them.

The Lesson: Kindness is rewarded, and jealousy is punished. What you give to others will return to you.

STORY TWENTY-TWO: Why the Goat and the Dog Are Enemies

Long ago, the Goat and the Dog were friends. They lived together in the same compound. They shared the same food. They worked the same farm.

One day, the King called them to him. "I am going on a journey," he said. "I am leaving my farm in your care. You will plant the yams. You will weed the fields. You will harvest the crops. When I return, we will share the harvest equally."

The Goat and the Dog agreed.

But the Dog was lazy. He did not want to work. He lay in the shade while the Goat dug the yams. He slept while the Goat weeded the fields. He ate the food that the Goat had prepared.

The Goat complained. But the Dog laughed. "I am the elder," he said. "You must respect me."

When the King returned, the harvest was ready. The Goat had done all the work. The Dog had done nothing. But the Dog went to the King and said: "The harvest is mine. I did all the work. The Goat did nothing."

The King looked at the Dog. He looked at the Goat. He saw the Goat's calloused hooves and the Dog's soft paws. He saw the Goat's thin body and the Dog's round belly. He knew who had done the work.

"You have lied," the King said to the Dog. "You have stolen the Goat's labor. For this, you will be punished. You will no longer live with the Goat. You will be driven from the compound. And wherever you go, you will be chased and beaten."

And so the Dog was driven from the compound. The Goat was given the whole harvest. And from that day forward, the Goat and the Dog have been enemies. The Goat remembers the theft. The Dog remembers the punishment. And they will never be friends again.

The Lesson: Do not steal the labor of others, for the truth will always be revealed.

STORY TWENTY-THREE: The Hunter and the River Maiden

There was once a hunter named Okonkwo who hunted in the forest by the great river. He was a skilled hunter. He never returned home empty-handed. He was respected by all the village.

One day, as he sat by the river, he saw a maiden. She was beautiful beyond description. Her skin was the colour of moonlight. Her hair flowed like the water. Her eyes were deep as the river.

Okonkwo fell in love at once. He approached her. He spoke to her. He asked her name.

"I am the daughter of the river," she said. "My father is the god of the water. I cannot leave the river. I cannot marry a man of the land."

But Okonkwo would not give up. He came to the river every day. He brought her gifts. He sang to her. He told her stories.

Finally, the river maiden agreed. "I will marry you," she said. "But you must make me a promise. You must never speak of my nature to anyone. If you do, I will leave you and never return."

Okonkwo promised. They were married. They lived together by the river. She cooked for him. She cared for him. She bore him children. They were happy.

But the people of the village whispered. They asked: "Where did Okonkwo find his wife? She is not from any village we know."

Okonkwo said nothing. He kept his promise. But one night, at a feast, he drank too much palm wine. His tongue loosened. And he told his friends: "My wife is the daughter of the river god."

The next morning, he woke and found his wife gone. The children were gone. The house was empty. He ran to the river. He called her name. But there was no answer. Only the water, flowing as it always had.

Okonkwo lived the rest of his life by the river. He never married again. He never hunted again. He sat by the water and wept, remembering the promise he had broken.

The Lesson: A promise is a bond that holds the worlds together. Break it, and you lose what you love most.

STORY TWENTY-FOUR: Why the Sky Is Far Away

Long ago, the sky was close to the earth. It was not high above, where it is now. It was just above the heads of the people. It was so close that you could reach up and touch it.

And the sky was generous. It gave food to the people. When they were hungry, they reached up and took pieces of the sky. The sky tasted like meat and yam and honey. There was no need to farm. There was no need to hunt. The sky fed everyone.

But the people grew careless. They took more than they needed. They threw pieces of the sky on the ground. They let it rot. They used it for play. They wasted the gift.

One day, a woman was cooking. She took a large piece of the sky and threw it into her pot. It was more than she could eat. She took another piece and threw it away.

The sky was angry. "You do not respect my gift," it said. "You waste what I have given. You take without gratitude."

And the sky rose. It rose higher and higher. It rose beyond the reach of the people. It rose until it was far above the earth, where it remains today.

The people wept. They begged the sky to return. But the sky did not answer. And from that day forward, the people had to work for their food. They had to farm. They had to hunt. They had to gather.

And so the sky remains far away—a reminder that gifts must be respected, and that waste is an insult to the giver.

The Lesson: Do not waste what has been given to you, for the generosity of the sky can be withdrawn.

STORY TWENTY-FIVE: The Wise Daughter of the King

There was once a King who had three daughters. The first was beautiful. The second was clever. The third was plain, but she had a heart full of wisdom.

One day, the King called his daughters to him. "I am growing old," he said. "I want to know which of you loves me most. Whoever loves me most will inherit my kingdom."

The first daughter said: "Father, I love you more than gold."

The second daughter said: "Father, I love you more than jewels."

The third daughter said: "Father, I love you more than salt."

The King was furious. "Salt? You compare your love for me to salt? You are ungrateful. You will leave my compound. You will not inherit my kingdom."

The third daughter left. She went to a distant village and became a servant. She worked hard. She was kind. She was loved by all who knew her.

Years passed. The King grew old. A great feast was held in his honor. The cooks prepared every dish—roasted meats, rich soups, sweet puddings. But the King's daughter, now a servant in the palace kitchen, was given a special task. She was told to prepare a dish for the King.

She prepared the finest soup in the land. But she put no salt in it. The soup was bland. It was tasteless. The King tasted it and spat it out.

"What is this?" he cried. "This soup has no salt! It is worthless!"

The daughter stepped forward. "Father," she said, "you banished me for saying I loved you more than salt. But without salt, the finest soup is worthless. Without salt, the greatest feast is tasteless. Salt is not precious like gold, but it is essential. It is the foundation of all flavor. And that is how I love you—not with the glitter of gold, but with the necessity of salt."

The King wept. He embraced his daughter. He restored her to her place. And when he died, he left his kingdom to her—the daughter who loved him like salt.

The Lesson: True love is not in grand words, but in the essential things that sustain us.

STORY TWENTY-SIX: Why the Leopard Has Spots

Long ago, the Leopard was the finest dancer in the animal kingdom. When the drums sounded at the village square, all the animals would gather to watch him. He moved like water. He leaped like fire. He was grace itself.

But the Leopard was proud. He boasted of his skill. He said: "There is no one who can dance as I do. I am the greatest dancer in the world."

The animals grew tired of his boasting. They said: "If you are so great, dance in the fire. If you can dance in the fire without being burned, then we will believe you."

The Leopard laughed. "Fire is nothing to me," he said. "I will dance in the fire. I will dance in the flames. And I will not be burned."

A great fire was built in the village square. The flames rose high. The animals gathered to watch. The drums sounded.

The Leopard stepped into the fire. He danced. He leaped. He spun. The animals watched in amazement. For a moment, it seemed he would survive.

But the fire was hot. The sparks flew. They landed on his skin. They burned him. They marked him. He danced faster. He tried to escape. But the fire was everywhere.

Finally, the Leopard leaped out of the fire. He ran into the forest. He rolled in the mud. He tried to put out the flames. But the burns remained. His beautiful golden coat was covered in dark spots—the marks of the fire.

The Leopard never danced in fire again. He never boasted again. He learned that pride is a dangerous thing, and that the greatest dancer can be humbled by a single spark.

The Lesson: Pride invites danger, and the strongest are not immune to the flames.

STORY TWENTY-SEVEN: Why the Snail Carries His House

There was once a Snail who was a chief. He had a fine house, the finest in the village. It was made of mud and thatch, with a door of carved wood. He was proud of his house. He invited all the animals to see it.

But the Snail had enemies. The Leopard wanted his land. The Eagle wanted his roof. The Snake wanted his door. They plotted against him.

One night, the enemies came. They burned the Snail's house. They destroyed his compound. They scattered his family. The Snail was left with nothing.

He went to Chukwu and said: "I have lost everything. My house is gone. My family is scattered. I have no home."

Chukwu looked at him with pity. "You have been faithful," He said. "You have been kind. You have been humble. For this, I will give you a gift. I will give you a house that cannot be taken from you. A house you can carry with you wherever you go."

And Chukwu gave the Snail a shell. It was a house—a small, round house. The Snail climbed inside it. It fit perfectly. He could carry it on his back. He could sleep inside it. He could take it with him wherever he went.

And so the Snail was never homeless again. He carried his house on his back. He was safe from the Leopard, the Eagle, and the Snake. And when you see a snail today, you see a creature who carries his home with him, remembering that safety is not in walls, but in the peace of a place you can always call your own.

The Lesson: What is taken from you can be restored in a different form. Home is not a place—it is a peace you carry within you.

STORY TWENTY-EIGHT: The Boy Who Talked to the Ancestors

There was once a boy named Ndubuisi who was born with a caul over his face. The elders said this was a sign. "He can see the spirits," they said. "He can speak to the ancestors."

And they were right. Ndubuisi could see what others could not. He saw the mmụọ—the spirits of the dead—walking among the living. He saw his grandfather, who had died before he was born, sitting by the fire. He saw his great-grandmother in the moonlight.

At first, the villagers were afraid of him. They whispered: "He is strange. He is not like us." But Ndubuisi was not strange. He was kind. He helped the sick. He comforted the grieving. He told the people what the ancestors wanted them to know.

One year, a famine came to the land. The rains did not fall. The crops withered. The people were starving. The elders did not know what to do. They consulted the diviner. They made sacrifices. But nothing worked.

Then Ndubuisi spoke. "The ancestors are angry," he said. "You have not honored them. You have not poured libation. You have not told the stories. The ancestors are waiting, and they are hungry for remembrance."

The elders were ashamed. They had forgotten the ancestors. They had become busy with the things of the world. They had neglected the spirits who had made them.

Ndubuisi led them to the shrine. He told them what to do. They poured palm wine. They offered kola nut. They told the stories of the ancestors. They sang the songs.

That night, the rain fell. The crops grew. The famine ended. And the people understood that the ancestors are always with us, waiting to be remembered.

Ndubuisi became a great healer and a great teacher. He taught the people to honor the ancestors. He taught them to tell the stories. And when he died, he joined the ancestors himself, and his voice was heard among them, guiding the generations to come.

The Lesson: The ancestors are not gone. They live in the stories we tell and the memories we keep. Honor them, and they will guide you.

EPILOGUE: The Stories That Remain

These are the stories the elders tell. Twenty-eight tales of animals who speak and spirits who walk. Twenty-eight tales of tricksters and fools, of kings and ghosts, of loyalty and betrayal. Stories that explain why the world is the way it is and why we must live the way we do.

The elders say that every story has a purpose. The Tortoise teaches us about cleverness and its limits. The Dog teaches us about loyalty. The Cat and the Rat teach us about betrayal. The Chameleon teaches us about pride. The Ghost teaches us about humility. The Sun and the Moon teach us about boundaries. The Cock teaches us about faithfulness. The Wasp teaches us about restraint. The Leopard teaches us about pride's fire. The Snail teaches us about home. The Boy teaches us about remembrance. The Sky teaches us about gratitude. The Kind Brother teaches us about generosity. The Bush Fowl teaches us about responsibility. The Palm Tree teaches us about sorrow transformed into joy.

These stories are not just entertainment. They are lessons. They are warnings. They are the wisdom of our ancestors, passed down from generation to generation, from the time when the sky was close to the time when the sky moved away.

And so, when the moon rises and the children gather around the fire, the stories are told again. And the lessons are learned again. And the world continues, as it has always continued, held together by the stories we tell.
