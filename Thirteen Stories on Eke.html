Thirteen Stories on Why the Eke (Python) Is Sacred in Igbo Land

1. STORY ONE: The Python That Was Our Father

A young man named Obiora was walking through the forest when he saw a great python coiled upon a rock. Its skin shimmered in the dappled light, and its eyes were calm, ancient, and knowing.

Obiora raised his machete. He had been taught to kill snakes, for snakes were dangerous. But something stayed his hand. The python did not move. It did not hiss. It simply watched him.

He returned to the village and told his father what he had seen. His father's face grew grave.

"My son," the old man said, "do you not know what you nearly did? That python is not a snake. It is an ancestor. It is your grandfather, returned from the spirit world to watch over you."

Obiora was confused. "How can a snake be my grandfather?"

His father sighed. "The elders say that when we die, our spirits do not simply vanish. They remain with us. Some live in the spirit world. Some are reborn as children. And some return as the Eke, the sacred python. The Eke is the reincarnation of our ancestors. It is addressed as 'our father.' To kill one is to kill your own blood."

Obiora never again raised his hand against a python. He learned that the Eke was not to be feared, but honored. It was a messenger from the world beyond.

The Lesson: The Eke is the reincarnation of ancestors. It is not an animal to be feared, but a kin to be honored.

2. STORY TWO: The Messenger of Ala

A woman named Nneka was troubled. Her crops were failing. Her children were sick. Her husband had grown distant. She went to the dibia and asked what she had done wrong.

The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He was silent for a long time.

"You have offended Ala," he said.

Nneka was shocked. "How? I have done nothing wrong."

"Think back," the dibia said. "Have you harmed any of Ala's messengers?"

Nneka thought. And then she remembered. Two moons ago, she had found a small python in her barn. She had been frightened. She had called her husband, and he had killed it.

The dibia nodded. "The Eke is the messenger of Ala, the earth goddess. It is the guardian of the land and the underworld. When you kill the Eke, you kill the messenger. You sever the connection between yourself and the earth. You invite Ala's anger."

Nneka wept. "What can I do?"

The dibia told her to make sacrifices. She was to offer a white cock and a white cloth at the shrine of Ala. She was to beg for forgiveness. She was to bury the python with the honors due to a kinsman.

Nneka did as she was told. In time, her crops recovered. Her children grew well. Her husband returned to her. And she never again harmed a python.

The Lesson: The Eke is the messenger of Ala, the earth goddess. To harm it is to offend the very ground beneath your feet.

3. STORY THREE: The Founding Father of Njaba

In the community of Njaba, in Orlu, the people tell a story that has been passed down for generations. Long ago, before the villages were built, before the farms were cleared, a great python led their ancestors to the land they now call home.

The python was not an ordinary serpent. It was a spirit, a guide sent by Chukwu himself. It slithered through the forest, and the people followed. It crossed rivers, and the people crossed with it. It stopped at a clearing, and the people knew that this was where they were meant to settle.

The python coiled itself around a great tree and watched as the people built their homes. And when the first child was born in the new village, the python was there, blessing the child with its presence.

From that day forward, the people of Njaba revered the python as their founding father. They called it Eke, and they did not harm it. When a python appeared in the village, it was considered a sign of blessing. When it entered a home, it was welcomed. It was the living link between the people and the land, the spirit of their ancestors made manifest.

The elders say that if you kill the Eke in Njaba, you are not just killing a snake. You are killing the founder of the community. You are killing your own heritage.

The Lesson: In some communities, the Eke is the founding father. It is the spirit of the land, and it must be protected.

4. STORY FOUR: The Python King of Idemili

In the land of Idemili, in Anambra State, there is a river that bears the name of the goddess who dwells within it. The goddess is Idemili, and her messenger is the Eke.

The people of Idemili have a saying: Eke Idemili, nne anyị—"Eke Idemili, our mother." The python is not just a messenger. It is a mother. It is the female goddess herself, taking the form of a serpent to watch over her children.

In Idemili, the python is treated with utmost respect and dignity. It is allowed to go wherever it chooses—into homes, into markets, into the sacred groves. No one harms it. No one eats it. No one even speaks of it with disrespect.

Charles Ejimbe of Akwukwu community in Idemili explained that the royal pythons are representatives of the gods and guidance of the children of Idemili. The python seldom bites people. If it does, it will sigh, retrieving its venom from its victim. It is not aggressive. It is not dangerous. It is a guardian.

The people of Idemili say that when a python appears, it is a sign that the goddess is near. It is a blessing. It is a reminder that the spirit world is not far away.

The Lesson: In Idemili, the Eke is the mother goddess herself. It is a protector, a guide, and a living symbol of the bond between the people and the divine.

5. STORY FIVE: The Python Lord of the Forest

The elders say that the python is the king of the forest. It is called Eke nwe ọhịa—the python, lord of the forest. It is not the largest animal. It is not the fastest. It is not the strongest. But it is the wisest. It is the most patient. It is the most powerful in ways that cannot be seen.

The python does not roar like the lion. It does not charge like the elephant. It waits. It watches. It moves in silence. And when it strikes, it strikes with precision.

This is why the Igbo admire the python. They see themselves in it. The Igbo are not a people of loud boasts. They are a people of quiet strength. They are patient. They are calculating. They are relentless.

The python is the lord of the forest because it knows the secrets of the forest. It knows where the prey hides. It knows where the water flows. It knows the paths that others have forgotten. It is the keeper of the forest's wisdom.

And so, when the Igbo call the python Eke nwe ọhịa, they are not just giving it a title. They are acknowledging its power. They are honoring its wisdom. They are reminding themselves that true strength is not loud. It is patient. It is knowing.

The Lesson: The python is the lord of the forest, not because of its size or speed, but because of its wisdom and patience.

6. STORY SIX: The Python That Came to the Wedding

A young couple in Idemili was getting married. The whole village was gathered. The drums were beating. The palm wine was flowing. Everyone was happy.

And then, in the middle of the ceremony, a great python appeared. It slithered into the compound and coiled itself beside the bride and groom.

Some of the guests were afraid. They began to move away. But the elders smiled. They raised their hands for silence.

"This is a blessing," the eldest man said. "The Eke has come to witness the marriage. The ancestors are here. They are happy."

The bride and groom were not afraid. They bowed their heads. The python remained for a moment, its eyes fixed on the couple. And then it slithered away, disappearing into the forest.

The marriage was a happy one. The couple prospered. And the elders said it was because the Eke had blessed them.

In Idemili and many other Igbo communities, the python is an expected guest at all significant celebrations—weddings, naming ceremonies, chieftaincy installations, and burials. When it appears, it is considered god-sent. It is a sign that the spirits are present, that the ancestors are watching, that the occasion is sacred.

The Lesson: The Eke appears at important ceremonies as a blessing. It is a sign that the ancestors are present.

7. STORY SEVEN: The Python That Retrieves Its Venom

A man named Okeke was walking through the forest when he accidentally stepped on a python. The python, startled, bit him. Okeke cried out in pain. He looked at his leg. There were two small puncture wounds. Blood was trickling out.

But then something strange happened. The python did not flee. It did not strike again. It looked at Okeke with what seemed like sorrow. And then it sighed. A long, deep, mournful sigh.

Okeke felt a strange sensation. The pain in his leg began to fade. The wound closed. The venom was gone.

The elders later explained what had happened. The python in Idemili and other communities is not like other snakes. It is sacred. It does not want to harm anyone. If it bites, it is because it was startled. And if it bites, it will sigh, drawing the venom back out of the victim.

The python is not aggressive. It is not dangerous. It is a gentle creature, a guardian, a mother. It does not want to hurt its children.

The Lesson: The Eke is not a creature to be feared. Even when it bites, it withdraws its venom. It is a protector, not a predator.

8. STORY EIGHT: The Killing of the Eke Nworie

In Owerri, there is a river called Nworie. And in that river, there lived a great python called Eke Nworie. It was an ancestral python, a deity, a guardian of the community. It had lived in the river for more than a hundred years. It had never harmed anyone.

But then, some students came to the area. They did not know the customs of the land. They did not understand the sacredness of the Eke. And they killed the python.

The community was outraged. The chief priest stormed the campus. He issued a seven-day ultimatum to the authorities. He warned that mysterious deaths might befall the community. He demanded that the students undergo a compulsory cleansing ceremony.

The killing of the Eke Nworie was not just a crime against an animal. It was a sacrilege. It was an offense against the ancestors. It was a desecration of the land.

The elders say that when you kill the Eke, you are not just killing a snake. You are killing your own father. You are severing the connection between the living and the dead. You are inviting the anger of the gods.

The Lesson: Killing the Eke is a sacrilege. It is like killing one's own father. It invites the wrath of the ancestors and the gods.

9. STORY NINE: The Python of Eke-Nnechukwu

The elders say that the python is not just an ancestor. It is not just a messenger. It is the feminine double of Chukwu himself. She is called Eke-Nnechukwu, the python that is the mother of the creator.

In the ancient mysteries, the python is the symbol of the earth goddess, Ala. But it is also the symbol of Chukwu's feminine aspect. It is the divine mother, the source of all life, the womb of creation.

The python's sinuous pose, its slow and graceful movements, its ability to shed its skin and be reborn—all of these are symbols of the cycle of life, death, and rebirth. The python is the ultimate symbol of transformation. It is the spirit that never dies, that simply changes form and continues.

When the Igbo look at the python, they do not see a snake. They see the mother of all things. They see the spirit of the earth. They see the source of life itself.

The Lesson: The Eke is the feminine double of Chukwu, the mother of creation. It is the symbol of transformation and eternal life.

10. STORY TEN: The Python That Guards the Sacred Grove

In every village, there are sacred groves. These are the places where the spirits dwell. These are the places where the ancestors are buried. These are the places where no human foot should tread without permission.

And in these sacred groves, the python is the guardian. It lives among the trees. It watches over the spirits. It ensures that no one disturbs the sacred ground.

The elders say that if you enter a sacred grove and see a python, you should not be afraid. You should bow your head and retreat. The python is not there to harm you. It is there to protect the grove. It is there to remind you that this is not your place. This is the place of the spirits.

If you harm the python, you harm the grove. If you harm the grove, you harm the ancestors. And if you harm the ancestors, you invite the wrath of the gods.

The Lesson: The Eke guards the sacred groves. It is the protector of the places where the spirits dwell.

11. STORY ELEVEN: The Python That Was Buried Like a Man

In some Igbo communities, when a python is accidentally killed, it is not thrown away. It is not eaten. It is not treated like a dead animal. It is buried with the honors due to a kinsman.

The culprit must go to the dibia. He must make sacrifices. He must undergo a ritual of cleansing called ikpu ani. He must appease the goddess of the land. And then he must bury the python in an elaborate ceremony, similar to the burial rites accorded to a human being.

The elders say that this is because the python is not an animal. It is a person. It is an ancestor. It is a member of the family. To kill it is to kill a kinsman. And a kinsman must be buried with honor.

When the python is buried, the people mourn. They do not shave their heads for a year, as a sign of mourning. They abstain from certain foods. They observe the rites of mourning as if a human being had died.

The Lesson: The Eke is not an animal. It is a kinsman. When it dies, it must be buried with the honor due to a human being.

12. STORY TWELVE: The Python That Brought Peace

A village was divided. Two families had been fighting for generations. The land was cursed. The crops failed. The children died. The people were suffering.

An old woman came to the village. She was a dibia, a woman of great wisdom. She looked at the two families and said: "You are fighting over land. But the land does not belong to you. It belongs to the ancestors. And the ancestors are angry."

She told them to go to the sacred grove. She told them to find the python that lived there. She told them to ask the python for forgiveness.

The two families went to the grove. They found the python coiled around a tree. They bowed their heads. They confessed their sins. They asked for peace.

The python did not move. It did not hiss. It simply looked at them with its ancient eyes. And in that look, the two families saw their ancestors. They saw the futility of their quarrel. They saw the pain they had caused.

They embraced. They made peace. And from that day forward, the village prospered.

The Lesson: The Eke is a peacemaker. It can heal divisions and restore harmony between families and communities.

13. STORY THIRTEEN: The Python That Never Dies

The elders say that the python is the only creature that never truly dies. When it grows old, it sheds its skin. It emerges new, young, and strong. It is reborn.

This is why the python is the symbol of reincarnation. This is why the Igbo believe that the ancestors return as pythons. The python is the proof that death is not the end. It is the proof that the spirit continues.

When an Igbo person dies, their spirit goes to the spirit world. But it does not stay there forever. In time, it returns. It may return as a child. It may return as a python. It may return in a form that we cannot see, but that we can feel.

The python is the reminder that life is a cycle. We are born. We live. We die. And we are born again. The python sheds its skin, and it is renewed. We shed our bodies, and we are renewed.

And so, when the Igbo see a python, they do not see death. They see life. They see the promise of rebirth. They see the endless cycle of the spirit.

The Lesson: The Eke is the symbol of reincarnation and eternal life. It reminds us that death is not the end, but a transformation.

These are the stories of the Eke. The python. The sacred serpent of Igbo land. It is the ancestor, the messenger, the guardian, the lord of the forest. It is the mother, the father, the protector. It is the spirit that never dies. And as long as the Igbo people remember the Eke, they remember who they are.
