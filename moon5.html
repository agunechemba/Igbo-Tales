Moon 5: Dreams and Visions

1. The Dream That Saved a Child

A woman named Adaeze woke in the middle of the night, her body drenched in sweat. She had dreamed of a river. In the dream, her daughter was playing at the water's edge. A crocodile rose from the depths. Its eyes were like burning coals. And then the child was gone.

Adaeze woke her husband. "We must go to the dibia," she said. "Something is wrong."

Her husband, a practical man, scoffed. "It was only a dream. Go back to sleep."

But Adaeze could not sleep. She held her daughter close, trembling. At dawn, she went to the dibia.

The dibia cast his Afa stones. He read the patterns. He was silent for a long time.

"Your dream is a warning," he said. "The crocodile is not a crocodile. It is a spirit. Someone has cursed your daughter. The river in your dream is the river of life, and the crocodile is the one who seeks to take her from you."

Adaeze wept. "What can I do?"

The dibia told her to make sacrifices to Ala, the earth goddess. He told her to offer a white cock and a white cloth at the river's edge. He told her to keep her daughter away from water for seven days.

Adaeze did as she was told. On the seventh day, a neighbor's child drowned in the river. The crocodile had taken another child instead.

Adaeze never again dismissed her dreams. She taught her children to listen to the night, for the night speaks in symbols, and the symbols carry life and death.

The Lesson: Dreams are warnings. To ignore them is to ignore the voices of the ancestors.

2. The Dibia Who Dreamed His Own Death

There was once a dibia named Nwankwo who was known throughout the land for his power. He could see the future. He could read the hearts of men. He could command the spirits.

One night, Nwankwo dreamed of a grave. It was open. His name was carved on the stone. And beside the grave stood a figure dressed in white, a spirit he did not recognize.

He woke and knew what it meant. His death was approaching.

He went to his fellow dibias and told them his dream. They cast their stones. They confirmed what he already knew.

"You have seven days," they said.

Nwankwo did not run. He did not hide. He gathered his family. He made sacrifices. He told his children everything he had learned in his long life. He taught them the secrets of the Afa. He blessed them.

On the seventh day, he lay down in his hut. He closed his eyes. And he did not wake.

The elders say that Nwankwo's dream was not a curse. It was a gift. The spirits had given him time to prepare. They had given him time to say goodbye. They had given him time to pass on his wisdom.

The Lesson: Not all dreams of death are to be feared. Sometimes they are invitations to prepare, to forgive, to leave a legacy.

3. The Dream of the Lost Tooth

A young man named Okeke had a dream. In the dream, one of his teeth fell out. It was painless. It was bloodless. But it fell into his hand, and he stared at it, confused.

He went to an old woman in the village, a woman known for her wisdom. He told her his dream.

The old woman was silent. She looked at him with eyes that had seen many things.

"The tooth is a person," she said. "A tooth in the mouth is a member of the body, just as a person is a member of the family. If the tooth falls, someone in your family will die."

Okeke was frightened. "Who? Which family?"

The old woman shook her head. "I cannot say. Only the dibia can tell you."

Okeke went to the dibia. The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He named a name.

Okeke was shocked. It was his uncle, a man who lived in a distant village.

Okeke sent word to his uncle. He sent gifts. He sent prayers. But the uncle, a stubborn man, did not listen. He did not make the sacrifices. He did not consult his own Chi.

Three weeks later, the uncle died.

Okeke learned that dreams are not always metaphorical. Sometimes the tooth is the tooth. Sometimes the symbol is the thing itself.

The Lesson: Dreams of losing teeth signify the death of a family member. When the ancestors speak, the wise listen.

4. The Dream of the Rising Water

A woman named Nneka had been barren for ten years. She had visited every dibia in the land. She had made sacrifices to Ala, to her Chi, to the ancestors. But her womb remained closed.

One night, she dreamed of water. It was not a river. It was not a stream. It was an ocean—vast, endless, rising. The water lifted her. It carried her. And she was not afraid.

She told her husband the dream. He did not understand.

She went to a dibia. The dibia listened. He nodded.

"Water is the symbol of the spirit world," he said. "In your dream, the water did not drown you. It carried you. This means that the spirits of the water are calling you. They want to bless you. They want to give you a child."

The dibia told her to go to the river. To offer white cloth and white kola. To ask the spirit of the water for a child.

Nneka did as she was told. Nine months later, she bore a son. She named him Chukwuebuka—"God is great."

The Lesson: Water in dreams is not always a warning. Sometimes it is an invitation. The spirits of the water can bless as well as take.

5. The Dream That Told the Future King

A young man named Eze was a hunter. He lived in the forest. He knew the animals. He knew the paths. He knew the secrets of the trees.

One night, he dreamed of a leopard. The leopard was not hunting him. It was walking beside him. It was leading him. And it led him to a compound he had never seen before. In the compound, there was an old man sitting on a stool. The old man wore the red cap of a chief.

Eze woke and told his father the dream.

His father was silent for a long time. Then he spoke.

"The leopard is Agu, the symbol of strength and leadership. It walked beside you, not against you. This means that you will become a great man. The old man in the dream is an ancestor. He is showing you your destiny."

Eze did not understand. He was a hunter. He had no desire to be a chief.

But in time, the old chief of the village died. There was no heir. The elders consulted the oracle. The oracle named Eze.

Eze became the chief. He ruled with wisdom. He ruled with strength. He ruled with the spirit of the leopard.

The Lesson: Dreams can reveal destiny. When an animal walks beside you in a dream, it is a sign of power, not danger.

6. The Dream of the Masquerade

A man named Okafor dreamed of a masquerade. The masquerade was tall and terrible. Its mask was black. Its body was covered in raffia. It danced in the village square, and the people fled in terror.

Okafor was not afraid. He stood his ground. He watched the masquerade dance. And then the masquerade spoke.

"You have been chosen," it said. "You will carry the spirit of the ancestors. You will speak for the dead."

Okafor woke and went to the dibia. The dibia nodded.

"The masquerade is the voice of the ancestors," he said. "When it speaks in a dream, it is calling you to a sacred duty. You are to become a priest of the ancestors. You are to serve the spirits."

Okafor was afraid. He did not want to be a priest. He wanted to farm his land. He wanted to raise his children. He did not want to carry the weight of the spirit world.

But the ancestors do not ask. They command.

Okafor became a priest. He served the spirits for the rest of his life. And in time, he understood that the dream had been a gift, not a burden.

The Lesson: Masquerades in dreams represent ancestral presence. When they call, they are calling you to service.

7. The Dream of the Flying Bird

A woman named Ada dreamed of a bird. It was a beautiful bird, with feathers of gold and blue. It flew above her, circling, singing. And then it landed on her shoulder. It whispered something in her ear. She could not hear the words, but she felt their meaning.

She woke with a feeling of peace. She did not know why.

She went to the market that day. She met a man. He was kind. He was gentle. He was everything she had ever wanted.

They were married within a year.

Ada always said that the bird in her dream had been her Chi, leading her to her destiny. The words she could not hear had been the voice of her spirit, telling her that love was coming.

The Lesson: Birds in dreams are messengers of the spirit world. They carry messages from your Chi and your ancestors.

8. The Dream of the Dead Father

A man named Chukwudi had a dream. His father, who had died ten years earlier, appeared to him. His father was not old and sick, as he had been in life. He was young and strong. He was smiling.

"My son," the father said, "you have not been to my grave. You have not made the offerings. I am hungry. I am cold. I am forgotten."

Chukwudi woke with tears on his face. He had been busy. He had been building his house. He had been raising his children. He had forgotten the ancestors.

He went to his father's grave. He cleared the weeds. He poured palm wine. He offered kola. He spoke to his father.

"Forgive me," he said. "I have been foolish. I will not forget again."

That night, he dreamed again. His father appeared. He was smiling.

"Thank you, my son," he said. "I am no longer hungry. I am no longer cold. You have remembered me. And I will remember you."

The Lesson: Ancestors visit in dreams. When they appear, they are not ghosts. They are living-dead, and they have needs. To remember them is to be blessed. To forget them is to invite misfortune.

9. The Dream of the Broken Pot

A woman named Ngozi dreamed that her favorite pot—the one she used for cooking for her family—had broken into pieces. She tried to gather the pieces. She tried to put them back together. But they would not fit.

She went to a diviner. The diviner cast his stones.

"The pot is your family," he said. "A broken pot means a broken family. Someone in your household is causing division. Someone is speaking against you. Someone is planning to leave."

Ngozi was shocked. Her family seemed happy. Her children were respectful. Her husband was kind.

But then she remembered. Her husband's brother had been visiting often. He had been whispering to her husband. He had been sowing seeds of doubt.

Ngozi confronted her brother-in-law. He denied everything. But his eyes betrayed him.

The family healed. The brother-in-law left. The pot was mended.

The Lesson: A broken pot in a dream signifies family conflict. It is a warning to guard your home against those who would divide it.

10. The Dream of the Seven-Headed Monster

A young warrior named Ike dreamed of a monster. It had seven heads and one eye. It stood in his path, blocking him. It opened its mouth to swallow him.

Ike woke in terror. He went to the dibia.

The dibia was grave. "The seven-headed monster is a dangerous spirit," he said. "It is a sign of great evil. Someone is plotting against you. Someone wants to destroy you."

The dibia told Ike to make sacrifices. He told him to wear a protective charm. He told him to avoid the path he had seen in the dream.

Ike obeyed. And for a time, nothing happened.

But then Ike grew careless. He forgot the dream. He walked the path he had been warned against.

The monster was waiting. Not a literal monster, but an ambush. Men with machetes. They had been hired by Ike's enemy.

Ike barely escaped with his life. He remembered the dream. He remembered the warning. He never again ignored the voice of the night.

The Lesson: A seven-headed monster in a dream is a symbol of deadly danger. When the spirits warn, the wise obey.

11. The Dream of the Snake

A man named Emeka dreamed of a snake. It was not a cobra. It was not a python. It was a small green snake, coiled around his leg. It did not bite. It simply held him.

He went to an old woman who knew the meanings of dreams.

"The snake is a spirit," she said. "A green snake is a spirit of the forest. It has chosen you. It wants to work with you. It wants to give you power."

Emeka was afraid. He did not want power. He wanted a quiet life.

But the snake came again in his dreams. And again. Each time, it coiled tighter. Each time, it whispered secrets he did not understand.

Finally, Emeka went to the dibia. The dibia confirmed what the old woman had said.

"You have been called by the spirit of the forest," he said. "You must accept. If you refuse, the spirit will turn against you. It will bring sickness. It will bring misfortune."

Emeka accepted. He became a healer. He learned the secrets of the forest. He learned to speak with snakes.

The Lesson: Snakes in dreams are spirits. They can be dangerous or they can be guides. To refuse their call is to invite their wrath.

12. The Dream of the Nakedness

A woman named Chioma dreamed that she was walking through the market, and she was naked. Everyone was looking at her. Everyone was laughing. She tried to cover herself, but she had no cloth. She tried to run, but her legs would not move.

She woke in shame. She could not look at herself in the mirror.

She went to a dibia. The dibia cast his stones.

"Nakedness in a dream is a sign of broken defenses," he said. "Someone has exposed you. Someone has revealed your secrets. Someone has shamed you."

Chioma thought of her sister-in-law, who had always been jealous. She thought of the things she had told her in confidence.

She went to her sister-in-law. She demanded the truth. The sister-in-law confessed. She had been spreading rumors. She had been telling Chioma's secrets to the village.

Chioma forgave her, but she did not trust her again. And she learned that some dreams are warnings not of spirits, but of people.

The Lesson: Nakedness in dreams signifies shame and broken defenses. It is a warning that someone is speaking against you.

13. The Dream That Came True

There was a man named Obiora who dreamed of a great feast. In the dream, he was seated at the head of the table. He was surrounded by his family. His children were laughing. His wife was smiling. The food was abundant. The wine was sweet.

He woke with a feeling of joy. He told his wife the dream.

"It is a good dream," she said. "It means that we will prosper."

Obiora nodded. But he did not simply wait for the dream to come true. He worked harder. He planted more yams. He traded more goods. He built a bigger house. He loved his family more deeply.

And in time, the dream came true. The feast was real. The joy was real. The abundance was real.

The elders say that a good dream is not a promise. It is an invitation. It is a seed. If you water it with work and faith, it will grow. If you neglect it, it will wither.

The Lesson: Good dreams are signs of good things to come. But they require action. The spirits can show you the path, but you must walk it yourself.

14. The Dream of the Market That Was Empty

A trader named Nnamdi dreamed that he went to the market, and the market was empty. There were no traders. There were no buyers. There were no goods. The stalls were bare. The ground was swept clean. And in the center of the market, a single vulture sat on a pole.

Nnamdi woke with a heavy heart. He went to the dibia.

The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He said: "The empty market is the sign of loss. Your trade will fail. Your goods will not sell. Your wealth will be scattered."

Nnamdi was afraid. He asked what he could do. The dibia told him to make a sacrifice to Ekwensu, the spirit of the market and of bargaining. He told him to offer a white cock at the crossroads. He told him to give to the poor.

Nnamdi did all this. But he also grew careless. He trusted the wrong partners. He lent money to men who did not repay. And in time, his trade failed, just as the dream had shown.

But Nnamdi learned from his loss. He began again, slowly, honestly. He never again ignored a dream of the market.

The Lesson: An empty market in a dream is a warning of financial loss. Take heed, or the dream will become your life.

15. The Dream of the Woman in White

A young man named Chidi dreamed of a woman dressed in white. She stood at the edge of a river. She was beautiful beyond description. Her skin shone like moonlight. She beckoned to him.

Chidi woke and told his mother the dream. His mother's face grew pale.

"The woman in white is a water spirit," she said. "She has chosen you. She wants to marry you. If you go to her, you will not return."

Chidi laughed. He did not believe in spirits. He went to the river the next day.

He saw a woman in white standing at the water's edge. She smiled at him. He stepped forward. But something stopped him—a voice in his head, the voice of his mother, the voice of his Chi.

He turned and ran. He did not look back.

That night, he dreamed again. The woman in white was angry. She said: "You have rejected me. I will wait. But you will never be happy with any other woman."

Chidi never married. He lived alone for the rest of his days. He had escaped the spirit, but he had not escaped her curse.

The Lesson: Dreams of water spirits are not to be taken lightly. To reject the spirit is to invite its vengeance.

16. The Dream of the Broken Ofo

An elder named Okonkwo dreamed that his ofo—the sacred staff of his ancestors—had broken in two. The two pieces lay on the ground, and no matter how hard he tried, he could not join them back together.

Okonkwo woke in despair. He went to the oldest man in the village, a man who remembered the days before the British came.

The old man said: "The ofo is the covenant between you and your ancestors. A broken ofo means a broken covenant. Someone in your lineage has broken an oath. Someone has told a lie on the sacred staff."

Okonkwo gathered his family. He questioned them. And finally, one of his sons confessed. He had sworn falsely on the ofo. He had lied to win a case against a neighbor.

Okonkwo wept. He took his son to the shrine. The son confessed before the elders. He made a sacrifice. He was cleansed. And Okonkwo's ofo was repaired—not the wood, but the covenant.

The Lesson: A broken ofo in a dream is a warning that the covenant with the ancestors has been broken. Restore it before it is too late.

17. The Dream of the Two Roads

A young man named Ikechukwu dreamed that he was standing at a crossroads. Two roads stretched before him. One was wide and smooth. The other was narrow and rocky. On the wide road, he saw crowds of people, laughing and dancing. On the narrow road, he saw only one figure—a man in white, walking alone.

Ikechukwu woke and went to the dibia.

The dibia said: "The wide road is the road of pleasure. It is easy, but it leads to destruction. The narrow road is the road of the ancestors. It is hard, but it leads to life."

Ikechukwu was tempted by the wide road. He wanted to be with the crowd. He wanted to laugh and dance. But something in him chose the narrow road.

He followed the man in white. He walked for many days. He grew tired. He grew hungry. But he did not stop.

At the end of the narrow road, he found a village. It was his village—but it was transformed. The people were happy. The crops were abundant. The children played without fear.

The man in white turned and said: "You have chosen wisely. This is the reward of those who walk the road of the ancestors."

The Lesson: The two roads in a dream are the choices of life. The easy path leads to death; the hard path leads to life.

18. The Dream of the Ancestor Who Was Weeping

A woman named Obiageli dreamed that her grandmother—who had died twenty years earlier—was sitting by the fire, weeping. The old woman did not speak. She simply wept. And her tears fell onto the ground and turned into blood.

Obiageli woke in terror. She went to her mother.

Her mother was silent for a long time. Then she said: "The weeping grandmother is a sign of sorrow in the lineage. The blood in the tears means that someone in the family has been dishonored. Someone has been wronged."

Obiageli investigated. She discovered that a cousin had been denied her inheritance. The men of the family had taken her father's land and refused to give her anything.

Obiageli went to the men. She spoke for her cousin. She said: "The ancestors are weeping. The grandmother has seen the injustice. If you do not make it right, the family will suffer."

The men relented. They gave the cousin her share. And that night, Obiageli dreamed again. Her grandmother was smiling. She was no longer weeping.

The Lesson: Ancestors weep when injustice is done in the family. Their tears call us to make things right.

19. The Dream of the Witch Who Came at Night

A man named Emeka dreamed that a witch came to his house at night. She was an old woman, bent and wrinkled. She carried a basket. She opened the door without knocking. She entered without permission. And she began to take things from his house—his yams, his cloth, his cowries.

Emeka tried to stop her. But he could not move. He was paralyzed. He watched as she took everything.

He woke in the morning, drenched in sweat. He went to the dibia.

The dibia cast his stones. He said: "The witch in your dream is real. She is a witch in the spirit world. She has been feeding on your fortune. You must protect your home."

The dibia gave Emeka a charm. He told him to place it at the door of his house. He told him to make a sacrifice to his Chi. He told him to watch for the witch in the waking world.

In time, Emeka discovered that an old woman in the village had been cursing him. She had been jealous of his success. He confronted her. She did not deny it. She was driven out of the village. And Emeka prospered once more.

The Lesson: Witches attack in dreams. The dream is the battlefield. Protect yourself, or the witch will take everything.

20. The Dream of the River That Turned to Blood

A woman named Nneoma dreamed that she was standing by the river. The water was clear. The fish were swimming. And then, slowly, the water began to turn red. It became blood. The fish died. The birds flew away. And a voice said: "The land is polluted. The ancestors are angry."

Nneoma woke and went to the dibia. The dibia was grave.

"The river of blood is a warning of abomination," he said. "Someone in the community has committed a great sin. The land is polluted. The ancestors are calling for cleansing."

The dibia told Nneoma to call the elders. He told her to gather the community. He told her to seek out the abomination and cleanse it.

The elders gathered. They questioned the people. They discovered that a man had committed incest—he had slept with his cousin. The abomination was hidden. The river was crying out.

The man was brought before the elders. He confessed. He made a great sacrifice. The land was cleansed. And the river ran clear again.

The Lesson: A river of blood in a dream is a sign of abomination in the land. It must be cleansed, or the whole community will suffer.

21. The Dream of the Talking Drum

A drummer named Okwu dreamed of the great Ikoro drum. It was not silent. It spoke. It said: "The village is in danger. The enemies are coming. Wake the people. Beat the alarm."

Okwu woke in terror. He ran to the village square. He beat the Ikoro. The people gathered. They were confused. There was no enemy. There was no fire. There was no flood.

The elders were angry. They said: "You have beaten the Ikoro in vain. You must be punished."

But Okwu insisted. "The drum spoke to me in a dream. It told me that enemies are coming."

The elders consulted the dibia. The dibia cast his stones. He nodded. "The dream is true. The Ikoro spoke. An enemy is coming—not from outside, but from within. Someone in the village is planning to betray us."

The elders investigated. They discovered that a man had been meeting with the warriors of a neighboring village. He was planning to lead them into the village at night.

The traitor was caught. He was punished. And from that day forward, no one in the village ever doubted the dreams of the drummer.

The Lesson: The talking drum in a dream is the voice of the ancestors. When the Ikoro speaks, the village must listen.

22. The Dream of the Dead Child Who Returned

A woman named Ada had lost her first child—a son—when he was only three years old. She had mourned for many years. But then, one night, she dreamed that the child came to her. He was not a baby anymore. He was a young man. He was smiling.

"Mother," he said, "I am coming back to you. I will be born again. And when I am born, you will know me by the mark on my shoulder."

Ada woke and wept. She became pregnant. She gave birth to another son. And when the baby was born, there was a mark on his shoulder—the exact mark she had seen in her dream.

She named him Chukwuebuka—"God is great." And she raised him with the knowledge that he was her first son, returned to her.

The elders said that this was the work of Chi—the personal god that guides a person's destiny. The Chi of the first child had chosen to return. The Chi of the mother had called him back.

The Lesson: Dreams can reveal the return of the dead. The Chi is the spirit of destiny. It can choose to be reborn.

23. The Dream of the Falling Stars

A young man named Chukwudi dreamed that he was standing in an open field. Above him, the stars were falling. One by one, they dropped from the sky. They fell to the earth and disappeared. And then, there was only darkness.

Chukwudi woke in fear. He went to the dibia. The dibia cast his stones. He was silent for a long time.

"The falling stars are the deaths of great men," he said. "One by one, the elders of the land will die. The generation that holds the wisdom will pass away."

Chukwudi was troubled. He went to the elders. He told them his dream. They did not laugh. They did not dismiss him. They said: "The dream is a warning. We must prepare. We must pass on our wisdom before we go."

And so the elders gathered the young people. They told them the stories. They taught them the customs. They shared the secrets of the ancestors. And in the years that followed, one by one, they died—but the wisdom lived on.

The Lesson: Falling stars in a dream signal the death of elders. Prepare to receive their wisdom before they go.

24. The Dream of the House With No Door

A woman named Chioma dreamed that she was trapped in a house. The house had no door. It had no windows. It had no way out. She walked around the walls. She beat on them with her fists. She screamed. But no one came.

Chioma woke in despair. She went to the dibia.

The dibia said: "The house with no door is the prison of the spirit. You are trapped in a situation that you cannot escape. You feel helpless. You feel alone."

Chioma wept. "What can I do?"

The dibia told her to make a sacrifice to her Chi. He told her to call upon her ancestors. He told her to look for the door that she could not see.

Chioma did as she was told. She made the sacrifice. She prayed. And one day, she realized that the door had always been there. It was not a physical door. It was a decision she had been afraid to make. She made the decision. She left the situation that had trapped her. And she was free.

The Lesson: A house with no door in a dream is a sign of entrapment. The door is always there—it is the choice you are afraid to make.

25. The Dream of the Pot That Would Not Boil

A woman named Ngozi dreamed that she was cooking. She had filled the pot with water. She had placed it on the fire. She had added the yams and the palm oil. But the pot would not boil. The fire burned. The wood crackled. But the water stayed cold.

Ngozi woke and went to the dibia. The dibia said: "The pot that will not boil is the sign of a blocked destiny. You are working hard, but nothing is happening. Your efforts are not bearing fruit."

Ngozi was discouraged. She had been working hard. She had been planting and trading and saving. But nothing had changed.

The dibia told her to consult her Chi. He told her to make a sacrifice. He told her to look for the block in her life.

Ngozi looked. And she found it. She had been harboring anger against her sister. She had been refusing to forgive. The anger had blocked her destiny.

Ngozi forgave her sister. She made peace. And the pot began to boil.

The Lesson: A pot that will not boil is a sign of blocked destiny. The block is often unforgiveness. Release it, and your destiny will flow.

26. The Dream of the Leopard That Spoke

A hunter named Ozo dreamed that he was in the forest. A great leopard stood before him. It did not attack. It did not flee. It spoke.

"You have hunted my brothers," the leopard said. "You have killed them and sold their skins. But I do not come for vengeance. I come with a message. You have been chosen. You will be a great leader. But you must stop killing the leopards. They are your kin."

Ozo woke in terror. He went to the dibia. The dibia was not surprised.

"The leopard is the spirit of your lineage," he said. "Your ancestors have chosen you. But you have been killing their messengers. You must stop. You must honor the Agu."

Ozo stopped hunting leopards. He made a shrine to the leopard. He offered sacrifices. And in time, he became a great leader—a chief whose word was law, a man whose wisdom was sought by all.

The Lesson: The leopard that speaks in a dream is the spirit of the lineage. When it chooses you, obey. Honor the Agu, and you will be blessed.

27. The Dream of the Masked Dancers Who Came for the Child

A mother named Ada dreamed that masked dancers came to her compound. They were not the familiar masquerades of the village. They were strange. They were silent. They came to her door and called for her child.

Ada woke in terror. She held her child close. She went to the dibia.

The dibia cast his stones. He said: "The masked dancers are the spirits of the other world. They have come for your child. They want to take her back to the spirit world."

Ada wept. "What can I do?"

The dibia told her to make a sacrifice. He told her to tie a protective charm around the child's neck. He told her to keep the child indoors for seven days.

Ada did as she was told. On the seventh day, she dreamed again. The masked dancers came. They stood at the door. But they could not enter. They turned and walked away. And Ada's child lived.

The Lesson: Masked dancers in a dream may be spirits who come for a child. When this dream comes, act quickly. Make the sacrifices. Protect the child.

28. The Dream That Never Ends

There was once a man named Ndubuisi who was born with a caul over his face. The elders said this was a sign. "He can see the spirits," they said. "He can speak to the ancestors. And he can dream dreams that never end."

And they were right. Ndubuisi dreamed every night. Not the small dreams of ordinary men. Great dreams. Visions. He saw the past. He saw the future. He saw the spirits walking among the living. He saw the ancestors gathered at the shrine.

At first, the villagers were afraid of him. They whispered: "He is strange. He is not like us." But Ndubuisi was not strange. He was kind. He helped the sick. He comforted the grieving. He told the people what the ancestors wanted them to know.

One year, a famine came to the land. The rains did not fall. The crops withered. The people were starving. The elders did not know what to do.

Then Ndubuisi spoke. "The ancestors are angry," he said. "You have not honored them. You have not poured libation. You have not told the stories. The ancestors are waiting, and they are hungry for remembrance."

The elders were ashamed. They had forgotten the ancestors. Ndubuisi led them to the shrine. They poured palm wine. They offered kola nut. They told the stories of the ancestors. They sang the songs.

That night, the rain fell. The crops grew. The famine ended. And the people understood that the dreamer is a gift. The dreamer is the bridge between the worlds.

Ndubuisi became a great healer and a great teacher. He taught the people to honor the ancestors. He taught them to listen to their dreams. And when he died, he joined the ancestors himself, and his voice was heard among them, guiding the generations to come.

The Lesson: The dreamer is the bridge between the living and the dead. Honor the dreamer, for the dreamer speaks for the ancestors.
