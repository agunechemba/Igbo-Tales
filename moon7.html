Moon 7: Oracles, Chi, and Ikenga

1. The Oracle of the Hills and Caves

A man named Unoka was known throughout Umuofia for his idleness and his constant misfortune. His yams were always the smallest, his harvest always the poorest. In desperation, he traveled to the Oracle of the Hills and Caves, the great Agbala, to learn why his life was so barren.

He crawled into the dark cave, where the priestess, Chielo, sat by a fire. Her voice was not her own. The spirit of Agbala spoke through her, its voice echoing off the stone walls.

"You are not cursed," the oracle said. "You are simply lazy. You plant when you should reap. You talk when you should work. Your Chi has turned its back on you because you have given it nothing to work with."

Unoka was ashamed. He had hoped for a curse to lift, but the oracle had named the truth. He returned home, but he did not change his ways. And so his Chi remained idle, and his life remained barren.

The Lesson: The oracle reveals truth, but it does not change destiny. It is the man himself who must act on what he hears.

2. The Priestess Who Carried a Child to the Oracle

The priestess Chielo was a woman of two faces. In the market, she laughed and traded and gossiped like any other woman. But when the spirit of Agbala came upon her, she was feared by all.

One night, she came to the compound of Okonkwo. She carried his daughter, Ezinma, on her back, taking the child to the oracle.

"Agbala wants to see you," she said, and her voice was not her own.

Okonkwo, a man of war and strength, pleaded with the priestess, but she would not listen. She carried the child through the forest paths, and the people of Umuofia watched in silence, knowing that no one challenges the oracle.

The child survived the night. But the memory of that journey stayed with her family forever. It reminded them that the oracle's will is absolute, and that the gods are not bound by human love or fear.

The Lesson: The oracle's demands can be terrifying, but they must be respected. The gods do not negotiate with mortal fear.

3. The Dibia's Certificate of Merit

In the colonial era, a great dibia named Chinweuba Onumba from Nando in Aguleri was known for his extraordinary skill with Afa divination. He was once summoned by Ezeugboyamba to inquire about his pregnant wife. Chinweuba cast his Afa and prophesied: the child would be born on an Afo day, an attempt would be made to kill him, and he would become an illustrious son of his people. All of this came to pass—the child was Dr. Akwaeke Nwafor Orizu, who became the second Senate President of Nigeria, and on the day of his birth, a pot of poison was found where he was born.

But the dibia's greatest test came when he was arrested and brought to court by a family who believed he had used sorcery to kill their patriarch. In the white colonial court, the judge brought out a pod and challenged Chinweuba: if he truly knew through Afa that the man had died, he should use his divination to tell the court how many seeds were in the pod.

Chinweuba called on his ancestors. He threw his Afa down. He declared: "You will find six seeds." The pod was opened. There were exactly six seeds. The judge was astonished. He declared that this was not magic, but a finely-honed tradition of the Igbo. He commanded that a Certificate of Merit be issued to Chinweuba Onumba, making him the first dibia to receive a license to practice divination during Nigeria's colonial era.

The Lesson: True skill and spiritual knowledge command respect, even from those who do not understand them.

4. The Ikenga of the Warrior

Ikenga was the son of Ngwu, and from his youth, he was unlike other children. He was a bold warrior and a fantastic wrestler, reputed with the ability to fly from one iroko tree to another. He was immensely favored by Amadioha, the god of thunder, to whom he owed his greatness and reputation. In battle, Ikenga fought mortals and immortals alike. He challenged gods and feared no deity.

But Ikenga's strength was not his own. It came from his right hand, which held the machete and the hoe. It came from his Chi, the personal god that Chukwu had given him at conception. It came from his ancestors, whose spirits watched over him. When he prepared for battle, he offered sacrifices to his Ikenga—the horned shrine that stood on his family altar. He rubbed it with white clay. He sprayed it with chewed kola nut. He prayed.

And because he honored his Ikenga, his right hand never failed him. His enemies fell before him. His fields yielded abundance. His name was spoken with awe throughout the land.

The Lesson: A man's strength is not his alone. It flows from his Chi, his ancestors, and the right hand that they bless.

5. The Kolanut That Told the Truth

A man named Okafor was accused of stealing from his neighbor. He denied it vehemently. He swore on his father's grave. He wept and cried out to the ancestors.

The elders brought a kolanut. It was a four-lobed nut, an oji ezumezu, the most sacred kind. The eldest man among them held it up and spoke to the ancestors. He asked them to come and eat. He asked them to reveal the truth.

He broke the kolanut and threw the lobes gently into a wooden bowl. The spread of the lobes was a coded message from the Ndi Ichie, the ancestors. Those who knew the art could read it.

The lobes fell in a pattern that spoke of deceit. The elders looked at Okafor. He began to sweat. He began to tremble. And then he confessed.

The kolanut had spoken. The ancestors had seen. There was no need for further trial.

The Lesson: The kolanut is a bridge between the living and the dead. When it is broken, the ancestors are present, and they do not lie.

6. Onalu and the Oracle of the Ancestors

There was once a woman named Onalu who had been married for ten years and had borne no child. Her husband's family mocked her. Her co-wives laughed at her. The village whispered that her Chi had turned its back on her, that she was cursed, that she was barren by the decree of the ancestors.

Onalu did not accept this. She said: "If my Chi has turned its back on me, I will go and find it. If the ancestors have cursed me, I will go and ask them why."

She went to the great oracle of her people—not the Long Juju, which was far away, but the smaller oracle of the ancestral shrine at the edge of the village. The oracle was not a cave or a tunnel. It was a sacred grove, guarded by an old priestess named Nne-Ani.

Nne-Ani welcomed her. She said: "You have come to ask about your Chi. But the Chi does not speak in words. It speaks in signs. You must spend the night in the grove. You must fast. You must listen."

Onalu spent the night in the grove. She did not sleep. She listened to the wind in the trees. She listened to the rustle of leaves. She listened to the small sounds of the earth. And in the morning, she saw a sign: a single white feather on the ground beside a small pool of water.

She returned to Nne-Ani and told her what she had seen. The priestess said: "The white feather is your Chi. The pool of water is your destiny. Your Chi has not turned its back on you. It is waiting for you to wash yourself in the pool of your own destiny. You must stop blaming the ancestors. You must stop begging. You must begin to live as if the child is already coming."

Onalu did as she was told. She stopped weeping. She stopped begging. She began to prepare—she wove baby clothes, she planted a garden, she sang songs of joy. And within a year, she bore a daughter, whom she named Chiamaka—"Chi is beautiful."

And Onalu taught her daughter: "The Chi does not speak in thunder. It speaks in the smallest signs. Listen. Wait. And then act as if your prayer is already answered."

The Lesson: The Chi does not abandon us; it waits for us to act. Stop begging the ancestors and start living your destiny, and the blessing will come.

7. The Chi That Chose Poverty

A man named Eze was born into a poor family. His father had been poor. His grandfather had been poor. The elders said that Eze's Chi had chosen poverty before he was born.

Eze did not accept this. He went to a dibia and asked: "Can I change my Chi? Can I choose a different destiny?"

The dibia shook his head. "Your Chi was chosen before you were born. It cannot be changed. But it can be worked with. Your Chi chose poverty because it knew what you could bear. If you honor it, if you make sacrifices to it, if you live in harmony with it, it may soften. But if you fight it, it will break you."

Eze listened. He began to make daily offerings to his Chi. He wore a ring of brass on his right hand to honor his Chi. He did not curse his poverty. He did not envy the rich. He simply worked.

And in time, his Chi softened. His yams grew larger. His barns filled. He was not the richest man in the village, but he was no longer the poorest.

The Lesson: You cannot choose your Chi, but you can learn to live with it. Destiny is not a prison; it is a conversation.

8. The Ikenga That Was Burned

A man named Nwosu had a powerful Ikenga. He had consecrated it with a yam, a cock, wine, and kola nuts. He had fed it daily with kola and wine. He had offered the blood of a cock before every important undertaking. And because of his Ikenga, he had prospered. His farm was the largest. His compound was the finest. His children were healthy and strong.

But Nwosu grew proud. He began to say: "My Ikenga is the reason for my success. Without it, I am nothing."

He stopped making sacrifices. He stopped feeding his Ikenga. He began to think that he could stand on his own.

His fortunes began to decline. His yams withered. His house leaked. His children fell sick.

The elders came to him and said: "Your Ikenga is not a god. It is a symbol of your right hand, your Chi, and your ancestors. If you neglect it, you neglect yourself."

But Nwosu did not listen. And when his fortune was completely destroyed, he burned his Ikenga. And with it, he burned what was left of his power.

The Lesson: The Ikenga is not a magic charm. It is a covenant. If you break the covenant, you lose the strength it represents.

9. The Dibia Who Saw the Future

A dibia named Akunna was known for his Afa divination. He could see things that others could not. He could read the patterns of the stones. He could hear the whispers of the ancestors.

One day, a young couple came to him. They were expecting their first child. They asked him to tell them about the child's future.

Akunna cast his Afa. He read the patterns. He closed his eyes and listened.

"Your child will be born on an Eke day," he said. "He will be a great man. But he will face a great danger in his youth. If he survives it, he will bring honor to your family. If he does not, you will have only his memory."

The couple was afraid. They asked what they could do.

"Make sacrifices to your Chi," Akunna said. "Make sacrifices to Ala, the earth goddess. Teach your child to honor the ancestors. And when the danger comes, it will not destroy him."

The child was born on an Eke day. And when the danger came—a terrible sickness—he survived. He grew to be a great man.

The Lesson: Divination does not just predict the future. It provides the tools to prepare for it.

10. The Oracle at Awka

The Agbala at Awka was one of the most feared oracles in Igboland. Its power came from the earth. It was called the oracle of the earth, because it was said to speak for Ala herself.

At the entrance to the Agbala was a large tree. Those who came seeking justice drove nails and iron staples into the tree, calling on the oracle to afflict their enemies. Each blow of the hammer was a curse.

The Agbala demanded sacrifices. It demanded money and gifts. The Osu-Agbala, the servant of the oracle, would burst from the bush with eagle feathers and short sticks, beating the petitioners until they made it worth his while to stop. The oracle itself would speak, commanding the people to advance no further lest they be swallowed up in the great water.

But the Agbala at Awka was eventually destroyed by the Government because of its bad influence. Its grove was desecrated. Its priests were hanged. The voice that had spoken for Ala was silenced.

The Lesson: Even the oracles of the earth are not eternal. When they become instruments of greed and oppression, they are destroyed.

11. The Woman Who Bargained with Her Chi

A woman named Nneka was barren. She had been married for ten years and had not conceived. Her husband's family mocked her. Her co-wives laughed at her. She was alone.

She went to a dibia and asked: "Why is my Chi so cruel? Why has it chosen barrenness for me?"

The dibia cast his stones. He was silent for a long time.

"Your Chi did not choose barrenness," he said. "Your Chi chose a child, but that child is waiting. You must bargain with your Chi. You must go to its shrine and speak to it."

Nneka went to the shrine of her Chi. She brought kola nut and palm wine. She poured a libation. She spoke.

"I know you are my personal god," she said. "I know you chose my destiny before I was born. But I am asking you now—please, let me have a child. I will honor you. I will make sacrifices to you. I will teach my child to honor you."

She wept. She poured more wine. She waited.

And in time, she conceived. She bore a son. She named him Chibueze—"Chi is the king." And she taught him to honor his mother's Chi, which had answered her prayer.

The Lesson: The Chi is not a distant, unfeeling force. It can be spoken to. It can be bargained with. It can be moved by a mother's tears.

12. The Kolanut and the Broken Family

Two brothers, Nnamdi and Okey, had not spoken in years. They had quarreled over a piece of land. The quarrel had become a fight. The fight had become a curse. And now they were enemies.

The elders of the village gathered them together. They brought a kolanut. The eldest man among them spoke to the ancestors.

"We are here to bring peace," he said. "We are here to mend what has been broken. We ask the ancestors to witness this kolanut. We ask them to bless this gathering. We ask them to restore what has been lost."

He broke the kolanut. The lobes fell in a pattern of unity. The elders looked at each other. They nodded.

"The ancestors have spoken," the eldest man said. "They want peace."

Nnamdi and Okey looked at each other. And for the first time in years, they smiled. They shared the kolanut. They shook hands. And the family was whole again.

The Lesson: The kolanut is not just for divination. It is for reconciliation. When the ancestors are invoked, the broken can be mended.

13. The Oracle of the Sun and Moon

In the ancient days, the Sun and the Moon were husband and wife. They lived together in the sky. They ruled the heavens together, and the earth below was fertile and happy.

But the Sun grew proud. He wanted to rule alone. He wanted to be the only light in the sky.

He went to the Oracle of the Sky, the great oracle that spoke for Chukwu himself. He asked the oracle to give him all the light, to take the Moon's light away.

The oracle heard him. And the oracle spoke.

"You ask for what cannot be given," it said. "The Moon's light is not hers. It is a reflection of yours. If you take her light, you will lose your own. You will burn too bright. You will burn everything you touch."

But the Sun did not listen. He took the Moon's light. He burned so bright that the earth began to scorch. The rivers dried up. The crops withered. The animals died.

Then the Sun understood. He had not gained power. He had lost it. And in his sorrow, he asked the oracle for help.

The oracle said: "The only way to save the earth is to give back the light. Let the Moon shine again. And let her shine from a distance, so that she cannot be taken for granted."

And so it was. The Sun gave back the Moon's light. And the Moon, once taken for granted, became the gentle guardian of the night.

The Lesson: Power taken by force is always a loss. The balance of the cosmos cannot be broken without consequence.

14. The Ofo Staff and the Broken Oath

There was once a man named Okoro who was known throughout the village for his honesty. He was a trader, and he never cheated. He was a neighbor, and he never stole. He was a kinsman, and he never betrayed. The elders said that Okoro's word was as solid as the iroko tree.

But Okoro had a secret. Years before, he had borrowed a large sum of money from his friend Nnamdi. He had promised to repay it within a year. But the year passed, and Okoro did not repay. Five years passed, and still he did not repay. Ten years passed, and Nnamdi died. Okoro thought that the debt had died with him.

But Nnamdi's son came to Okoro one day. "My father told me of the debt before he died," he said. "He said you were a good man, and that you would repay."

Okoro was ashamed. But he was also greedy. "I do not remember any debt," he said. "Your father must have been mistaken."

The son went to the elders. The elders summoned Okoro. They asked him to swear on the ofo—the staff of truth that stands for the ancestors. "If you did not borrow the money," they said, "then swear on the ofo, and you will be free."

Okoro looked at the ofo. It was a simple wooden staff, cut from the branch of the ofo tree. It was nothing to look at. But Okoro knew what it represented. It represented the ancestors. It represented the truth. It represented the one thing that could not be broken.

He reached for the ofo. He held it in his hand. And then, before he could speak, the staff grew hot in his hand. It burned like fire. Okoro screamed and dropped it. The elders looked at him. And Okoro confessed. He repaid the debt. He begged for forgiveness. And the son, who had his father's heart, forgave him.

The Lesson: The ofo cannot lie. Do not swear on it unless you are ready to face the truth.

15. The Shrine of the Ancestors

In every Igbo compound, there was a shrine to the ancestors. It was not a grand building. It was often just a small hut, or a corner of the obi, or a collection of carved posts. But it was the most important place in the compound. It was where the family spoke to the dead.

A man named Ekene built a new compound. He was a wealthy man, and his compound was the finest in the village. But he did not build a shrine. He said: "The ancestors are dead. What use is a shrine? I am alive. I will build for the living."

His neighbors warned him. They said: "The ancestors are not dead. They are the living-dead. They watch over us. If you do not honor them, they will turn their backs on you."

But Ekene laughed. He did not build a shrine. And then, one by one, misfortune began to visit his compound. His children fell sick. His yams withered. His wives quarreled. His barns were robbed. Nothing he did prospered.

Finally, Ekene went to a dibia. The dibia cast his stones and said: "The ancestors are angry. You have built a compound for the living, but you have forgotten the dead. Go home. Build a shrine. Make a sacrifice. And beg for their forgiveness."

Ekene went home. He built a shrine in the corner of his compound. He offered a white ram. He poured palm wine. He spoke to the ancestors: "I was wrong. I forgot you. Please forgive me. Please return to this compound."

And the ancestors returned. The children recovered. The yams grew. The wives made peace. The barns were full. And Ekene learned that the dead are not gone. They are simply in another room, waiting to be remembered.

The Lesson: The ancestors are the foundation of the family. Honor them, and they will bless you. Neglect them, and they will withdraw.

16. Onwuelo and the Ikenga of the Right Hand

There was once a young man named Onwuelo who was born with a withered left hand. His right hand was strong, but his left hand was weak and useless. The people of his village whispered that he was cursed, that his Chi had abandoned him, that he would never amount to anything.

But Onwuelo's father did not listen to the whispers. He took his son to the dibia and said: "Give him an Ikenga. Give him the shrine of the right hand. For it is the right hand that holds the machete, and the right hand that holds the hoe, and the right hand that holds the spear."

The dibia carved a small Ikenga from the wood of the iroko tree. He consecrated it with a white cock. He rubbed it with nzu—white chalk. He spoke to the ancestors and asked them to bless the boy's right hand.

Onwuelo grew. He honored his Ikenga every morning. He offered it palm wine. He greeted it with the words his father had taught him: "Right hand of my fathers, work with me today."

And the right hand of Onwuelo became famous throughout the land. He could split a log with a single stroke of his machete. He could dig more yam mounds in a day than any other man in the village. He could throw a spear farther than any warrior. His left hand was still useless. But his right hand was the strongest in the land.

When the great famine came, Onwuelo did not starve. His barn was full. His neighbors came to him and begged for food. He shared with them all. And when the famine ended, the elders gathered and said: "Onwuelo is not cursed. His Chi has blessed him. For the right hand is the hand of the ancestors, and the ancestors do not abandon those who honor them."

Onwuelo lived to a great age. His Ikenga was passed down to his eldest son. And when Onwuelo died, the elders said: "His left hand was weak. But his right hand was strong. And that is all that matters. For it is the right hand that builds, and the right hand that defends, and the right hand that blesses."

The Lesson: The right hand is the hand of the ancestors. Honor your Ikenga, and even your weakness will become your strength.

17. The Dibia Who Lost His Power

A dibia named Ogbuefi was the most powerful healer in the land. He could cast the stones and read the patterns. He could hear the voices of the ancestors. He could heal the sick and drive out evil spirits. People came to him from many villages. He was rich and respected and feared.

But Ogbuefi grew proud. He began to think that his power was his own. He began to charge exorbitant fees. He began to curse those who did not pay. He began to use his power for selfish ends.

The ancestors watched. They were not pleased. And one day, when Ogbuefi tried to cast his stones, nothing happened. The stones fell in meaningless patterns. The voices were silent. His power was gone.

Ogbuefi went to his shrine. He offered sacrifices. He poured libation. He begged the ancestors to return his power. But they did not answer.

He went to another dibia, a humble man who lived in a small village. The humble dibia cast his stones and said: "You have abused your gift. You have used it for greed, not for service. The ancestors have taken back what they gave you."

Ogbuefi wept. He had lost everything. His wealth. His reputation. His power. And from that day forward, he was no longer a dibia. He was just a man.

The Lesson: Power is a gift, not a possession. Use it for service, not for self. The ancestors give, and the ancestors take away.

18. The Sacred Python of Idemmili

The river goddess Idemmili had many servants. But her most sacred servant was the python. In the communities that worshipped Idemmili, the python was not a snake. It was a holy creature. It was the messenger of the goddess. To harm a python was to invite the wrath of the river.

There was once a young man named Uche who did not believe in the old ways. He had gone to the city and learned the white man's religion. He laughed at the stories of Idemmili. He said: "A snake is just a snake. It cannot harm me."

One day, Uche was walking by the river. He saw a python coiled on a rock. It was a large python, golden-brown, with eyes like dark stones. It did not move. It did not threaten him. But Uche, in his arrogance, picked up a stone and threw it at the python. He struck it on the head. The python did not attack. It simply slid into the water and disappeared.

That night, Uche fell ill. He burned with fever. He raved in his sleep. He saw visions of the river rising, of the python growing larger and larger, of the goddess Idemmili rising from the water with eyes of fire.

His family took him to a dibia. The dibia cast his stones and said: "You have offended the goddess. You have harmed her sacred servant. You must go to the river and make a sacrifice. You must beg for forgiveness."

Uche's family took him to the river. They sacrificed a white cock and a white goat. They poured palm wine into the water. And Uche, weak and trembling, confessed his sin. "I am sorry," he said. "I was wrong. I will honor the goddess."

The fever broke that night. Uche recovered. And from that day forward, he never again mocked the old ways. He taught his children to respect the python, and to honor the river.

The Lesson: The sacred is not to be mocked. The gods do not forget the harm done to their servants.

19. The Oracle at Umunoha

There was once an oracle at Umunoha that was famous throughout the land. It was called the Oracle of the Iron Gate, and it was said to speak with the voice of the ancestors themselves. People came from far and near to consult it. They came with questions about marriage, about children, about business, about war. And the oracle answered them all.

But the oracle at Umunoha had a secret. It was not a cave or a grove or a shrine. It was a gate—a gate made of iron, which stood at the edge of the village. When a petitioner came, they would stand before the gate and speak their question. And the gate would answer. The voice would come from nowhere and everywhere. It would echo through the village. It would silence the birds. It would make the children stop playing.

A man named Ndubuisi came to the gate one day. He was a wealthy man, and he wanted to know if his wealth would continue. He stood before the gate and asked: "Will my riches remain with me?"

The gate answered: "Your riches will remain as long as you are generous. But if you hoard them, they will be taken from you."

Ndubuisi did not listen. He hoarded his wealth. He gave nothing to the poor. He refused to help his kinsmen. And within a year, his fortune was gone. His storehouses were empty. His barns were bare. His children left him. He died a poor man.

And the gate at Umunoha was silent. It had spoken. It had warned. And the man had not listened.

The Lesson: The oracle speaks the truth, but it does not force you to obey. You are free to choose your destiny, even if you choose wrong.

20. The Ikenga and the Farmer's Right Hand

There was once a farmer named Emeka who had an Ikenga that was famous throughout the village. It was carved from the finest mahogany. It was polished with palm oil. It was adorned with cowries and feathers. And it had been consecrated by a great dibia, who had offered a white ram and a cock to the ancestors.

Emeka's Ikenga was not just a shrine. It was the source of his strength. Every morning, before he went to his farm, he would stand before it and ask for blessing. He would rub his right hand with nzu—white chalk—and touch the Ikenga. He would say: "I go to work. Work with me."

And his right hand never failed him. His yams were the largest in the village. His barns were full. His family never went hungry. The people said: "Emeka's right hand is blessed. His Ikenga is powerful."

But Emeka did not become proud. He continued to honor his Ikenga. He continued to make sacrifices. He continued to share his harvest with the poor. And when the elders asked him the secret of his success, he said: "It is not my hand. It is the hand of my Chi. It is the hand of my ancestors. It is the hand of my Ikenga. I am only the vessel."

The Lesson: The Ikenga is not a magic charm. It is a covenant with your Chi, your ancestors, and your right hand. Honor it, and it will honor you.

21. The Chi of the Twins

When twins were born in the old days, they were considered sacred. They were called ejima, and they were said to share a single Chi. The Chi of the twins was a special spirit, a spirit that brought blessing to the family and to the village.

There was once a woman named Nneoma who gave birth to twins—a boy and a girl. The village rejoiced. The elders said: "The Chi of these children is powerful. They will bring good fortune."

But Nneoma was afraid. She had heard the old stories. She knew that twins could also bring misfortune. She did not know what to do. She went to a dibia.

The dibia cast his stones and said: "Do not be afraid. The Chi of the twins is a blessing. But you must honor it. You must make sacrifices. You must teach the children to honor their Chi. If you do this, they will bring you joy."

Nneoma obeyed. She made the sacrifices. She taught her children the stories of the ancestors. She taught them to respect the Chi. And the twins grew strong and healthy. They brought joy to their family. The boy became a great warrior. The girl became a great healer. And the village prospered because of them.

The Lesson: The Chi is the spirit of destiny. When it is honored, it brings blessings. When it is neglected, it withdraws.

22. The Kolanut and the Wedding

No marriage in Igboland could be complete without the kolanut. Before the bride could be given to the groom, before the families could be joined, the kolanut had to be presented. It was the sign of welcome. It was the sign of acceptance. It was the sign of the ancestors' blessing.

A young man named Chidi came to the compound of his beloved, a woman named Adaeze. He brought yams and palm wine. He brought cloth and cowries. He brought the bride price, which had been agreed upon. But he did not bring kolanut.

Adaeze's father looked at Chidi. "Where is your kolanut?" he asked.

Chidi was confused. "I brought the bride price," he said. "I brought the yams. I brought the wine. What more do you need?"

Adaeze's father said: "The bride price is for the family. The yams are for the feast. The wine is for the celebration. But the kolanut is for the ancestors. Without the kolanut, the ancestors are not present. Without the ancestors, this marriage is not blessed."

Chidi understood. He went to the market and bought the finest kolanut he could find. He returned and presented it to Adaeze's father. The father blessed it. He broke it. He shared it with the ancestors. He shared it with the family. And then he said: "Now the marriage is blessed. Now the ancestors are present. Now you may take your bride."

And Chidi and Adaeze were married, with the blessing of the ancestors, because Chidi had brought the kolanut.

The Lesson: The kolanut is not just a nut. It is the presence of the ancestors. Without it, no gathering is complete. No marriage is blessed.

23. The Oracle That Refused a King

There was once a powerful king who ruled over many villages. He was feared for his armies and his wealth. He had conquered his enemies. He had taken their lands. He had made their people his slaves.

But the king was not content. He wanted to know what the ancestors thought of him. He wanted to know if his conquests were blessed. So he traveled to the great oracle at Nri—the oldest oracle in the land. He brought many gifts. He brought slaves. He brought gold. He came with a great procession, with drums and dancers and warriors.

But when he arrived at the oracle, the priestess said: "You cannot enter. The oracle will not see you."

The king was furious. "I am a king," he said. "I have brought gifts. I have brought slaves. Who are you to refuse me?"

The priestess said: "The oracle does not care about your gifts. It does not care about your slaves. It does not care that you are a king. The oracle sees the heart. And your heart is full of blood. You have killed innocent people. You have stolen their lands. You have enslaved their children. The oracle will not speak to you until you have repented."

The king was stunned. He had never been spoken to in this way. But he was also afraid. He knew that the oracle spoke for the ancestors. He knew that the oracle could not lie. He went away, humbled.

And from that day forward, he changed his ways. He freed his slaves. He returned the lands he had stolen. He made peace with his enemies. And then, when he had repented, the oracle spoke to him. It said: "You have done well. Now you may ask your question."

The Lesson: The oracle does not respect titles or wealth. It respects the truth. It respects justice. It respects the heart.

24. The Woman Who Was Married to a Spirit

In the old days, it was said that sometimes a woman would be married to a spirit. The marriage would happen in the spirit world, and the woman would not know it. She would simply find that no human suitor could satisfy her. She would find that she was always longing for something she could not name. And then, she would go to a dibia, and the dibia would tell her the truth.

A woman named Obiageli had this problem. She was beautiful and kind. She had many suitors. But none of them pleased her. One was too tall. One was too short. One was too poor. One was too rich. She rejected them all.

Her parents were confused. They went to a dibia. The dibia cast his stones and said: "Obiageli is married to a spirit. She has a husband in the spirit world. He is the one she longs for. No human man can compare."

Obiageli's parents were afraid. "What can we do?" they asked.

The dibia said: "You must break the spirit marriage. You must make a sacrifice. You must appeal to the spirit husband and ask him to release her."

The sacrifice was made. The spirit husband was appeased. He released Obiageli from the marriage. And from that day forward, Obiageli was able to see human men as they were. She chose a good man—a farmer who loved her—and she married him. She lived a long and happy life, free from the spirit husband who had held her captive.

The Lesson: The spirit world and the human world are not separate. They interact in ways we cannot always see. But with the help of the dibia, the bonds of the spirit can be broken.

25. The Nzu That Revealed the Truth

There was once a dispute between two men, Okeke and Nwosu. Okeke said that Nwosu had stolen his goat. Nwosu said that he had bought the goat from a trader. Neither could prove his case. The elders were confused.

They decided to use nzu—white chalk. They brought a bowl of nzu and a white cloth. They told the two men to mark themselves with the nzu. Okeke marked himself. Nwosu marked himself.

Then the elders said: "Now swear on the nzu. Swear on the ancestors. Swear on the earth. If you are telling the truth, the nzu will remain white. If you are lying, the nzu will turn black."

Okeke swore. His nzu remained white. Nwosu swore. His nzu began to darken. It turned from white to grey, from grey to black. The people gasped. Nwosu tried to wipe it off. But the nzu would not come off. It had become a part of his skin.

Nwosu confessed. He had stolen the goat. He returned it. He made sacrifices to Ala, the earth goddess, and to the ancestors. And from that day forward, no one in the village dared to swear a false oath on the nzu.

The Lesson: The nzu is the symbol of truth. It cannot be worn by a liar. When the ancestors are called, the truth will always come to light.

26. The Oracle of the Forest

In the deep forest, far from the villages, there was an oracle that few knew about. It was not a grand shrine or a famous cave. It was a tree—a great iroko tree, ancient and vast, whose roots spread through the earth and whose branches touched the sky. And it spoke. Not in words, but in signs. The way the leaves moved. The way the branches swayed. The way the birds nested in its crown. Those who knew how to read the signs could ask the tree a question and receive an answer.

A hunter named Ozo was lost in the forest. He had been hunting for many days and had found nothing. He was hungry and tired and afraid. He came upon the great iroko tree and, in desperation, he asked: "Where can I find food?"

The tree did not speak. But the leaves rustled. The branches swayed to the east. Ozo followed the direction of the branches. He walked for an hour. And there, in a clearing, he found a herd of antelopes. He killed one. He had food.

Ozo returned to the tree. He offered a sacrifice of the antelope's meat. He poured libation. He thanked the tree. And from that day forward, he told everyone about the oracle of the forest. But few believed him. They said: "A tree is a tree. It cannot speak."

But Ozo knew. He had heard the tree. He had been saved by the tree. And he never forgot.

The Lesson: The spirit world is everywhere. It speaks in the wind, in the trees, in the rivers, in the stones. Those who listen will hear.

27. The Chi of the Dying Man

There was once a man named Nnamdi who was very old. He had lived a long life. He had married. He had raised children. He had taken titles. He had seen his grandchildren and his great-grandchildren. And now, he was ready to die.

But Nnamdi was afraid. He had heard the stories of the spirit world. He had heard of the mmụọ, the spirits of the dead, who wander between the worlds. He was afraid that his Chi would not accept him. He was afraid that his ancestors would turn their backs on him. He was afraid that he had not lived a good life.

He called his eldest son to him. He said: "Go to the dibia. Ask him to tell me what my Chi says. Ask him if I am ready to die."

The son went to the dibia. The dibia cast his stones. He read the patterns. He was silent for a long time.

"Your father's Chi is pleased," the dibia said. "Your father has lived a good life. He has honored the ancestors. He has kept the laws. He has been kind to the poor. He has been generous to his family. His Chi will welcome him. His ancestors will receive him."

The son returned and told his father. Nnamdi wept with relief. He called his family together. He blessed them. He told them the stories of the ancestors. He told them the stories of the Chi. And then, peacefully, he died.

The Lesson: The Chi is with us from birth to death. If we live in harmony with our Chi, if we honor the ancestors, if we keep the laws of the land, then death is not an end. It is a return.

28. The Restoration of the Ancestral Shrine

After the Nigerian Civil War, many Igbo families returned to their villages to find that their ancestral shrines had been destroyed. The churches had grown, and the old ways had been abandoned. Many people did not know how to honor the ancestors anymore. They did not know how to break the kolanut. They did not know how to cast the stones. They did not know how to pour libation. The ancestral traditions were fading.

An old man named Chukwuka saw this. He was one of the last of the old dibias. He had survived the war, but his shrine had been burned. He had lost his ofo. He had lost his ikenga. He had lost everything. But he had not lost his knowledge.

He gathered the young people of the village. He said: "You have forgotten the ancestors. You have forgotten the Chi. You have forgotten the Ikenga. But the ancestors have not forgotten you. They are waiting. They are calling. You must restore the shrine."

The young people listened. They were confused. They did not know how. But Chukwuka taught them. He taught them how to cut the ofo. He taught them how to carve the ikenga. He taught them how to break the kolanut. He taught them how to cast the stones. He taught them how to speak to the ancestors.

And slowly, the shrine was restored. The ancestors were honored. The Chi was remembered. The Ikenga was blessed. And the village began to heal. The young people learned the stories. The old ways returned. And Chukwuka, before he died, said: "The ancestors are not in the past. They are in the present. As long as we remember them, they are with us."

The Lesson: Tradition is not a relic of the past. It is a living thing. It must be tended, restored, and passed on. As long as we remember the ancestors, they are with us.
