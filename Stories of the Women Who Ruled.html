Stories of the Women Who Ruled

1. STORY ONE: The Omu, Mother of Society

In the Igbo communities west of the Niger, in the area called Anioma, there was an institution older than memory: the Omu. She was not a queen consort. She was not the wife of a king. She was the "mother of society," a woman chosen by the oracle, installed with the Obi title, and given authority over the marketplace and the spiritual welfare of the town .

The Omu dressed like a king. She had her own palace. She sat with the male elders and chiefs in council, for her installation qualified her to take the Obi title and belong to their rank . But her power was not the same as theirs. Her power was over the women. Her power was over the market. Her power was over the rituals that kept the community in balance with the spirits.

The Omu Nwagboka of Onitsha was a signatory to an 1884 treaty with the British—a woman whose signature bound her people to an agreement with a foreign empire . She was not a figurehead. She was a ruler.

The Lesson: Women's power in Igbo society was not borrowed from men. It was a separate domain, with its own authority, its own rituals, and its own voice.

2. STORY TWO: The Omu Who Opened the Market

In Asaba, the market could not open until the Omu arrived. She would come to the marketplace with her calabash. She would take her seat. And only then, after she had declared the market open, could trading begin. If any woman sat down before her, the Omu could seize that woman's calabash or fine her .

The Omu did not just control trade. She controlled the conduct of women. Husbands brought complaints about their wives to her. She settled marital disputes. She punished women for non-attendance at the market, for going to other markets in other towns. She and her cabinet made medicines to protect the town from disease. If sickness was in a nearby town, they would place charms at the entrance to their own town to keep it out .

When there was drought and sickness and people were dying, the Omu and her cabinet would walk naked in the night with live brands to curse whoever had brought the disease. These were not empty rituals. They were acts of spiritual warfare, performed by women, for the protection of the community .

The Lesson: The Omu's power was economic, judicial, and spiritual. She was the guardian of the market, the regulator of women's conduct, and the protector of the town's health.

3. STORY THREE: The Umuada, Daughters of the Lineage

In every Igbo village, there was another source of women's power: the Umuada. They were the daughters of the lineage—women who had been born into the family, whether they still lived there or had married into other villages. Their authority was unique. They were not wives. They were not outsiders. They were blood .

The Umuada had the power to intervene in the affairs of their natal village. If a man mistreated his wife, the Umuada could summon him. If there was a dispute between families, the Umuada could mediate. If the male elders made a decision that the women opposed, the Umuada could organize collective action to resist it .

The senior Ada, the oldest daughter of the lineage, represented the women of her natal village in the Omu's palace. The Omu and the Umuada discussed matters of public importance. They discussed affairs concerning the women in the town. They worked together, a dual authority of women, one elected and one inherited .

The Lesson: The Umuada were the conscience of the lineage. Their power came not from marriage or wealth, but from blood. They were the daughters who never left, even when they left.

4. STORY FOUR: The Isi Ada, the Oldest Daughter

In many Igbo communities, the Isi Ada—the oldest daughter of a lineage—held a place of honor and authority. She was not just the firstborn female. She was a political and judicial figure. Her reports to the women of her group could lead to collective action opposing the decisions of the male political leaders .

The Isi Ada was the voice of the daughters. When the men made decisions that affected the women, the Isi Ada was the one who spoke. When the women needed representation, the Isi Ada was the one who stood before the elders. She was a bridge between the male and female spheres, a woman with a seat at the table because of her birth, not her marriage.

The Lesson: In Igbo society, authority was not only male. The oldest daughter held a power that was hers by right of birth.

5. STORY FIVE: The Women Who Fought Alongside Men

The oral histories of the Igbo are filled with stories of women who went to war. There was a queen who defied the odds to search for her husband in battle, entering enemy territory to rescue him. There was a mother who composed a famous battle song to protect her only son from going to war .

These were not fairy tales. They were heroic legends, passed down through generations, telling of women who held the fates of their husbands and children in their hands. They were activists, supernatural supporters, strategists. They were not passive observers of war. They were participants.

The Lesson: The Igbo woman was not confined to the compound. When the community was threatened, she could take up the fight alongside the men.

6. STORY SIX: The Ahebi Ugbabe, the Female King

Ahebi Ugbabe was born around 1880 in Enugu-Ezike. She was exiled from her community as a young woman. She became a prostitute. She traveled widely. She learned to speak many languages. She became a close companion of the Igala kings and the British officers .

And then she returned home. And she became a man.

She was appointed a warrant chief. And then she became a king—the Eze. She took the title of King Ahebi. She married many wives, as a man would. She sat in judgment over cases. She ruled her community with an iron will .

The British supported her claim because she was useful to them. The Igala kings supported her because she was their companion. But her own people, the men of her community, resented her. They said she was "governing too much." She faced constant opposition and conflict. But she never bowed .

The Lesson: A woman could become a king in colonial Nigeria. The sex and gender did not coincide in northern Igboland. Women could occupy positions that in other societies were exclusively male .

7. STORY SEVEN: The Women's War of 1929

In 1929, the British colonial administration began to count women, children, and livestock—a prelude to taxing women, a thing unheard of in Igbo custom. The women of Oloko knew what this meant. It was not just about money. It was about their independence, their dignity, and their place in the world.

The protest began in Oloko and spread like wildfire. Tens of thousands of women rose up. They danced, they sang, and they marched. They demanded the removal of the warrant chiefs. They destroyed the Native Courts and the European factories that symbolized their oppression .

The British fired on the women. In Aba, in Abak, in Utu Etim, women were killed. The police fired, causing eighteen casualties, all of them women . But the movement did not stop.

The Women's War, known in Igbo as Ogu Umunwanyi, forced the British to abandon the census and the taxation of women. It led to the end of the warrant chief position in British colonial Africa . The women had won.

The Lesson: When women unite, they possess a power that no empire can suppress. The Women's War was the first time that women in Nigeria organized on such a massive scale to confront colonial power.

8. STORY EIGHT: Madam Nwanyerewa, the Voice of the Revolt

Among the women who led the Women's War was Madam Nwanyerewa of Oloko, an ancient town in what is now Abia State. She played an important role not only in triggering the revolt but also in gaining recognition for her articulate testimony and intelligence .

She was not a queen. She was not a chief. She was a woman who spoke the truth and refused to be silenced. Her words, her courage, and her intelligence made her a leader. The colonial governor-general of Nigeria, Lord Frederick Lugard, was forced to observe of the Igbo woman: "She is ambitious, self-reliant, hard-working, and independent... she claims full equality with the opposite sex and would seem indeed to be the dominant partner" .

The Lesson: Leadership does not always come from titles. Sometimes it comes from the courage to speak.

9. STORY NINE: The Women Who Held the Economy

Before 1900, Igbo women were the backbone of the economy. They were active in agricultural cultivation and food processing. They manufactured and marketed palm oil and cassava. They produced salt, pottery, cloth, and mats. They dominated trade and commerce in the Igbo region .

Sylvia Leith-Ross, a British observer, was so impressed with the role women played in the Igbo economy that she concluded: "their women, because of their economic importance both as mothers, farm cultivators and traders, have rather more power than is generally thought" .

The market was a woman's domain. The Omu controlled it. The women traded in it. The wealth of the community flowed through their hands. And because they held the economy, they held power.

The Lesson: Economic power is political power. The Igbo woman's control of the market gave her a voice that could not be ignored.

10. STORY TEN: The Otu Omu, the Women's Cabinet

The Omu did not rule alone. She had a cabinet—the Otu Omu—a council of women who advised her, supported her, and helped her govern. Together, they decided on matters concerning theft, adultery, and other violations of traditional laws and customs in the market. They settled complicated marital conflicts. They maintained the traditional religion through public enlightenment campaigns .

The Otu Omu were the spiritual and political heart of the women's constituency. They performed rituals. They made medicines. They acted as spies for the town, especially in the area of security. They called the Umuada and the Inyemedi to order if there was any problem between them .

The Lesson: Women's power was not solitary. It was collective. The Omu was the head, but the Otu Omu was the body.

11. STORY ELEVEN: The Dual-Sex System

The Igbo society was not a patriarchy in the simple sense. It was a dual-sex system. There was a male sphere and a female sphere, each with its own authority, its own institutions, and its own leaders .

The male sphere had the Obi, the Eze, the Umunna. The female sphere had the Omu, the Umuada, the Otu Omu. Each was sovereign in its own domain. The men did not rule over the women. They ruled alongside them.

The British did not understand this. They saw only the male chiefs. They appointed warrant chiefs—all men. They ignored the Omu. They ignored the Umuada. They ignored the women's institutions. And in doing so, they disrupted the balance .

The Lesson: Colonialism did not just take land and resources. It took women's power. The dual-sex system was dismantled, and women were pushed out of the political sphere they had once occupied.

12. STORY TWELVE: The Mkpuke and the Obi

Ife Amadiume, a scholar of Igbo society, described the structure of the Igbo world as a balance between the Mkpuke and the Obi. The Obi is the male-focused house. The Mkpuke is the female-focused, matricentric unit .

These two units are not enemies. They are partners. The Mkpuke brings compassion, love, peace, and the spirit of common motherhood. The Obi brings competitiveness, valour, force, and the spirit of the warrior. Each is necessary. Each balances the other .

The Igbo world is not a world of male dominance. It is a world of balance. The feminine principle is not subordinate. It is equal. It is essential.

The Lesson: The Igbo cosmos is centered around women. The earth goddess Ala is the moral authority. The Omu is the mother of society. The Umuada are the conscience of the lineage. The feminine is not secondary. It is foundational .

13. STORY THIRTEEN: The Women Who Survived the War

When the Nigerian Civil War came, and Biafra was blockaded and bombed, it was the women who held the communities together. They took on the role of primary caregivers. They ensured that children and older people received what little food and medical care were available. They organized local food-sharing systems. They secretly preserved crops. They improvised nutritional supplements to save children suffering from malnutrition .

The writer Flora Nwapa documented their experiences in stories like "Wives at War" and "Daddy Don't Strike the Match." She wrote of the risks women faced in the "attack trade"—the dangerous journeys to find food—and the persistent efforts to ensure families' survival amid dangerous conditions. Women managed the evacuation of civilians whenever Nigerian forces threatened to take over a town .

Far from being passive victims, Igbo women stepped into roles of survival, care, and leadership. They ensured the continuity of their families and communities in the face of destruction.

The Lesson: The power of Igbo women is not just in the past. It is in the present. When the world falls apart, it is the women who hold it together.

14. STORY FOURTEEN: Moremi Ajasoro, the Woman Who Saved Her People

Long before the British came, in the city of Ile-Ife, there lived a woman of extraordinary courage named Moremi Ajasoro. She was the wife of the King, Oranmiyan, and she was beloved by all for her beauty and her wisdom.

But Ile-Ife was at war. A mysterious people called the Igbo—raiders from the east who dressed in strange garments and covered their bodies with raffia—came every year to raid the city. They could not be defeated. They seemed to appear from nowhere and disappear into the forest. The people of Ife were helpless.

Moremi went to the river goddess, Esinmirin, and offered a sacrifice. She said: "Give me the power to save my people, and I will give you anything you ask."

The goddess heard her. She gave Moremi the power to be captured by the Igbo raiders. Moremi allowed herself to be taken. She lived among the Igbo for three years. She learned their secrets. She discovered that they were not spirits at all, but men—men who wore raffia costumes to frighten their enemies. She discovered the secret of their fire, which they used to burn the towns they raided.

When she escaped and returned to Ile-Ife, she told her people everything. The people of Ife prepared. When the Igbo raiders came again, they were ready. They used torches to burn the raffia costumes, and the raiders were defeated. Ile-Ife was saved.

Moremi returned to the river goddess to keep her promise. "What do you want?" she asked. The goddess said: "I want your only son." Moremi wept. But she gave her son, Oluorogbo, as a sacrifice. And the goddess blessed her, and her name was remembered forever.

The Lesson: A woman's courage can save a nation. The greatest sacrifice is the one made for the sake of the people.

15. STORY FIFTEEN: Queen Amina of Zazzau

In the 16th century, in the land of Zazzau—what is now Zaria—there lived a queen whose name was Amina. She was the daughter of Queen Bakwa Turunku. She was raised among the warriors of her people, and she learned the arts of war before she learned the arts of the palace.

When her mother died, Amina's brother became king. But he was weak. He did not expand the kingdom. He did not protect the trade routes. Amina watched, and she waited.

When her brother died, Amina became queen. And she became a warrior. She led her armies into battle. She conquered lands from the Niger to the Benue. She opened the trade routes. She built walls around the cities she conquered, and those walls are called ganuwar Amina—the walls of Amina—to this day.

Amina never married. She took lovers from among the defeated kings, and when she was finished with them, she sent them away. She was the queen who ruled by the sword and by wisdom. She was the woman who ruled like a man, and better than most.

She ruled for thirty-four years. And when she died, the people of Zazzau mourned her as the greatest ruler they had ever known.

The Lesson: A woman can be a warrior. A woman can be a conqueror. A woman can rule with the sword and the wisdom of the ancestors.

16. STORY SIXTEEN: The Queen of Nri and the Sacred Ofo

The Kingdom of Nri was the oldest and most sacred of all Igbo kingdoms. It was ruled by priest-kings called the Eze Nri. But the Eze Nri did not rule alone. There was another power in Nri—the Queen, the Omu Nri.

The Omu Nri was not a queen consort. She was a ruler in her own right. She was the keeper of the sacred ofo—the staff of truth that bound the living to the ancestors. She was the guardian of the spiritual welfare of the kingdom. She sat in council with the Eze Nri, and her voice was equal to his.

There was once an Eze Nri named Eze Nrijiofor who wished to rule without the Omu. He said: "I am the king. I do not need a woman to tell me what to do." He dismissed the Omu and ruled alone.

That year, the crops failed. The rains did not come. The people fell sick. The ancestors were angry. The elders went to the Eze Nri and said: "You have broken the balance. The Omu is the mother of the kingdom. Without her, the kingdom cannot stand."

The Eze Nri repented. He called the Omu back. He restored her to her place. And the rains returned, and the crops grew, and the people were healed. And from that day forward, no Eze Nri ever ruled without the Omu.

The Lesson: The balance between male and female power is sacred. When one is suppressed, the whole kingdom suffers.

17. STORY SEVENTEEN: The Mother of the Masquerade

In the beginning, the masquerades were not men. They were women. The first masquerade was the spirit of the earth, and it appeared to a woman named Nneka, a priestess of Ala. The earth goddess said: "You will be my voice among the people. You will wear this mask and speak my words."

Nneka became the first masquerade. She wore the mask of the earth. She spoke the words of the goddess. The people listened to her, and they obeyed.

But as time passed, the men grew jealous. They wanted the power of the masquerade for themselves. They went to Nneka and said: "You are a woman. The masquerade is for men."

Nneka refused to give up her mask. She said: "The goddess gave this to me. I will not surrender it."

The men went to Ala and said: "The masquerade is too powerful for a woman. Give it to us." Ala listened. She was silent for a long time. And then she said: "The masquerade is my voice. It can be carried by anyone who honors me. But if the men take it, they must remember that the masquerade is sacred. They must not use it for greed or for pride. They must use it for the community."

And so the masquerade passed to the men. But the women remembered. They remembered that the first masquerade was a woman. They remembered that the spirit of the masquerade is feminine—it is the spirit of the earth, of the mother, of Ala herself. And so, even today, the women do not touch the masquerade, but they know its secrets. They know that the power behind the mask is the power of the mother.

The Lesson: The feminine principle is the foundation of the sacred. Even when the visible power passes to men, the invisible power remains with women.

18. STORY EIGHTEEN: Nneka, the Priestess of the Oracle

Nneka was a woman of extraordinary spiritual power. She was a priestess of the oracle, chosen by the goddess to speak for the spirit world. She lived in a cave at the edge of the village, and the people came to her for guidance.

But Nneka was not just a priestess. She was a ruler. The people listened to her. They obeyed her. They honored her. And the male elders grew jealous. They said: "A woman cannot hold such power. The oracle should be served by men."

Nneka said: "The oracle chooses whom it will. I did not seek this power. It was given to me."

The elders went to the oracle and asked: "Should Nneka be removed?" The oracle spoke: "Nneka is my voice. She is my daughter. She is the mother of this village. If you remove her, you remove my blessing."

The elders were afraid. They did not remove Nneka. And Nneka served the oracle for the rest of her life. She healed the sick. She settled disputes. She brought peace to families that had been broken. And when she died, the people of the village wept for a year, for they had lost a mother.

The Lesson: Spiritual power is not gendered. The oracle chooses whom it will, and women have always been the vessels of the divine.

19. STORY NINETEEN: The Women Who Built the Markets

In the old days, the markets were not built by men. They were built by women. The Omu chose the site. The women cleared the ground. The women built the stalls. The women made the laws of the market.

The market was the heart of the community. It was where the people came to trade, to meet, to talk, to arrange marriages, to settle disputes. It was where the ancestors were remembered, for the market day was a sacred day, given by Chukwu himself.

A woman named Umuada was the founder of the great market at Nkwo. She was a woman of vision. She saw that the people needed a place to gather. She saw that the women needed a place to trade. She cleared the ground with her own hands. She built the first stall. She called the people to come.

And the market at Nkwo became the greatest market in the land. People came from far and near. They traded. They celebrated. They remembered. And they honored Umuada, the woman who built the market.

The Lesson: The market is a woman's creation. The wealth of the community flows through the hands of women. And the women who build markets build communities.

20. STORY TWENTY: The Daughter Who Became the Son

In some Igbo communities, there was a tradition called Nwanyi bu Nwoke—"woman who is man." This was a woman who took on the role of a man. She married a wife. She raised children. She inherited the family title. She sat in the council of men.

A woman named Nwafor was the only child of her father. When her father died, there was no son to inherit his title. The elders said: "The lineage will end. The ancestors will be forgotten."

But Nwafor said: "I am my father's child. I will carry his name. I will inherit his title. I will be his son."

The elders were astonished. But Nwafor was determined. She went to the shrine of the ancestors. She made a sacrifice. She spoke to her father's spirit. And her father's spirit accepted her as his heir.

Nwafor became the son her father had never had. She married a wife. She raised children. She took the Ozo title. She sat in the council of elders. And when she died, she was buried with the honors of a man.

The Lesson: Gender in Igbo society was not fixed. A woman could become a man if she had the courage and the calling. The spirit of the lineage is more important than the body that carries it.

21. STORY TWENTY-ONE: The Queen Mother of Onitsha

In Onitsha, the Obi ruled. But beside the Obi was the Omu, the Queen Mother. She was not the Obi's wife. She was a woman chosen by the oracle, installed with the Obi title, and given authority over the market and the women.

The Omu of Onitsha had her own palace. She had her own staff. She had her own council of women. She sat with the Obi in council, and her voice was heard. When the Obi died, the Omu played a role in selecting his successor.

A woman named Nwagboka was one of the greatest Omu of Onitsha. She was a signatory to an 1884 treaty with the British. She was a woman who stood before the colonial governor and spoke for her people. She was a woman who refused to be silenced.

When the British tried to ignore the Omu and deal only with the male chiefs, Nwagboka protested. She said: "I am the mother of this town. I am the guardian of the market. You cannot speak for my people without speaking to me."

And the British were forced to acknowledge her. They included her name in the treaty. They recognized her authority. And Nwagboka became a symbol of the power of the Igbo woman—the power that no colonial administration could erase.

The Lesson: The Omu is the mother of the town. Her power is not borrowed. It is hers by right. And she will not be silenced.

22. STORY TWENTY-TWO: The Women Who Buried the Dead

In the Igbo tradition, when a person died, the burial rites were the responsibility of the family. But in some communities, there were women who were specially trained to prepare the body, to perform the rites, to guide the spirit to the land of the ancestors.

These women were called Ndi Ncha—the ones who cleanse. They were not mourners. They were not passive. They were the midwives of death. They prepared the body. They washed it. They wrapped it in the finest cloth. They spoke the words that would guide the spirit to the spirit world.

A woman named Oduenyi was one of these women. She had performed the rites for hundreds of people. She knew the songs. She knew the prayers. She knew the paths of the spirit world.

When her own mother died, Oduenyi performed the rites herself. She washed her mother's body. She wrapped her in the finest cloth. She sang the songs. She spoke the words. And then she said: "Go, mother. Go to the ancestors. Go to the spirit world. We will follow you when our time comes."

And the spirit of her mother departed in peace.

The Lesson: Women are the keepers of the passage. They bring life into the world, and they guide it out. The power of the feminine is the power of transformation.

23. STORY TWENTY-THREE: The Daughter of the King Who Refused to Marry

There was once a king who had three daughters. The eldest was beautiful. The second was clever. The third was wise. When they came of age, the king said: "I will give you in marriage to the men who can bring me the greatest gifts."

The eldest married a man who brought her gold. The second married a man who brought her jewels. The third said: "I do not wish to marry. I wish to rule."

The king was furious. "A woman cannot rule. You must marry."

But the third daughter said: "Why not? The Omu rules. The Umuada rules. The women of this land have always ruled. Why can I not rule?"

The king was silenced. He had no answer. And so he gave his third daughter a portion of his kingdom. He said: "Rule well. Prove that a woman can be a king."

The daughter ruled with wisdom and justice. She built markets. She settled disputes. She protected the poor. And when the king died, the people chose her to be their ruler. She became the first Queen of the land.

The Lesson: A woman can rule. A woman can lead. The only thing that stands in her way is the doubt of those who do not believe.

24. STORY TWENTY-FOUR: The Woman Who Spoke for the Earth

Ala is the earth goddess. She is the moral authority of the Igbo world. But Ala does not speak directly to the people. She speaks through her priestesses. She speaks through women.

A woman named Nne Ala was the chief priestess of Ala in her village. She was the voice of the earth. When the people did something wrong, she would speak. When the land was polluted, she would speak. When the ancestors were angry, she would speak.

One year, the men of the village decided to sell sacred land to a stranger. Nne Ala spoke: "You cannot sell the land. The land belongs to Ala. It belongs to the ancestors. If you sell it, the earth will turn against you."

The men laughed. They said: "A woman cannot tell us what to do with our land."

They sold the land. That year, the crops failed. The rains stopped. The people fell sick. The elders went to Nne Ala and said: "We have done wrong. What can we do?"

Nne Ala said: "Go to the stranger. Buy back the land. Make a sacrifice to Ala. Beg for forgiveness."

The men did as she said. They bought back the land. They made the sacrifice. The rains returned. The crops grew. And from that day forward, the men of the village never made a decision about the land without consulting Nne Ala.

The Lesson: The earth speaks through women. Those who do not listen to the mothers will bring destruction upon themselves.

25. STORY TWENTY-FIVE: The Women Who Kept the Records

In the days before writing, the Igbo people kept their history in their memories. And it was often the women who kept the records. They remembered the genealogies. They remembered the stories. They remembered the laws. They remembered the songs.

A woman named Ada was the keeper of the history of her lineage. She could recite the names of her ancestors back to the founding father. She could tell the stories of every marriage, every birth, every death. She could sing the songs of every festival.

When the elders needed to know something about the past, they went to Ada. When there was a dispute about land, they went to Ada. When they wanted to honor the ancestors, they went to Ada.

Ada was not a chief. She was not a priestess. She was a woman with a good memory. But her memory was the memory of the community. Without her, the community would have forgotten itself.

The Lesson: Memory is a sacred duty. Those who keep the records keep the community alive.

26. STORY TWENTY-SIX: The Woman Who Taught the Children

In the Igbo tradition, the education of children was the responsibility of the community. But it was the women who did most of the teaching. They taught the children the language. They taught them the customs. They taught them the stories. They taught them the laws.

A woman named Oma was known throughout the village for her wisdom. She had no children of her own. But she taught every child in the village. She told them the stories of the ancestors. She taught them the songs. She taught them the proverbs. She taught them what it meant to be Igbo.

When Oma died, the whole village mourned. They said: "Oma was our mother. She taught us everything we know. Without her, we would not be Igbo."

And the children she had taught grew up and taught their own children. And the knowledge she had given them passed from generation to generation.

The Lesson: The teacher is the mother of the community. Those who teach the children shape the future.

27. STORY TWENTY-SEVEN: The Women Who Negotiated Peace

When there was war between villages, it was often the women who brought peace. They would go to the battlefield. They would stand between the warriors. They would raise their hands. They would speak the words of peace.

A woman named Nneoma was famous for her peacemaking. She had stopped three wars. She had brought peace to five villages. She had saved thousands of lives.

Once, two villages were about to go to war over a piece of land. The warriors were ready. The drums were beating. The women went to Nneoma and said: "You must stop this war."

Nneoma went to the battlefield. She stood between the armies. She raised her hands. She spoke: "This land is not worth your lives. These men are your brothers. These women are your sisters. Put down your weapons. Let us talk."

The warriors lowered their weapons. They listened. They talked. They made peace. And the land was divided fairly, and no one died.

Nneoma was honored. The two villages gave her a title. They said: "Nneoma is the mother of peace. Without her, we would have destroyed each other."

The Lesson: Women are the peacemakers. When men want war, women bring peace.

28. STORY TWENTY-EIGHT: The Women Who Will Always Rule

In every generation, there are women who rule. They are the Omu. They are the Umuada. They are the Isi Ada. They are the priestesses. They are the healers. They are the teachers. They are the peacemakers.

They do not always sit on thrones. They do not always wear crowns. But they rule. They rule in the market. They rule in the home. They rule in the shrine. They rule in the hearts of the people.

The Igbo say: Nneka bu ihe—"Mother is supreme." This is not a sentiment. It is a truth. The mother is the foundation of the family. The mother is the foundation of the community. The mother is the foundation of the nation.

And so the women of the Igbo will always rule. They will rule in the market. They will rule in the council. They will rule in the spirit world. They will rule because they are the mothers. They will rule because they are the daughters. They will rule because they are the keepers of the covenant.

As long as the earth remains, the women will rule. As long as the ancestors are remembered, the women will rule. As long as the stories are told, the women will rule.

The Lesson: The power of the Igbo woman is eternal. It is the power of the earth, the power of the ancestors, the power of life itself. It cannot be taken away. It cannot be forgotten. It will always be.

These are the stories of the women who ruled. The Omu who opened the market. The Umuada who spoke for the lineage. The Ahebi who became king. The Nwanyerewa who led a revolt. The women who traded and farmed and fought and survived. Their power was not given to them. It was theirs by right. And as long as the Igbo people remember, it will never be forgotten.
