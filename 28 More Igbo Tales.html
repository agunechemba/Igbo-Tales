📖 Twenty-Eight More Igbo Tales

1. The Tortoise and the Boar's Debt

The Tortoise, ever the schemer, borrowed a substantial sum of money from the Boar. He promised to repay the debt on an agreed date, but as the day approached, the Tortoise conveniently "forgot." The Boar, known for his impatience and quick temper, grew tired of waiting. One day, he confronted the Tortoise and, in his anger, seized the Tortoise's wife's precious grinding stone and threw it into the river.

The Tortoise, seeing an opportunity, went to the King. He wept and claimed that the stone the Boar had thrown away was not just any stone, but contained the very money he had intended to repay. The Boar, horrified at the accusation, was forced to search the riverbed in vain. This is why, even today, the pig can be seen constantly nosing around on the ground, still searching for the lost grinding stone. The Lesson: It is not the same humility used in borrowing money that is used in repaying it.

2. The Lizard's Judgement

The Lizard and the Wall Gecko were once the best of friends. The Lizard was honest and hardworking, while the Gecko was lazy and deceitful. One day, the Gecko tricked some lepers into giving him all their food, promising them a cure he did not possess. The Lizard, discovering this, went to the lepers and confessed his friend's crime. The Earth Goddess, Ala, angered by the Gecko's cruelty, struck him with leprosy. The Lizard, for his honesty, was blessed. From that day on, the Lizard lived freely, while the Gecko hid in the walls of houses, shamed and outcast. The Lesson: Never repay good with evil, and honesty is always rewarded.

3. The Elephant and the Ant

The Elephant was arrogant, believing his size and strength made him superior to all other creatures. He trampled anthills and drank from streams without sharing. One day, a tiny Ant crawled into the Elephant's ear and began to bite. The pain was maddening. The Elephant trumpeted, shook his head, and ran through the forest, but he could not dislodge her. Exhausted and humbled, the Elephant finally collapsed. The Ant crawled out and simply said, "Size is not everything." The Elephant never again disrespected the smallest of creatures. The Lesson: Do not despise the small, for even the smallest can bring down the mighty.

4. The Woodpecker's Pride

The Woodpecker was once a beautiful bird with a sweet song. He was the envy of the forest, but he refused to share his food during a famine, mocking the starving birds. When the famine ended, the other birds reported him to Chukwu. As punishment, Chukwu took away his beautiful feathers and his melodious voice. He was given a new task: to peck endlessly at the trees for insects. His song became a harsh, repetitive knock. The Lesson: Greed and selfishness lead to loss; share what you have, for abundance is meant to be shared.

5. The First Mermaid

Long ago, a young woman named Adaeze lived by the river. One day, she found a beautiful mirror in the water. It belonged to Mami Wata, the water spirit. Instead of keeping it, Adaeze returned the mirror, asking for nothing in return. Mami Wata, touched by her honesty, blessed Adaeze with wealth, beauty, and a family. She warned her to never forget the river. Adaeze prospered and brought offerings to the spirit every year, living a long and happy life. The Lesson: Kindness and honesty are always rewarded, especially by the spirits.

6. The Ogbanje Who Chose to Stay

Nneka was a heartbroken mother who had lost five children, all spirit-children known as ogbanje who returned to the spirit world. When she bore a sixth child, Obiageli, she feared the same fate. She went to a diviner, who told her the child had chosen to stay. Love for her mother had broken the pact with the spirit world. Obiageli grew strong and healthy, married, and had her own children. She taught them the story of her choice, proving that love can bridge the gap between the human and spirit worlds. The Lesson: Love can break even the strongest bonds, and the human and spirit worlds are not as far apart as they seem.

7. The King's Drum

A terrible drought struck a village. An old woman recalled a legend of a magical drum hidden deep in the forest, guarded by spirits, that could call the rain. The strong young men went to find it but returned empty-handed, having tried to take it by force. A young girl named Ada went next, armed only with faith and humility. She walked without fear or anger and was able to find the drum. She played it, and the rain returned. Ada did not boast, simply saying, "The rain came because we trusted." The Lesson: Faith and humility can achieve what strength and force cannot.

8. The Promise of the Kolanut

Okafor, a man known for his wisdom, broke a sacred promise to his dying brother to care for his children. He treated them as servants, keeping them hungry and scared. A stranger came to the village and refused to share a kolanut with Okafor, saying he would not eat until the truth was told. The stranger was the spirit of Okafor's brother, who had returned to judge him. Okafor was punished with poverty and shame, but his brother's children stayed and forgave him. The Lesson: Promises are sacred. Breaking a promise dishonors the ancestors and the kolanut that binds the living to the dead.

9. Why the Crocodile Lives Alone

Long ago, the Crocodile and his wife lived on the riverbank. The Crocodile was a hard worker, but his wife was lazy and greedy. She would eat all the food he caught before he returned home. Tired of her behavior, the Crocodile devised a test. He caught a large fish and told his wife to cook it. He then pretended to leave, but hid and watched. His wife ate the entire fish herself. The Crocodile was so disgusted that he drove her away from his home. From that day forward, the Crocodile has lived alone in the deepest parts of the river. The Lesson: Greed and selfishness drive love and companionship away.

10. The Wrestler's Ghost

There was a wrestler whose back never touched the ground. He was so proud that he boasted no one, man or spirit, could defeat him. A silent stranger with eyes that did not reflect light came to the village and challenged him. The match was strange. The wrestler could not move the stranger, who was as heavy as stone. The stranger then revealed he was a ghost from the land of the dead. The wrestler's back touched the ground in defeat, teaching him a lesson he never forgot. The Lesson: Pride goes before a fall, and the strongest man is weak before the spirit world.

11. The Shapeshifting Crow

Udenolu was a large, black bird known as the king of the crows. He possessed a magical gift: he could shapeshift. He often transformed into a handsome young man to walk among the villagers, tricking them and stealing their food. The people grew suspicious of this beautiful stranger who was always followed by a large crow. They set a trap. When Udenolu was discovered, he tried to fly away, but the villagers caught him. His shame was so great that he never again returned to the village in human form. The Lesson: Deceit may bring temporary gain, but it always leads to a fall.

12. The Tortoise and the Cane Rat

The Tortoise and the Cane Rat were friends, but the Tortoise was lazy. When the other animals went on a hunting expedition, the Tortoise refused to join them, preferring to stay and eat the food they had stored. The Cane Rat shared his food with the Tortoise, but soon the Tortoise ate more than his share. When the hunters returned, they found their stores empty and the Tortoise fat and happy. The Cane Rat, ashamed and angry, banished the Tortoise from his home, and the two were never friends again. The Lesson: Laziness and greed will exhaust the patience of even the best of friends.

13. The Mysterious Messengers

In the days when humans and spirits still walked together, the people were worried about death. They sent two messengers to Chukwu to ask for a reversal. One, a fast Dog, was told to deliver a message of life. The other, a slow Sheep, was told to deliver a message of death. The Dog, distracted by the smell of fresh palm oil, stopped to eat and forgot his errand. The Sheep, slow and steady, delivered his message first. And so, death became a permanent part of the world. The Lesson: Great consequences can hinge on small distractions.

14. Why the Tortoise Has a Cracked Shell

In the days when the sky was close to the earth, there was a great feast in the heavens, and all the birds were invited. The Tortoise, Mbe, had no wings, so he begged the birds to lend him a feather each. They agreed, and with his patchwork of feathers, he flew with them. But before they entered the sky, Mbe told the birds that in the sky, each should take a new name. "Call me 'All of You,'" he said, "so that when the Sky King welcomes us, I will know he means me."

At the feast, the Sky King said, "Welcome, all of you," and the Tortoise stepped forward and ate everything. When the birds saw they had been tricked, they waited until the feast was over, then each plucked back the feather they had lent him. Mbe fell from the sky. Before he fell, he called to his wife to pile soft things in the courtyard. But a parrot, remembering the trick, flew ahead and told his wife to pile hard things—stones and iron—for Mbe was testing the strength of his shell. Mbe landed on the hard pile, and his shell shattered. A medicine man glued the pieces back together, but the cracks remained. The Lesson: Greed and ingratitude will always be repaid, for the sky is high and the fall is long.

15. Why the Bat Flies at Night

The Bat and the Bush Rat were once close friends who lived together in the forest. The Bat was ugly, but the Bush Rat was beautiful, with soft fur and a pleasant face. The Bat grew jealous and wanted the Bush Rat's beauty for himself. He invited the Bush Rat to bathe with him in the river, and while the Bush Rat washed, the Bat secretly boiled a pot of water. He told the Bush Rat to jump into the water to become clean, and the Bush Rat, trusting his friend, obeyed. He was scalded to death. The Bat then put on the Bush Rat's skin and walked among the animals, pretending to be him. But the animals saw his strange eyes and his crooked walk, and they knew what he had done. They chased the Bat from the village, and he was forced to hide in caves and dark places, emerging only at night. The Lesson: Wickedness will always be discovered, and the wicked will be driven into darkness.

16. The Cat and the Rat

The Cat and the Rat were once the closest of friends. They lived in the same hut, shared their meals, and traveled together to visit their families in other villages. One day, they decided to prepare a great feast. They made the famous soup e richaa e chefuo uwa—the soup that makes you eat and forget the world. The Rat promised to fetch the spices; the Cat promised to kill a fowl. Soon, the soup was boiling, and its delicious smell filled the compound. To let it cool, the two friends went for a short walk.

But the Rat could not stop thinking about the soup. He pretended he had forgotten his smoking pipe and begged the Cat to let him return and fetch it. The Cat warned him to come back quickly. When the Rat reached the hut, he began to eat the soup as fast as he could. But the pot tipped over and trapped him. He was still inside, chewing on the scraps, when the Cat grew impatient and ran home. The Cat lifted the pot and found the Rat covered in soup, his belly swollen, his paws greasy with guilt. Without a word, the Cat looked at him with a gaze that held all the betrayal in the world. From that day forward, the Cat has chased and killed every Rat he has found. The Lesson: Greed destroys friendship, and betrayal leaves a wound that time cannot heal.

17. The Hawk and the Hen

Long ago, a Hawk fell in love with a young Hen. He was a fierce hunter, and she was beautiful. He promised to protect her and provide for her, and she agreed to marry him. They lived happily together until a rich Rooster came to the village. The Rooster promised the Hen grain, corn, and a warm coop, so she left the Hawk and went to live with the Rooster. The Hawk was heartbroken. He went to the Rooster and demanded payment for the loss of his wife, but the Rooster laughed at him, saying he was protected by the farmer and his fence.

The Hawk said, "You are right. I cannot touch you. But from this day forward, I will take what is yours." And so the Hawk began to carry off the Rooster's chicks, one by one. Ever since, whenever a hawk circles in the sky, the mother hen gathers her chicks beneath her wings and says, "Stay close to me, for my talons are soaked in blood." The Lesson: A broken vow may take its revenge on the innocent for generations.

18. Why the Chameleon Changes Colour

Before the first man pressed his foot on the earth, Ogwurugwu the Chameleon was one of Chukwu's chosen messengers. Of all the creatures, Chukwu found him the wisest and most trustworthy. Because of this, Chukwu gave him a colour of his own—a rich bronze that glowed like metal fresh from the blacksmith's fire. But the animals admired him so much that pride crawled into his heart. He began to wonder why he remained only a messenger while others ruled as kings on earth.

One day, Ogwurugwu climbed a tall tree and called out to Chukwu, asking to be made king over the other animals. Chukwu was surprised, for never before had a messenger asked for a throne. "A crown is heavier than it appears," Chukwu said. "You are beautiful, you are wise, you are loved—but you are not content. And so you must learn the lesson of contentment." Chukwu took back the solid bronze colour and gave the Chameleon instead the ability to change, to shift, to blend. From that day forward, the Chameleon could never hold a colour of his own. He could only reflect the colours of others, swaying uncertainly as he moves, as if still searching for the beauty he once had. The Lesson: A person who despises what they have may spend a lifetime wearing the gifts of others.

19. The Girl Who Married a Skull

Afiong was the most beautiful girl in her village, and every young man wanted to marry her. But she refused them all, mocking them as ugly, old, or poor. "I will only marry a man who is handsome and strong," she said. Her parents pleaded with her to consider character over beauty, but she mocked them too. One day, a tall, handsome stranger came to the village, dressed in fine cloth and carrying a bag of cowries. He asked for Afiong's hand, and she accepted at once, despite her parents' warnings that no one knew where he came from.

She followed the stranger to his home deep in the bush. At first, she was treated well. But at night, the stranger removed his skin, and beneath it was a skull—bare bone and hollow eyes. "You married a skull," it said. "Now you will live with the dead." Afiong fled through the forest, pursued by the skull and his mother, until she reached a river. A kind ferryman carried her across, and when the skull demanded she be returned, the ferryman refused. The skull could not cross the water, and Afiong was saved. She returned home humbled and ashamed, and warned every young girl in the village never to marry a man she did not truly know. The Lesson: Appearances deceive. True love requires knowledge, not desire, and the counsel of parents is a shield, not a chain.

20. Why the Mosquito Buzzes in the Ear

The Mosquito once fell in love with the Ear, thinking her the most beautiful creature he had ever seen. Every day, he flew to her and whispered words of love, promising to protect her forever. But the Ear laughed at him. "You are too small," she said. "You are nothing. Why would I marry a creature no bigger than a speck of dust?"

The Mosquito was humiliated. His love turned to bitterness. "I will make you pay," he said. "Every day, I will come to you. Every day, I will remind you of what you did to me. You will never have peace." And so, from that day forward, the Mosquito has flown close to the Ear and buzzed—reminding her of the love she rejected, refusing to be forgotten. The Ear slaps at him, trying to drive him away, but the Mosquito always returns. The Lesson: Scorn not the smallest of suitors, for a wounded heart never forgets.

21. The Sun and the Moon

In the beginning, the Sun and the Moon were the best of friends. The Sun, Anyanwu, was bold and fiery. The Moon, Mmanwu, was gentle and calm. They walked together in the sky, and the whole world rejoiced in their harmony. But the Sun grew proud. He wanted to be greater than the Moon. He wanted the praise and the glory for himself alone.

One day, the Sun invited the Moon to a feast in his palace. But when the food was served, the Sun used his burning heat to spoil the Moon's portion, so that she could not eat. The Sun feasted while the Moon sat in silence. "Friend Sun," she asked, "why have you done this? Did we not promise to walk together in unity?" But the Sun laughed. "I am the brighter one," he said. "The sky belongs to me. You are but a pale shadow beside my greatness." The Moon left the feast in sorrow and returned to her cool dwelling in the night. From that day forward, the Sun and the Moon never shared the sky as they once did. Sometimes, during an eclipse, they meet again—and the people say they are still quarreling, reminding the world of the danger of betrayal. The Lesson: Once trust is broken, harmony is lost forever.

22. The Origin of Palm Wine

Long ago, before the villages knew the taste of palm wine, there lived a hunter named Okeke. One day, he wounded an antelope and followed it deep into the forest until he lost his way. As night fell, he lay down beneath a great palm tree to wait for morning. But sleep would not come, for above him he heard a strange sound—soft, musical weeping, dripping down from the tree. He looked up, and the palm tree was crying. From the top of its trunk, a sweet liquid fell, drop by drop. Okeke caught some in his hand and tasted it. It was sweet, warm, and more delicious than anything he had ever tasted.

The tree spoke to him. "I was once a woman named Akwa," it said, "who loved a spirit. When the spirit returned to the other world, I followed him, but I could not enter. I was turned into a tree and planted here, at the edge of the world, to weep for him forever. When you return to your village, cut my trunk and let my tears flow into calabashes. Let the people drink and be merry, for joy is the greatest gift." Okeke did as the tree asked. He carried the sweet liquid home in calabashes, and from that day forward, the people knew the taste of palm wine—a gift born from sorrow, meant to be shared in joy. The Lesson: Sorrow can be transformed into joy, and the gift of the spirit world is meant to be shared.

23. The Calabash Child

There was once a King who had no children, and his wife, the Queen, wept every night, for a woman without a child is like a tree without fruit. One day, as the Queen worked in her farm, she saw a little girl sitting among her calabashes—a child so beautiful that she seemed born of the earth itself. "Who are you?" the Queen asked. "I am your daughter," the child replied. The Queen took her home and presented her to the King, who rejoiced and named her Adaeze, the King's daughter.

But the King had other wives who had no children of their own, and they grew jealous of the child. One day, they took Adaeze into the forest and abandoned her, telling the King that she had wandered off and been eaten by wild animals. The King mourned for his daughter. But Adaeze did not die. She was found by an old woman who lived in the forest and raised her as her own. Years later, a hunter from the King's village saw her and recognized her. She returned to the palace and stood before the King and his wives. "I am not a spirit," she said. "I am your daughter. And these women—these women who abandoned me in the forest—they are the ones who bring misfortune to your house." The King banished the wicked wives and welcomed his daughter home, and she ruled by her father's side, bringing wisdom and peace to the kingdom. The Lesson: Jealousy destroys the jealous, and the child you try to harm may be the one who saves you.

24. The Hornbill and the Yams

The Hornbill gave his wife some yams and said, "Cook these, and eat them. I will get better food at my mother-in-law's house." His wife cooked the yams and ate them, as he had told her to do. But when the Hornbill arrived at his mother-in-law's house, he found that they had already eaten, and he was given nothing.

He flew home angry and hungry. "Bring me those yams!" he shouted at his wife. But the yams were gone—she had eaten them, as he had commanded. The Hornbill went to bed hungry, and to this day, he remains a restless and dissatisfied bird, always searching for food, never satisfied with what he has. The Lesson: Contentment with what you have is better than chasing what you do not need.

25. Why the Agama Lizard Bobs His Head

In the beginning, when Chukwu was creating the world, the earth was soft. It was like mud that has not been pressed. The animals walked upon it and sank. Trees planted their roots and wavered. Nothing could stand. Chukwu called all the creatures together and said, "The earth is not firm. Someone must press it down, so that life may grow."

The Agama Lizard stepped forward. "I will do it," he said. And so he began to press the ground with his head—bobbing up and down, up and down—packing the earth until it was hard and stable. He worked for days, for weeks, until his head ached and his neck was sore. When the earth was finally firm, Chukwu called the animals together again and asked, "Who will be honored for this work?" But the animals had forgotten. They were busy with their own affairs. They did not thank the Lizard. The Lizard was hurt, for he had given his strength to the earth, and no one remembered. And so, even today, the Agama Lizard still bobs his head—still testing the firmness of the earth, still doing the work he was never thanked for. The Lesson: The greatest work is often done by those who receive the least recognition; honor them while you can.

26. Why the Leopard Has Spots

Long ago, the Leopard was the finest dancer in the animal kingdom. When the drums sounded at the village square, all the animals gathered to watch him. He moved like water, he leaped like fire, and he was grace itself. But the Leopard grew proud. "There is no one who can dance as I do," he boasted. "I am the greatest dancer in the world." The animals grew tired of his boasting. They said, "If you are so great, dance in the fire. If you can dance in the flames without being burned, then we will believe you."

The Leopard laughed. "Fire is nothing to me," he said. A great fire was built in the village square. The drums sounded. The Leopard stepped into the flames and began to dance. But the fire was hot. Sparks flew and landed on his skin. They burned him and marked him. He danced faster, trying to escape, but the fire was everywhere. Finally he leaped out of the flames and ran into the forest, rolling in the mud to put out the burns. But the marks remained—dark spots on his golden coat. From that day forward, the Leopard never danced in the fire again, and he never boasted again. The Lesson: Pride invites danger, and the strongest are not immune to the flames.

27. Why the Snail Carries His House

There was once a Snail who was a chief. He had the finest house in the village, made of mud and thatch, with a door of carved wood. But the Snail had enemies. The Leopard wanted his land. The Eagle wanted his roof. The Snake wanted his door. One night, they came together and burned the Snail's house, scattered his family, and left him with nothing.

The Snail went to Chukwu and said, "I have lost everything. My house is gone, my family is scattered, I have no home." Chukwu looked on him with pity. "You have been faithful, kind, and humble," He said. "For this, I will give you a house that cannot be taken from you—a house you can carry with you wherever you go." And Chukwu gave the Snail a shell. The Snail climbed inside it, and it fit perfectly. He could carry it on his back, sleep inside it, and take it with him wherever he went. And so the Snail was never homeless again. The Lesson: What is taken from you can be restored in a different form; home is a peace you carry within you.

28. The Boy Who Spoke with the Ancestors

There was once a boy named Ndubuisi who was born with a caul over his face. The elders said this was a sign: "He can see the spirits. He can speak with the ancestors." And they were right. Ndubuisi could see what others could not. He saw the mmụọ—the spirits of the dead—walking among the living. He saw his grandfather, who had died before he was born, sitting by the fire. At first, the villagers were afraid of him and whispered that he was strange. But Ndubuisi was not strange—he was kind. He helped the sick, comforted the grieving, and told the people what the ancestors wanted them to know.

One year, a famine came to the land. The rains did not fall, the crops withered, and the people were starving. The elders consulted the diviner and made sacrifices, but nothing worked. Then Ndubuisi spoke. "The ancestors are angry," he said. "You have not honored them. You have not poured libation. You have not told the stories. The ancestors are waiting, and they are hungry for remembrance." The elders were ashamed, for they had forgotten the ancestors. Ndubuisi led them to the shrine. They poured palm wine, offered kola nut, told the stories of the ancestors, and sang the songs. That night, the rain fell. The crops grew. The famine ended. Ndubuisi became a great healer and teacher, and when he died, he joined the ancestors himself, guiding the generations to come. The Lesson: The ancestors are not gone; they live in the stories we tell and the memories we keep.
