Thirteen Stories of Ancient Igbo Communities

1. STORY ONE: The Priest-King of Nri

Long before the British came, before the wars and the treaties, there was a kingdom that did not fight with swords. It fought with the ofo—the staff of ritual authority. It was called Nri, and it was the oldest kingdom in what would one day be called Nigeria.

The people of Nri did not conquer with armies. They conquered with purity. The Eze Nri, the priest-king, was not a warrior. He was a ritual specialist, a man so sacred that he could not be looked upon by ordinary eyes. His face was never seen in public. When he traveled, he was hidden behind cloth. When he spoke, his words were law.

According to the migration history, the founders of Nri left Aguleri on the River Anambra and settled in a place they called Nri, which comprised three villages: Agukwu, Diodo, and Akamkpisi. But their influence did not stay in those three villages. Nri men traveled to the farthest reaches of Igbo land, carrying their otonsi—the spear of peace and diplomatic immunity—in their left hand, and their ofo—the staff of ritual and political authority—in their right hand.

They were considered sacred people. They could not be molested or killed. They were the symbols of inter-village peace. They carried the ritual power of cleansing abomination, of making and dissolving taboos, of arbitrating between warring villages, of consecrating chiefs and title holders.

The power of Nri was the power of the spirit, not the sword. And for centuries, it held Igbo land together.

The Lesson: True authority is not taken by force. It is granted by the people, and it is maintained by wisdom, purity, and the trust of the ancestors.

2. STORY TWO: The Bronze Casters of Igbo-Ukwu

In the 9th century, long before the kingdoms of Europe had mastered the art of bronze casting, there was a community in the heart of Igbo land that had perfected it. It was called Igbo-Ukwu, and its bronze works were the finest in all of West Africa.

In 1939, a man named Isaiah Anozie was digging a cistern in his compound. His hoe struck something hard. He pulled it out and found a bronze vessel, intricately decorated with insects and animals. He showed it to his neighbors. Some were afraid. They thought it was a thing of the spirits. But others were curious, and the news spread.

When archaeologists finally came, they uncovered a treasure trove: cast bronze sculptures, chieftaincy paraphernalia, glass pendants, and thousands of beads. The bronze objects were decorated with a style that had never been seen before—deep channelling, projecting bosses, and a geometric precision that spoke of an advanced civilization.

The Igbo-Ukwu findings dated to the 9th century CE. They were the earliest known bronzes in West Africa. They proved that the Igbo people were not a primitive people waiting to be discovered. They were the builders of an advanced civilization, participating in long-distance trade, connected to the wider world.

The Lesson: The Igbo past is not a void. It is filled with bronze and gold and the evidence of genius. The history of the Igbo people is a history of civilization.

3. STORY THREE: The Founding of Arochukwu

In the tropical rain forest of what is now Abia State, there was a town that was not founded by one people, but by three. The Igbo, the Ibibio, and the Akpa came together to create a kingdom that would reshape the history of the entire region.

The first Igbo settlers were the Eze Agwu group, led by Agwu Inobia. They arrived in the land that the Ibibio had already settled. They were not welcomed. The Aro-Ibibio wars began, a long and bitter conflict that seemed to have no end.

But there was a man among the Igbo named Nnachi. He saw that the war could not be won by force alone. He went to the Ibibio king and spoke of peace. He offered a pact, a union of peoples. The king agreed, and the Kingdom of Arochukwu was born.

Arochukwu was not just a town. It was a state. It had a king, the Eze Aro. It had a council of chiefs. It had the Oracle of Ibini Ukpabi, the Long Juju, which drew pilgrims from across Igbo land and beyond. It had wealth from trade, from slaves, from the palm oil that flowed down the rivers to the coast.

The date of its foundation is calculated to be around 1690/1720. But the legacy of Arochukwu lasted far longer than its years. It was an empire built not on land, but on influence.

The Lesson: A kingdom can be built not just by conquest, but by diplomacy and the union of peoples.

4. STORY FOUR: The Long Juju of Arochukwu

The Oracle of Ibini Ukpabi was the most powerful oracle in all of Igbo land. It was called the Long Juju by the British, but to the Igbo, it was the voice of Chukwu himself. Pilgrims came from distant lands to seek its judgment. They descended into a gorge, where the oracle spoke. Those found guilty walked into dark tunnels and were never seen again. Those found innocent returned to their families.

But the priests of the oracle grew greedy. They discovered that they could sell the guilty into slavery instead of sacrificing them. The verdicts were falsified. Hundreds of people visited the oracle and never returned. Their communities believed the oracle had devoured them. The red water that flowed from the shrine was taken as proof that the condemned were dead.

The Long Juju became the center of the slave trade in Igbo land. The Aro people grew wealthy and powerful, controlling the oracle and the trade routes. But the blood of the innocent cried out from the tunnels. And eventually, the British came with guns and cannon, and the Long Juju was destroyed.

The Lesson: Even the most sacred oracle can be corrupted by greed. The voice of the gods can be twisted by human hands.

5. STORY FIVE: The King Who Crossed the River

In the 16th or early 17th century, there was a great upheaval in the Kingdom of Benin. A dispute arose between the Oba and a man named Chima. Some say Chima was an Igbo man who had been taken to Benin as a slave. Others say he was a prince of the royal house. But all agree that he was forced to flee.

Chima gathered his followers—men, women, and children—and left Benin. They moved through western Igbo land, founding towns along the way. These towns became known as Umu Ezechima, the children of Chima.

When they reached the River Niger, Chima died. His followers divided into two groups. One group, led by Oreze, crossed the river and founded a new state. They called it Onitsha. The other group, led by Esumai, went south and founded Aboh.

Onitsha grew into a great kingdom. It was an Igbo state in language and custom, but it retained elements of Benin influence, most evident in the institution of the Obi, or king. The Onitsha people remembered their origin. They told stories of their migration. They honored the memory of Chima.

The Lesson: A people can lose their home and find a new one. Migration is not a loss; it is a journey to a new destiny.

6. STORY SIX: The Mixed Kingdom of Aboh

Aboh was a kingdom of two peoples. The Edo immigrants from Benin had conquered the earlier Igbo settlers. But the conquered were more numerous, and in time, they imposed their language on the conquerors. The Edo may have been stronger, but the Igbo tongue prevailed.

The Obi of Aboh was not a hereditary king. When an Obi died, anyone from the Umude section—the descendants of the Edo—could aspire to the throne. The Odua, the oldest man from the Umude section, consulted with the elders and announced the new king.

The Iyasere was the chief counsellor and commander-in-chief. He had to be of mixed parentage—one parent from the Umude, one from the Ndiche, the descendants of the non-Edo. This was the genius of Aboh. It was a kingdom that balanced the two peoples, that gave each a place, that forged a new identity from the union of two.

Aboh controlled the river trade. Its influence extended over the riverain clans and some upland clans. It was powerful enough to command the loyalty of Ogume, Ashaka, Amai, Ossissa, and many others.

The Lesson: Unity in diversity is not a weakness. It is a strength. A kingdom that embraces all its peoples is a kingdom that endures.

7. STORY SEVEN: The Blacksmiths of Awka

Awka was a town of iron. The Awka blacksmiths were renowned throughout Igbo land, and even beyond its borders. They were the ones who forged the tools that cleared the farms, the weapons that defended the villages, the hoes that dug the yam mounds.

The Awka area was the site of the Nri civilization that produced the earliest documented bronze works in Sub-Saharan Africa around 800 AD. The Umueri blacksmith clan traced their origin to Awka. The received wisdom was that Igbo ironworking technology originated with Awka blacksmiths, at least as far back as the 9th century.

Before the British came, Awka was governed by titled men known as Ozo and Ndichie. The town was divided into seven Igbo groups sharing common blood lineage, divided into two sections: Ifite and Ezi. Half of the men of Awka were itinerant artisans and traders, traveling to distant lands, plying their trade, returning with wealth and stories.

The Lesson: A people who master a craft will never be poor. The blacksmiths of Awka proved that skill is the greatest wealth.

8. STORY EIGHT: The Ancient People of Nsukka

Nsukka is one of the oldest settlements in Igbo land. Archaeology has shown that human activity in the area dates back to 3000 BCE, and pottery has been found that is dated to about 2500 BCE. The people of Nsukka are an ancient people, and they know it.

Nsukka Asadu Ideke Arumona was the original settlement. It was made up of four quarters: Nkpunano, Nru n'ato Ezke Ekwensi, Ihe, and Owere Dialoke Ndidiaba. Nine autonomous communities were created out of it, and in all, there are 117 villages.

The social organization of Nsukka was based on kinship. The nnaanyi was the father of the family. The okpara umunna was the eldest son of the kindred. The onyishi was the head of the village, and he possessed the arua, the symbol of authority with which he piloted the affairs of the village.

The ndishi n'akpruarua, the council of elders, and the nd oha, the council of titled men, governed the town. The nzemaa, the council of maskers, guarded the traditions. The umuda, the council of first daughters, spoke for the women. The nd igwe, the council of traditional rulers, stood at the top.

The Lesson: A community is governed not by one voice, but by many. The wisdom of the elders, the strength of the titled men, the spirit of the masquerades, and the voice of the daughters—all are needed to keep the village in balance.

9. STORY NINE: The Heartland of the Igbo

The elders say that the heart of Igbo land is not in one place. It is in three places: Awka, Orlu, and Okigwe. These three towns form a belt, and the people in this area have no tradition of coming from anywhere else. They are the original Igbo.

Scholars have long debated this. Some say the Igbo migrated from the north, from the Benue valley. Some say they came from the west, from the Benin kingdom. But the people of Awka, Orlu, and Okigwe say they have always been here. The archaeology supports them. The Nsukka-Udi-Okigwe cuesta has witnessed human activity from very early times.

The Nri-Awka-Orlu complex was probably the earliest center of Igbo settlement in southern Nigeria. The Nri-Awka and Isuama (Orlu) area has been described as "the Igbo center or core". It was from this core that the Igbo people spread across the land, by waves of migration, carrying their language, their customs, and their spirit.

The Lesson: The Igbo people are not newcomers to their land. They are the original inhabitants, the children of the soil, the descendants of the first settlers.

10. STORY TEN: The Sacred Land of Aguleri

Aguleri is a town on the River Anambra. It is a sacred town, for it is here that the Igbo people trace their origin to Eri, the first man, the one whom Chukwu lowered from the sky onto an anthill.

According to oral tradition, Eri's first son was named Agulu, and it was he who settled in the land that would become Aguleri. The town still celebrates a festival called Olili Obibia Eri, held in the seventh native month, to honor Eri, the father of the Igbo race.

Other Igbo communities come to Aguleri to offer sacrifices in the sacred temples. They come to request favors, to seek blessings, to connect with the source of their ancestry. The sacred place of Obu-gad is used for social and religious occasions. It is a place of pilgrimage, a place of origin, a place where the spirit of Eri still lives.

The Lesson: To know where you come from is to know who you are. Aguleri is the root of the Igbo tree.

11. STORY ELEVEN: The Cradle of the Igbo

There is a controversy among the Igbo people. Two towns claim to be the cradle of the Igbo: Aguleri and Nri. Aguleri says that Eri, the father of all Igbo, landed there. Nri says that the founders of Nri left Aguleri and founded the settlement of Nri, which became the center of Igbo civilization.

But some say the dispute is unnecessary. Umueri, a town near Aguleri, claims to be the ancestral home of all Igbos. A team of Igbo researchers confirmed Umueri as the ancestral home, saying that all Igbos can trace their ancestral home from the ancient town founded by the children of Eri. It was the oldest human settlement established by early Igbo founders, the earliest human settlement east of the Niger.

The controversy continues. But the elders say that the dispute is a sign of love, not division. The Igbo people love their history. They love their origins. They want to know where they come from.

The Lesson: The search for origins is a search for identity. The Igbo people are still searching, still remembering, still honoring the ancestors.

12. STORY TWELVE: The Fishermen of Oguta

Oguta is a town on the east bank of Oguta Lake, in what is now Imo State. The people of Oguta trace their origins to the great Benin kingdom, during the reign of Oba Ewuare the Great. They migrated from Benin and settled in their present abode due to the exploits of three great brothers: Osu, and others.

In the beginning, Oguta was a fishing community. The people lived on the fish of the lake. But then the palm oil trade came. European and African merchants established stations at Oguta. The town was transformed from a predominantly fishing community to a relatively prosperous and cosmopolitan town.

Oguta was one of the first territories used by the British to advance into the Igbo hinterland. It was a place of encounter, of trade, of change. The people of Oguta adapted. They learned the ways of the merchants. They learned the ways of the British. But they did not forget their origins. They did not forget the great Benin kingdom from which their forefathers came.

The Lesson: Change is not a loss of identity. A people can adapt to new circumstances while still honoring their past.

13. STORY THIRTEEN: The Legacy of Eri

The stories of the ancient Igbo communities all lead back to one man: Eri. Whether it is Nri, Aguleri, Umueri, or any of the other towns that claim descent from him, the memory of Eri is the memory of the beginning.

Eri was the first man. He was lowered from the sky by Chukwu. He lived in the age when the sky was close and the people did not have to work. He was the father of the Igbo race.

When Eri died, the sky moved away. The age of abundance ended. The people had to learn to farm, to hunt, to build, to trade. They had to learn to live in a world that was no longer easy. But they did not forget Eri. They did not forget the lessons he taught. They did not forget the covenant between the human world and the spirit world.

And so, the ancient communities of Igbo land are not just towns on a map. They are the living memory of Eri. They are the children of the first man. They are the keepers of the covenant. They are the heirs of the sky that once fed us.

The Lesson: The history of the Igbo people is not just a story of the past. It is a living tradition, passed down from generation to generation, from the time of Eri to the present day. The ancestors are not gone. They live in the towns they founded, in the customs they established, in the stories they told. And as long as the Igbo people remember, they will never be lost.
