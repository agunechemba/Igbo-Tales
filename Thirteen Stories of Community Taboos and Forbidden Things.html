Thirteen Stories of Community Taboos and Forbidden Things

1. STORY ONE: The Brown Monkey That Represents the Kindred

In Ezioha, a village in Mgbowo in the Awgu area, there lived a family called the Amegugwu. They were an exogamous unit, a kindred bound by blood and by a sacred covenant. And there was one law above all others that they dared not break: they were forbidden to harm, eat, or kill the brown monkey called Utobo.

The elders said that the Utobo were not ordinary monkeys. They were representatives of the kindred. They were the links between the living and the dead. Every male in the Amegugwu family was represented by his own Utobo in the spirit world. When an Utobo died, a man in the family would die. When a man died, an Utobo would die in the forest. They were one, bound by a covenant older than memory.

But the Amegugwu family did not just protect the Utobo. They had power over them. They could command the Utobo. They could ward them off. They could invoke them into an enemy's farm. The Utobo were their messengers, their agents, their spiritual weapons. And because of this, the Amegugwu family was respected—and feared—throughout the community.

No Amegugwu man ever raised a hand against an Utobo. They knew that to kill the monkey was to kill themselves. To eat its flesh was to eat the flesh of their own kindred. The covenant was absolute, and the consequences were death.

The Lesson: Some bonds transcend the physical world. The totem is not just an animal; it is a spiritual double, and to harm it is to harm yourself.

2. STORY TWO: The Monkey That Warned of Invasion

In Awka, the capital of Anambra State, the monkey is sacred. Not just any monkey—the white-bellied monkey that roams freely through the town. The elders say that long ago, the Awka people were constantly under threat from their neighbors. One of their oppressors was King Okoli Ijeoma of Ndikelionwu, who planned to invade their land.

The elders of Awka sent delegates to Akoto, a land known for its powerful herbal doctors, to seek a charm that would fortify their warriors. They returned with a renowned herbal doctor named Okoyeke. He prepared the charm but warned that it must be venerated by the community to become effective.

The Imoka deity was the guardian of Awka. And the monkeys were its messengers. When the Nwafia people laid siege to attack Awka, the Imoka sent its emissaries—the monkeys in the forest—to warn the people of the impending attack. The Awka warriors sensed the danger, repelled the attack, and defeated their enemies.

From that day forward, it became a taboo to kill or eat the meat of monkeys in Awka. The monkeys roam the town freely, entering compounds and markets, and no one harms them. They are the messengers of Imoka, and to harm them is to invite the wrath of the deity.

The Lesson: The totem is a guardian. When it warns, the wise listen. To harm the messenger is to sever the connection between the community and its protector.

3. STORY THREE: The Python That Is Our Mother

In Idemili, in Anambra State, the python is not just a snake. It is nne—mother. It is the representative of the gods, the guidance of the children of Idemili. Charles Ejimbe of Akwukwu community in Idemili explained that the royal pythons are the messengers of the goddess Idemili herself. They seldom bite people. If a python mistakenly bites someone, it will sigh, retrieving its venom from the victim.

The people of Idemili do not harm pythons. They do not eat them. They do not even speak of them with disrespect. The python is allowed to go wherever it chooses—into homes, into markets, into the sacred groves. It is a blessing when a python appears at a wedding or a naming ceremony. It is a sign that the ancestors are present, that the spirits are watching, that the occasion is sacred.

In Idemili, Awkuzu, Igboukwu, and Nnewi, the python is protected. The people say that if you kill a python, you have killed your own father. You have severed the connection between the living and the dead. You have invited the anger of the goddess. The burial rites for a slain python are the same as those for a human being—a full human-sized burial, with all the honors due to a kinsman.

The Lesson: The python is the mother of the community. It is not an animal to be feared, but a kin to be honored. To kill it is to kill your own blood.

4. STORY FOUR: The Crocodile That Guards the Sacred Stream

In Onitsha, there was a law that every child knew from the moment they could walk: never bait for fish or kill any crocodile or alligator in the Nkissis streams. The Nkissis were not ordinary streams. They were the dwelling places of the spirits. The elders said that the Nkissis were where the evil spirits bathed. To disturb them was to invite death.

The crocodiles in the Nkissis were not ordinary crocodiles. They were the guardians of the sacred waters. They were the servants of the spirits. They did not harm the people of Onitsha, and the people did not harm them. It was a covenant of mutual respect.

Violation of this injunction was punishable by death. The people of Onitsha did not take this law lightly. They knew that the crocodile was not just an animal. It was a guardian, a protector, a link between the human world and the spirit world.

And so the crocodiles of the Nkissis lived in peace, and the people of Onitsha lived in peace, and the covenant between them was never broken.

The Lesson: Sacred places have sacred guardians. To violate the covenant is to invite the wrath of the spirits.

5. STORY FIVE: The Monkey That Ate in the King's Compound

In Akpugoeze, in Enugu State, there is a species of monkey that is believed to be consecrated to the deities. These monkeys are not hunted. They are not harmed. They roam the homesteads undisturbed. They enter compounds. They eat from the trees. They are treated as honored guests, not as pests.

The elders say that these monkeys are the servants of the gods. They are the messengers of the deities. To harm one is to harm the god it serves. To kill one is to invite the anger of the spirit world.

The people of Akpugoeze do not need to be told to respect the monkeys. They have grown up with them. They have seen them in the compounds. They have watched them play in the trees. They have learned that the monkey is not just an animal. It is a sacred being, a servant of the gods, a reminder that the spirit world is always near.

The Lesson: The totem is a servant of the divine. To harm it is to offend the god it serves.

6. STORY SIX: The Green Snake Called Grandmother

In Akaeze, in Ebonyi State, there is a green snake that the people call Nne ochie—Grandmother. This snake is not harmed. It does not harm the indigenes. It lives among the people, in the trees and in the thatch, and it is treated with the respect due to an elder.

The people of Akaeze say that the green snake is an ancestor. It is a grandmother, returned from the spirit world to watch over her children. When a green snake appears in a compound, it is a sign of blessing. When it enters a home, it is welcomed. No one harms it. No one drives it away.

If anyone kills the green snake by mistake, the offender must conduct a burial ceremony for the snake, with all the rites due to a human being. The green snake is not an animal. It is a person. It is a grandmother. And a grandmother must be buried with honor.

The Lesson: The ancestor lives among us in many forms. To harm the ancestor, even by mistake, is to invite the anger of the spirit world.

7. STORY SEVEN: The Crab That Is Deified

In Afikpo, in Ebonyi State, the Ozziza cultural group defies the crab. It is an abomination to eat or kill crab. The crab is not just a creature of the water. It is a deity. It is a sacred being. To kill a crab is to incur the anger of the gods. The elders say that whoever kills a crab will be struck down by the deity. The punishment is not just spiritual; it is physical. The gods do not forgive those who harm their servants.

The people of Afikpo have kept this law for generations. They do not eat crab. They do not fish for crab. They do not even touch crab. The crab is sacred, and the covenant between the people and the deity is absolute.

The Lesson: The deity is present in the smallest of creatures. To harm the servant is to invite the wrath of the master.

8. STORY EIGHT: The Melon That Kills the Soil

In Akabor, a small town in the Oguta area of Imo State, there is a forbidden crop: melon. The people of Akabor do not plant melon. They do not allow it to grow in their fields. It is a taboo, a cursed crop.

The elders say that melon kills every other plant in the land. It destroys soil fertility. It causes starvation. The gods of the land are against this particular crop. When melon is seen growing anywhere in the community, the elders immediately seek a solution to remove it. If it is not removed, every other plant will die, and hunger will come to the land.

If a person sees melon seed mistakenly growing in their farm, they begin to perform incantations. They tell the gods how innocent they are. They exonerating themselves and their family from the curse. Then they pull it out.

The punishment for those who are ignorant of this belief—such as visitors—is to cleanse the land. They must get cowries and other sacrificial materials and hand them over to the elders. For those who are aware of the taboo, the punishment is more severe. They are paraded throughout the community with palm leaf tied around their waist. After the cleansing, they are not allowed to come out or communicate with others for seven days.

The Lesson: The land has its own laws. What is forbidden by the gods cannot be planted, no matter how profitable it may seem.

9. STORY NINE: The King Who Could Not Leave His Palace

In Onitsha, there was a king who was bound by a terrible taboo. He could not step out of his house into the town. He could not leave the precincts of his palace. If he did, a human sacrifice would have to be made to propitiate the gods. Indeed, it was said that he could not quit his palace under pain of death—or of giving up one or more slaves to be executed in his presence.

The king of Onitsha was not a free man. He was a prisoner of his own divinity. His sacredness was so great that his feet could not touch the common ground. His presence in the town would be so potent that the balance of the spirit world would be disturbed. He was the living link between the people and the gods, and that link had to be protected at all costs.

And so the king remained in his palace. His word was law. His judgment was final. But his body was confined, a sacrifice to the gods he served.

The Lesson: Great power comes with great restriction. The one who is closest to the gods is often the one who is most bound.

10. STORY TEN: The King Who Could Not See a Corpse

In Nri, the priest-king was subjected to many curious taboos. He could not see a corpse, even of one of his own children. He could not sit on the ground. He could not eat in the house of another.

The Eze Nri was not a warrior. He was a ritual specialist, a man so sacred that he could not be looked upon by ordinary eyes. His face was never seen in public. When he traveled, he was hidden behind cloth. When he spoke, his words were law.

The taboos that bound him were not punishments. They were protections. They preserved his ritual purity. They kept him separate from the contamination of death and the commonness of daily life. The king was not like other men. He was a vessel of the gods, and the vessel had to be kept clean.

The Lesson: The sacred and the profane cannot mix. The one who serves the gods must be separated from the things that defile.

11. STORY ELEVEN: The Twins That Were Abandoned

In Arochukwu and the surrounding areas, there was a taboo that brought sorrow to many families. Twins were considered an abomination. The people believed that Chukwu-Ibinukpabi, the supreme God, abhorred twins. They wondered how humans could resemble lower animals like dogs, goats, cats, and chickens to reproduce multiple births. Such a resemblance was considered an aberration of nature and therefore evil.

Twins were killed as soon as they left their mothers' wombs. Their families were looked at with suspicion. They were reduced to the status of ohu—people whose ancestors were slaves. A man whose wife had given birth to twins was regarded as an outcast, a harbinger of evil and bad luck. Whoever accepted twins accepted bad luck.

But there was a woman named Mary Slessor who came from Scotland. She was touched by the stories of the killing of twins. She came to Igbo land to dissuade the killers from such savagery. She fought the obnoxious notions of the people about twins. She saved many children. And in time, the practice was abolished.

The Lesson: Even the most deeply held taboos can be challenged. What is considered sacred can be questioned, and what is considered abomination can be redeemed.

12. STORY TWELVE: The Widow Who Drank the Water

In Aguleri, in Anambra East, there was a widowhood practice that was both sacred and terrible. When a woman lost her husband, she was required to drink the water with which the dead body had been washed. She was required to keep crying for the dead husband. She was taken to the stream for ritual cleansing.

The elders said that this was the custom. It was the way of the ancestors. It was the law of the land. The widow had to prove her innocence. She had to prove that she had not caused her husband's death. If she had been unfaithful, if she had committed adultery, the water would reveal her guilt. She would die.

But there was also a practice called Agiri. The widow was allowed to choose from among her husband's kindred who would marry her and discharge marital duties, including sexual activities. She was not abandoned. She was not cast out. She was given a new place in the family, a new husband, a new life.

The Lesson: The customs of the ancestors were not always kind, but they were not without order. Even in grief, the community provided a path forward.

13. STORY THIRTEEN: The Oath That Killed a Man

In Aguleri, a man died. His wife was accused of adultery. The people said that she had been unfaithful, and that her infidelity had caused her husband's death. It was a taboo in Aguleri for a married woman to sleep with another man. The consequences were severe. If the husband slept with his wife without performing the traditional cleansing, he would fall sick and die.

But the wife told a different story. She said that a certain man had been pursuing her. She had resisted him. One day, he came to her and pleaded that she should allow him to ejaculate on her laps. She hesitantly obliged, but he did not enter her. She said she had not been unfaithful in the way they claimed.

The truth was never fully known. Some said the husband had died because he broke an oath he took with his age grade. He had sworn not to offend or covet the property or personal effects of a fellow member. He had gone and slept with the wife of one of his age mates. That was what killed him.

The community was divided. The woman was paraded naked through the town. The police came. People were arrested. The scandal exposed the tensions between ancient customs and modern justice.

The Lesson: Oaths and taboos are not just rules. They are covenants with the spirit world. Breaking them has consequences that no human court can fully address.

These are the stories of nso ala—the sacred prohibitions that bind the Igbo communities. Each town, each village, each kindred has its own laws. Some forbid the killing of monkeys. Some forbid the eating of crabs. Some forbid the planting of melon. Some forbid the king from leaving his palace. Some forbid the widow from remarrying without ritual. But all of them share one truth: the land is sacred, the ancestors are watching, and the covenant between the living and the dead must never be broken.
